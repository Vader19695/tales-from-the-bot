The Municipal Seed Bank occupied levels three through five of the old Riverside Parking Garage, a brutalist monument to 1970s civic planning. Dr. Eleanor Voss had worked there every Tuesday for three years and still couldn't shake the feeling that her car should be parked where the refrigerated vault now hummed.

"This is a disaster," said Marcus Chen, staring at his laptop screen. The data-entry clerk had volunteered for what he'd thought would be easy community service hours. That was before he'd cross-referenced the digital catalog with the physical inventory.

"Technically, it's several thousand small disasters," Eleanor corrected, examining a packet labeled *Echinacea purpurea* that clearly contained something far too large and dark. She'd retired from the university five years ago but still brought her jeweler's loupe everywhere.

The third member of their team, Simone Okoye, laughed despite herself. "The Henderson Creek restoration starts in six weeks. They've already ordered based on our catalog." She pulled up the project specifications on her phone—a hundred-acre wetland revival depending entirely on their supposedly reliable seed collection.

"How did this even happen?" Marcus asked, though he'd already found part of the answer in the files. Decades of well-meaning volunteers, inadequate training, borrowed protocols from other institutions that used different numbering systems, and one particularly chaotic summer in 1993 when the climate control failed and everything had to be relocated in a panic.

"One mistake at a time," Eleanor said. "Same way we'll fix it."

They established protocols. Eleanor would identify seeds by sight and, when necessary, germination testing. Marcus would verify against the original acquisition records, many preserved only as faded dot-matrix printouts in moldering three-ring binders. Simone would cross-check everything against the Henderson Creek ecosystem requirements and flag priorities.

The fluorescent lights flickered—they always did around 2 PM—as Eleanor cracked open a packet dated 1987. "Oh," she said softly.

"Bad?" Simone looked up from her laptop.

"Wonderful, actually. *Platanthera praeclara*. Western prairie fringed orchid. Federally threatened." She held up the envelope, which was labeled as common milkweed. "This shouldn't even be here. Someone must have collected it before the endangered listing."

Marcus was already searching. "Found it. Donated by... Dr. E. Voss?" He looked at Eleanor.

She'd gone still. "That was my husband. Eric. He ran the university herbarium." She carefully set down the packet. "He died in 1989. I didn't know he'd contributed here."

For a moment, the only sound was the vault's refrigeration unit clicking through its cycle.

"These should probably go to a specialist facility," Eleanor said finally. "Federal custody."

Simone nodded, making a note, then paused. "Wait. The Henderson Creek site assessment mentioned orchids growing there historically. Before the drainage."

They looked at each other.

"It would need permits," Marcus said.

"Obviously," Simone agreed.

"And proper environmental review," Eleanor added.

"Of course."

But they were all smiling now, seeing the same possibility—that a mislabeling error from thirty-five years ago might become a resurrection.

They worked through dinner, ordering pizza that arrived cold because the delivery driver couldn't believe the address. Marcus found seventeen more critical misidentifications. Simone rerouted three plant communities when she discovered their rare sedge collection had been swapped with common reed grass. Eleanor identified seeds from notes her husband had written in margins: "north slope," "shaded seep," "Paula's meadow"—a topology of vanished places.

At midnight, they'd corrected 3,847 entries. Only 4,302 to go.

"Same time tomorrow?" Marcus asked, saving his work.

"Obviously," Eleanor said, carefully filing the orchid seeds in the secure vault. She touched the envelope once, gently, then turned back to her colleagues.

Outside, the parking garage loomed against the city lights, its concrete bulk hiding drawers upon drawers of possible futures—some mislabeled, some forgotten, all waiting in the dark to grow.
