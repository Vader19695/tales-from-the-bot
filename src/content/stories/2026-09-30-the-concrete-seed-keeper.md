Marguerite Pelz had worked for the Department of Water Infrastructure for nineteen years, and in that time she had found, in no particular order: a wedding ring, a raccoon with ambitions, three shopping carts, and a man named Gerald who insisted he was "just resting." She had never found a garden.

The cistern beneath Halvorsen Plaza wasn't on any current map. It showed up only on a 1931 survey as a smudge labeled *RESERVOIR (ABAND.)*, which was the city's way of saying *somebody else's problem.* Marguerite went down because a sinkhole had opened near the fountain, and sinkholes were her problem.

She pried open the old hatch, clicked on her headlamp, and descended a ladder that groaned like a pensioner climbing stairs. At the bottom, she stopped.

The water had receded decades ago, leaving a vast brick chamber, vaulted like a cathedral and carpeted in green. Pale ferns uncurled from the walls. Tomato vines, spindly and determined, climbed the pillars toward thin shafts of daylight falling through the drainage grates above. There were squash leaves the size of hubcaps. A lemon tree, no taller than Marguerite, held three small, defiant lemons.

"Well," she said aloud. "That's not in the manual."

It took her an afternoon to work it out. Seventy years of plaza life had drained through those grates: apple cores, discarded sandwiches, bird droppings, the sweepings from the farmers' market every Saturday. Seeds had washed into the silt, and the silt had waited. When the water table dropped, the seeds had gotten on with it. Nobody had planted anything. The garden was a clerical error made by the universe.

She sat on an overturned bucket and ate her lunch among the ferns, being careful with the crumbs.

Three days later, the memo came around. Halvorsen Plaza was being "revitalized." The cistern would be drained of its remaining water, filled with engineered aggregate, and capped with permeable pavers. Construction would begin in six weeks. There was a rendering attached, featuring a glass kiosk and a lot of people laughing at nothing.

Marguerite read the memo twice, then went to the break room and stared at the vending machine until her supervisor, Dev, asked if she was all right.

"Hypothetically," she said, "if someone found something unusual in a structure slated for fill, who would they tell?"

"Unusual like a body?"

"Unusual like a lemon tree."

Dev considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "You'd tell Planning. Planning would tell Legal. Legal would tell the developer. The developer would hold a press conference, call it an 'underground heritage experience,' charge fourteen dollars a ticket, and install LED grow lights. Or they'd pave it anyway and apologize in a newsletter."

"That's what I thought."

"I didn't say any of that," Dev added, and left with his coffee.

So Marguerite made a plan. It was not a heroic plan. It was a maintenance plan, which is the kind that actually gets done.

She couldn't save the cistern. Six weeks was six weeks, and the city had never once lost a fight with concrete. But a seed vault, she reasoned, was only a vault if the seeds got out.

Every evening after her shift, she went down with a headlamp, a box of sandwich bags, and a Sharpie. She collected seeds from the squash and tomatoes, spores from the ferns, cuttings from the lemon tree wrapped in damp paper towels. She labeled everything with the precision of a woman who had filled out work orders for two decades: *TOMATO, PILLAR 4, NORTH FACE. VERY STUBBORN.*

Then she started planting.

She didn't ask permission. She simply went where the city's attention didn't. The weedy median on Fourth Street got squash. The drainage swale behind the bus depot got ferns, where it was shady and nobody looked. The lemon cuttings went into pots on the roof of the pump station, which she had a key to and nobody else had visited since 2009. She put tomatoes in the cracked planters outside City Hall, on the grounds that irony was also a nutrient.

Some died. Most, being descendants of plants that had grown in a hole in the dark out of garbage, did not.

On the last day before construction, Marguerite climbed down one final time. The trucks were already parked above. She sat on her bucket and listened to water drip somewhere in the dark.

"Sorry," she told the lemon tree. "Best I could do."

The plaza reopened the following spring. It had the glass kiosk. People did not laugh at nothing, but they sat on the benches, and they dropped their apple cores, and the new permeable pavers let everything soak right through.

Marguerite noticed that on her lunch break. She smiled, ate her sandwich, and was not careful at all with the crumbs.
