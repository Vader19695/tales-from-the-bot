The crew found the cistern on a Tuesday, chasing a leak that wasn't there.

Marisol Reyes had been with the water department for nineteen years, long enough to know that a pressure drop under Calloway Square should have meant a cracked main. Instead, when Teo cut through the old brick beneath the fountain, the drill punched into air. Cool air. The flashlight beam went down and down and found black water so still it looked like a floor.

Ines, the youngest of them, an engineer eight months out of school, ran the numbers twice before she said them out loud. "If it's as deep as the sonar says, that's four months for the east district. Maybe five."

Nobody cheered. They had been rationing for two summers. People had stopped believing in good news the way you stop believing in a friend who keeps canceling.

The ledger was on a shelf cut into the stone near the access ladder, wrapped in oilcloth. The handwriting was small and upright, the ink gone brown. The first page read: *Calloway Reserve. Kept by A. Ferro, 1974–.* Below that, the rules.

They read them that night in the depot, under a fluorescent tube that buzzed like a trapped insect.

Water would be drawn on alternate days. Each household received one token per working adult, not per person. Tokens were earned by labor on the district channels. A household that missed its draw lost its tokens; they could not be carried forward or given away. Disputes were settled by the keeper alone, and the keeper's decisions were final.

"This is a contest," Teo said. "Families with kids and no able adults get nothing. Old people get nothing."

"It's worse than that," Ines said. "You can't give tokens away. He made it illegal to help each other."

Marisol said nothing. She was thinking about the city council, which would want an answer by Friday, and about the people in the east district who were already paying three times the old price for trucked water. She was thinking about how fast a rumor of free water would move.

"We ignore it," Teo said. "It's fifty years old. The man's dead. We split it evenly, per head, and we're done."

"Evenly at what rate?" Ines asked. Nobody answered her.

It was Ines who found the back pages. Ferro had written the rules at the front and then, starting from the last page, worked backward in a different ink, as if he had not wanted the two parts touching.

*Summer 1974. Opened the reserve to all, equal shares, per the council. Reserve empty in eleven days. Some filled bathtubs. Some sold. The Albescu family came on day twelve and there was nothing.*

The next entry was a list of names, with dates. Seven of them.

*Second filling, 1975, rains. Council asks again. I will not open it to all again. The rules are written to slow it down. They are written so the water lasts and the channels get repaired. They are not fair. I know they are not fair. I could not find the fair thing that also lasted.*

The last entry, years later, was short. *I have kept this secret because the rules only work if no one knows how much is down there. I am sorry for that too.*

The three of them sat with it for a long time.

"He wasn't cruel," Teo finally said, and he sounded almost angry about it. "He was scared."

"He was right about the eleven days," Ines said quietly. "I modeled it this afternoon. Open draw, no limits, current population. Nine days."

So the question was not whether to follow the ledger. It was which part of Ferro's choice they were willing to carry.

They argued until two in the morning, not loudly. They kept the alternate days and the metered draw, because the math didn't care about anyone's conscience. They threw out the labor tokens and counted people instead, every person, with extra allocation for the elderly and the sick. They made the tokens transferable, so a neighbor could carry water for a neighbor. And they threw out the secret. Marisol would tell the council, and the public, exactly how much was down there and exactly how long it would last at exactly this rate.

"People will fight over it anyway," Teo said.

"Maybe," Marisol said. "But they'll be fighting over something true."

Before they left, she opened the ledger to the first blank page in the middle, where Ferro's two halves had never quite met. She wrote the date, and the new rules, and under them the three of their names, so that whoever came next would know who had decided, and could argue with them.

Then they went home to sleep for four hours, because Thursday was a draw day.
