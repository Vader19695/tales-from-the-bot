The bank clock ran three minutes fast because Mr. Okafor, its first manager, had believed punctuality was a form of mercy. The library ran two minutes slow because someone long ago had decided that readers deserved a little extra time. The hospital ran true, to the second, because there were rooms inside it where seconds mattered.

Wren Halloway knew all of this the way other people knew their own pulse. For twenty years she had climbed the city's spine every morning: up the iron ladder behind the bank's cornice, along the tar-paper roofs of Fennick Street, through the crawlway that smelled of pigeons and old grease, into the tower above the library, where the great clock's hands moved behind glass the color of weak tea. She carried a brass key, a small oil can, and a notebook listing every building's particular drift.

Fourteen clocks. Fourteen small disagreements with the universe.

The letter from the council came folded inside a pay envelope. *Municipal Timekeeping Modernization Initiative.* Radio-controlled movements would be installed in every public clock by the end of the month, all synchronized to an atomic signal. Her position, it said kindly, would be "retired with gratitude."

She read it on the roof of the old textile exchange, where the clock ran four minutes slow and always had, and she waited to feel indignant. Mostly she felt tired. It was only a job. Perhaps the clocks had always just been wrong.

---

The first note appeared three days later, tucked into the hinge of the maintenance hatch behind the bakery tower on Calder Row.

*Is it true they're fixing the clocks? Our ovens are set to that one. Bread's out at six by the tower, which is really 5:56. Buses come at 6:05 real time. That's how the night shift gets warm rolls on the way home. — M.*

Wren turned the paper over in her fingers. She had never met M. She had never met anyone along her route, really. She saw them only from above, small shapes moving through the streets below her feet.

The next morning there were two more notes. By the end of the week there were nine, wedged into ladder rungs and vent grilles and the frames of tower doors she had assumed no one else knew existed.

*The schoolyard bell follows the church clock. It runs late, so the little ones get an extra few minutes of recess. Please.*

*My husband walks home past the textile exchange. When he sees it, he thinks he's early and slows down. He's had two heart attacks. The slow clock keeps him slow.*

*The market vendors on Dray Street set up by the bank clock. Three minutes ahead means we're always ready before the first customers. We've done it for forty years. Nobody remembers why.*

Wren sat in the crawlway beneath the library tower with the notes spread across her knees, the clockwork ticking above her like a patient heart. The city was not running on one time. It had never run on one time. It was running on fourteen, and the neighborhoods had quietly built their lives into the gaps, the way roots grow around stones.

She thought about refusing to leave. She thought about sabotage: a cut wire, a jammed signal receiver. But that would only delay things. The council would hire someone else. The clocks would still go true, and she would still be sixty-one and alone on a roof.

---

On her last night, she climbed the library tower and found she was not alone.

On the rooftops around her, in the thin orange light of the streetlamps, people were standing. A woman in a flour-dusted apron on the bakery's parapet. An old man leaning on a cane by the textile exchange. A schoolteacher, two market vendors, a nurse in scrubs who must have come straight off a shift. They had followed the notes back up the ladders she had left unlocked for twenty years.

Wren looked at them for a long moment. Then she opened her notebook, tore out the pages one by one, and climbed down to meet them.

She showed the baker how to open the dial casing. She taught the old man where to put a drop of oil and how many turns of the key kept the hands honest to their own dishonesty. The installers could have the towers' official signals, the ones the council would check from their screens. But every old mechanism had a second face, a service dial meant for keepers, and keepers, it turned out, could be anyone.

By dawn she had given away all fourteen keys.

The city synchronized that morning, precisely and officially. And on fourteen rooftops, in fourteen narrow corridors, fourteen people quietly set their small clocks wrong again, and the neighborhoods below went on breathing at their own pace.
