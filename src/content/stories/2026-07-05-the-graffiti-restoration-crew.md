Marcus adjusted his safety goggles and squinted at the rust-streaked wall of the Old Avenue station. Thirty-seven years of street art cascaded down the concrete like geological strata—each layer a time capsule of spray paint and marker ink.

"This is going to take weeks," said Chen, the art student, already snapping photos with her tablet. "There's at least fifteen distinct layers here."

"Sixteen," corrected Dmitri, mixing solvent in a plastic bucket. His hands, once famous in the local tagging scene, moved with practiced efficiency. "I can see Viktor's work from '03 under that awful bubble-letter phase everyone went through."

Marcus climbed the scaffolding with the deliberate movements of a man who'd spent forty years up ladders for the electric company. "City's paying us by the hour. I suggest we take our time and do it right."

The municipal contract was straightforward: document and remove all graffiti before the wrecking balls arrived. The historical society wanted records. The artists wanted respect. The city wanted the whole mess gone.

They worked in companionable silence, the rhythm of documentation and removal almost meditative. Chen photographed each section from multiple angles, her thesis on urban art evolution depending on this data. Dmitri applied solvents with surgical precision, occasionally pausing to admire a particularly bold piece. Marcus handled the scaffolding and muttered about OSHA regulations nobody else cared about.

"Look at this technique," Dmitri said on their third day, exposing a section from the late nineties. "Single-can work. Very controlled. Probably had ten minutes before transit cops showed up."

"Romantic," Chen said dryly, uploading files. "Breaking the law at three in the morning. Very artistic."

"You're writing a hundred-page thesis about it."

"Doesn't mean I'm romanticizing it."

Marcus descended for more solvent. "You're both romanticizing it. It's vandalism and paperwork, and my back hurts."

By the second week, they'd reached the deeper layers—older tags, faded and ghost-like. The art became simpler, rawer. Fat marker tags and early spray-paint experiments from the mid-eighties.

"Slower here," Chen cautioned. "This is where it gets historically significant."

Dmitri worked with even more care, revealing a section inch by inch. Then he stopped.

"What?" Marcus asked.

"This tag." Dmitri's voice had changed. "CIPHER-87."

Chen leaned in. "Significant?"

"Maybe." Dmitri pulled out his phone, searched, then went quiet. He showed them a website—a cold case database. A missing person report from 1987. Jeremy Winters, seventeen, aspiring artist, disappeared after telling his parents he was "going to make his mark." The case file included examples of his early work: a distinctive cipher-like signature.

The three of them stared at the wall.

"Could be coincidence," Chen said. "Cipher's a common tag name."

"With the same loop on the 'C'?" Dmitri shook his head. "That's his style. Distinctive."

Marcus climbed down slowly. "So the kid came here, tagged the wall, and then what? Disappeared? Ran away? The file doesn't say he was found."

"No body," Chen confirmed, scrolling. "Just... vanished. Family never got closure."

They stood there as traffic hummed past on the street above. The wall suddenly felt heavier, more present.

"We report it," Chen said finally. "Family deserves to know."

"Know what?" Dmitri challenged gently. "That their kid spray-painted a wall and maybe hopped a train to California? This gives them nothing but more questions. Maybe he reinvented himself. Got out. Got clean. If he wanted to be found, he'd have reached out."

"Or maybe he couldn't," Chen countered.

Marcus sat on an overturned bucket. "Or maybe he's been living as someone else for thirty-seven years. Got a family. A job. And we're about to blow up his whole life because we found his teenage signature."

"Or we give a family answers," Chen insisted.

They sat with it. The weight of someone else's life, hidden beneath layers of other people's art, beneath time itself.

Finally, Marcus stood. "My vote? We photograph it like everything else. We document it. We put it in the archive with all the rest. And if someone—family or the person himself—ever comes looking, it'll be there. But we don't go digging up graves that might not exist."

Chen looked at Dmitri. He nodded slowly.

"Democracy," Marcus said. "And more solvent. This isn't going to clean itself."

They worked quietly for the rest of the day, careful with the tag, respectful. In the archive database, Chen labeled it simply: "CIPHER-87, unknown artist, documented and preserved."

Some mysteries, they decided, didn't need solving.

Only remembering.
