Renata Holm had sealed cracks for the City of Marlow Department of Public Works for eleven years, and in that time she had developed opinions. Transverse cracks were honest. Alligator cracking meant a failed base and somebody upstream had cut corners in 1987. Block cracking was the pavement getting old, which was nobody's fault, least of all the pavement's.

What she did not expect was for a crack to come back.

It was on Fennimore, outside the dry cleaner: a long, shallow curve she had routed and filled with hot rubberized sealant on a Tuesday. On Friday it was back. It wasn't reopened, because the sealant was still there, black and intact. The new crack ran a quarter inch to the left, perfectly parallel, as if someone had traced her work and moved it over.

She photographed it because that was procedure. Then she photographed the next one, and the one after that.

By the third week she had two hundred and forty photos and a laminated District 4 map on her kitchen table, marked up in grease pencil. Her husband, Theo, ate around it.

"It's a lot of lines," he said.

"They connect," she said. "Look. Fennimore runs into the Kroger lot, and the lot crack picks up where it leaves off, same angle. Then across Dutton, then the bus pad on Ninth." She drew it with her finger. "Cracks don't do that. Cracks follow stress. These ignore joints and change material. One went straight through a brick crosswalk."

"So what are they following?"

That was the question. She went back out with a flashlight and a four-foot level. The new cracks had clean walls, too clean. At the bottom of one she found a fine gray paste, the kind a wet diamond blade leaves behind. Somebody was cutting them by hand, at night, carefully, at a depth of about three-quarters of an inch, which was exactly what you'd choose if you wanted it to look natural and didn't want to hit rebar.

That narrowed it down to people who knew pavement.

The map kept changing. Every few days a line extended, or a new one branched off. She updated the grease pencil and noticed that the lines weren't random branches. They were closing in, like a drawstring bag being pulled shut. All of them bent toward the same spot, the corner of Alder and Ninth.

She drove past it on her lunch break. There was a laundromat, an electrical substation behind chain link, and between them a wedge of old concrete about the size of a parking space. She had passed it a thousand times and couldn't remember ever looking at it. In the middle was a round iron cover, stamped MCSD 1931.

She checked the GIS layer on her tablet. There was nothing there, no utility, no easement, no asset ID. According to the city, the wedge was a rounding error.

She sat in the truck and thought about Gary.

Gary Pruett was her supervisor. He was not a bad man. He was a man with a backlog of four hundred and twelve work orders and a director who measured him in closed tickets. If she filed this, it would read *vandalism, District 4, pattern unclear*, and it would go into the queue. Someone would come out in six weeks and seal the cracks, and that would be the end of it.

Or she could open the cover herself. That was against procedure, but it was also only a cover.

She came back at six-thirty that evening with a pry bar and a gas monitor clipped to her vest. When she got there, a man was already sitting on an upturned bucket beside the wedge. He was around seventy, wearing a reflective vest so faded it had gone pink, and a walk-behind saw sat next to him under a tarp.

"You're the sealant lady," he said. "You're fast. I had to keep moving the lines over."

"You could have called the city."

"I did, nine times, starting in 2019." He handed her a folder. It held soil reports, settlement readings, and a hand-drawn cross-section. "Marlow County Storm District. They built a brick culvert under here in '31, abandoned it in '58, and never filled it. It's collapsing. The void is about here," he tapped the folder, "and it's growing roughly a foot a month toward the substation. I drew its outline on top of it. I figured somebody who looks at pavement for a living would eventually look."

Renata read the readings twice. Then she photographed every page.

She didn't open the cover. She didn't need to.

At 7:14 p.m. she called Gary at home, which she had never done.

"It's after hours," he said.

"I know. I'm filing an emergency ticket for subsidence near the Alder substation. I've got documentation, a historical source, and a map." She looked at the old man, who raised one hand modestly. "It's a very good map."
