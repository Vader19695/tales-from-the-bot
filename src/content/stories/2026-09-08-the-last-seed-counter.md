Dr. Marion Kessler was seventy-three and had promised herself she was done with obsessive cataloging. Twenty years at the botanical garden, another fifteen consulting for agricultural nonprofits—that was enough spreadsheets for one lifetime. So naturally, here she was on a Tuesday morning, squinting at a packet of Cherokee Purple tomatoes in a converted subway platform that smelled like concrete and dried earth.

"These are wrong," she said.

The teenager beside her—Ravi, name tag slightly crooked—looked up from his clipboard. "Wrong how?"

Marion held the packet to the fluorescent light. The seeds inside were too uniform, too perfect. Cherokee Purples should have varied slightly in size and color, the beautiful irregularity of four generations of careful saving. These looked like they'd been stamped out by a machine.

"Get me the winter squash," she said. "The Waltham Butternut."

Ravi navigated the metal shelving that lined the old platform with surprising grace. The community seed bank had been his idea originally—or rather, his eighth-grade science project that had somehow convinced the transit authority to donate this abandoned station. Now he spent three afternoons a week here, and Marion had been volunteering for six months, mostly because her daughter worried she was becoming a hermit.

The squash seeds were wrong too. So were the Kentucky Wonder beans.

"Someone's swapping them," Marion said, sitting heavily on a plastic folding chair. "Replacing heirlooms with F1 hybrids. Sterile varieties."

Ravi's eyes widened. "But we distribute these to thirty community gardens. If people plant them—"

"They'll grow this season. Next year, nothing. Or weak plants that won't breed true." Marion felt a familiar anger kindle in her chest. "They'd have to come back and buy more seeds."

"From the companies that make F1 hybrids," Ravi finished. He was quick, this kid. "But we're a seed bank. We give them away free."

"Exactly."

They spent the next four hours checking packets, Marion's fingers remembering the weight and texture of a thousand varieties, Ravi creating a color-coded spreadsheet that would have made her weep with pride if she were the sentimental type. By late afternoon, they'd identified thirty-seven compromised varieties, all replaced within the past month.

"Who has access?" Marion asked.

Ravi scrolled through his phone. "Besides us? Four other volunteers. And Brian from the botanical garden comes by sometimes to consult."

Marion's stomach sank. Brian Chen. Her former protégé, now running the garden's partnership with AgriGlobal Seeds. She'd thought it was idealistic of them, that partnership—bringing big agriculture expertise to heritage preservation.

She'd been naive.

They found him on his next visit, standing near the nightshade family section with a canvas bag slung over his shoulder. Marion watched from behind a concrete pillar as he swapped out a packet of Cherokee Purple tomatoes—the same variety she'd caught that morning.

"Brian," she said, stepping forward.

He jumped, then deflated. "Dr. Kessler."

"Why?"

He set down the bag carefully. Ravi appeared beside Marion, silent solidarity.

"AgriGlobal's metrics," Brian said finally. "They wanted proof that heirloom seeds aren't viable for feeding populations. That standardization is necessary." He looked genuinely miserable. "I thought—I convinced myself it was just data collection. That we'd give everyone replacement seeds afterward."

"You thought you'd prove the past didn't work," Marion said quietly, "by sabotaging it yourself."

The silence stretched between them, broken only by the distant rumble of trains running through active tunnels nearby.

"I'm sorry," Brian whispered.

Marion looked at Ravi, who nodded almost imperceptibly. They'd already documented everything, backed up their findings.

"You'll resign from the partnership," Marion said. "And you'll help us replace every single packet you've touched. The real seeds—I know you kept them. You're not malicious, just stupid."

Brian's laugh was hollow. "I did keep them. I couldn't quite..."

"Couldn't quite destroy them," Marion finished. "Good. There's hope for you yet."

As Brian left to retrieve the seeds, Ravi turned to Marion. "You think he meant well?"

"I think progress and preservation aren't actually enemies," Marion said, "until someone decides they have to be." She squeezed his shoulder. "Come on. We've got work to do before spring."
