The structural assessment of Municipal Garage C was supposed to take forty minutes. Margaret Hale had blocked the time on her calendar between a zoning variance hearing and a budget call, and she resented every step down the concrete ramp into the second basement level, where the fluorescent lights buzzed like trapped insects.

"Storage room 14," said Daniel Ortiz from Code Compliance, consulting his clipboard. "Should be empty. Slated for the electrical upgrade."

It was not empty.

When Daniel forced the warped door, the four of them stood in the threshold and said nothing. Rows of steel filing cabinets, the old municipal kind with brass label frames, lined the walls. A dehumidifier hummed in the corner. On a folding table lay a city map, its streets almost invisible beneath hand-inked annotations in green and brown: tiny symbols, dates, Latin names written in a careful, slanted script.

Margaret opened a drawer. Inside, hundreds of glassine envelopes stood upright like index cards. *Asclepias tuberosa — Butterfly weed. Collected: rail embankment behind Fulton Street, Sept. Germination 84%.* Another: *Rudbeckia hirta. Vacant lot, 9th & Delmar. Self-sustaining since 2019.*

"Whose is this?" asked Priscilla Wong, the transit liaison, in the hushed voice people use in churches.

Margaret already knew. She recognized the handwriting from a dozen site drawings that had crossed her desk, always too detailed, always annotated with notes no one had requested about soil drainage and pollinator corridors.

---

Nadia Ferreira came down when summoned, still wearing her lanyard, and did not pretend. She was twenty-eight, two years into her position as a junior architect, and she stood among her cabinets with her hands folded.

"Three years," she said. "About four thousand accessions. Most are native species the city's own landscaping contracts stopped specifying in the nineties. Some are heirlooms from community gardens that got paved for the stadium lots."

"You're using city property," Daniel said. "City electricity. That's misappropriation, at minimum. And the planting—" he gestured at the map—"that's unpermitted alteration of municipal land. Dozens of sites."

"Hundreds," Nadia said quietly. "The culvert on Ridgeway had nothing living in it in 2021. Now there are bumblebees. Native ones, *Bombus impatiens*. I have photographs."

Margaret studied the map. She knew these places. She had signed off on most of them as "remnant parcels," municipal shorthand for land too small, too awkward, or too contaminated to bother with. Strips beside on-ramps. The triangular wedge behind the water authority. Places the city had decided did not count.

Nadia had decided otherwise.

"Why not go through channels?" Priscilla asked.

Nadia laughed, not unkindly. "I did. I submitted a proposal for a municipal seed bank in my first month. It's in the Sustainability Committee's queue. Item forty-one. They've met six times since then and reached item nineteen."

---

Daniel wanted to file the report that afternoon. He was not a cruel man, Margaret knew. He believed, as she once had, that rules were the scaffolding that kept a city from collapsing into a thousand private preferences. If Nadia could plant whatever she wanted, so could anyone. A developer could. A crank could.

"She's right about the bees, though," Priscilla said in the elevator.

"Being right isn't a permit," Daniel replied.

That night Margaret sat at her kitchen table with the envelope she had pocketed without quite meaning to. *Echinacea purpurea. Purple coneflower. Collected: median strip, Route 9 interchange.* She thought of the forty-one items, the six meetings, the thirty-one years she had spent believing that patience was a form of integrity. She thought of all the remnant parcels she had stamped and forgotten.

Daniel was right that rules mattered. But rules were supposed to serve something. When the machinery stopped serving anything but itself, was obedience still a virtue, or only a habit?

---

The next morning, Margaret filed her assessment of Garage C. Storage room 14, she wrote, contained "an active botanical archive of municipal ecological significance," and she recommended it be preserved during the electrical upgrade and formally transferred to the Parks Department's jurisdiction, pending review.

Then she walked to the Sustainability Committee chair's office and asked, politely and with the full weight of her seniority, that item forty-one be moved to the top of next week's agenda.

Daniel found her afterward. "That's not exposing it," he said.

"No," Margaret agreed. "It's making it legal. Slowly. The way we do everything."

He was quiet a long time. Then he took the report from her and initialed it.

That evening, Margaret knelt in the strip of bare dirt between her building and the bus stop, pressed three coneflower seeds into the soil, and did not ask anyone's permission at all.
