The fluorescent lights hummed their eternal song three levels beneath the city streets, casting everything in the parking garage-turned-vault the color of ice. Dr. Elena Vasquez ran her fingers along the climate-controlled drawers that had replaced painted parking lines, each containing thousands of seeds—a library of extinct grocery store tomatoes, forgotten wheat varieties, beans that had fed civilizations.

"Dr. Vasquez?" Mika's voice cracked slightly, the way seventeen-year-old voices do. "I think you should see this."

Elena looked up from her clipboard. The teenager stood by the Cherokee Purple tomato drawer, holding two packets that should have been identical.

"The weight's wrong," Mika said. "I was doing routine checks, and this whole section—the mass is off by point-two grams average."

Elena felt her chest tighten. After forty years studying plant genetics, she knew that point-two grams might as well be a canyon. She crossed the concrete floor, her sensible shoes squeaking.

Under the magnifying lamp, the difference became clear. The seeds in the right packet—drawn from backup storage—showed the characteristic irregular shape of true Cherokee Purples, each seed slightly different. The seeds in the left packet, supposedly from primary storage, were too uniform. Too perfect.

"Counterfeits," Elena breathed.

They worked in expanding circles from that first discovery, pulling samples, weighing, comparing. By midnight, they'd found forty-seven compromised varieties. By two a.m., the number had climbed to ninety-three. All heirloom vegetables. All rare.

"We should call someone," Mika said, pushing purple-dyed hair from their eyes. "The police? The agriculture department?"

Elena sat heavily on a folding chair, her mind racing through protocols and procedures. "By the time they investigate, whoever's doing this could finish the job. Or disappear."

"So what do we do?"

Elena studied the teenager. Most volunteers lasted a month before the tedium drove them away. Mika had stayed for six, asking questions, learning the Latin names, understanding why this mattered. Why seeds that seemed so ordinary were actually irreplaceable genetic keys.

"We figure out who," Elena said. "The access logs. Someone's been coming here with time and privacy."

They pulled up the digital records on Elena's laptop. The garage held forty thousand seed varieties; no one could swap them all at once. It would take weeks, months. A pattern had to exist.

Mika proved surprisingly adept at spreadsheets, filtering and cross-referencing while Elena examined more specimens. Three hours of quiet, methodical work yielded a name: Marcus Chen, night security supervisor. Four months of late shifts. Long enough to learn the system. To understand what was valuable.

"Why would a security guard want to replace heirloom seeds?" Mika wondered aloud.

Elena thought of seed prices in specialty catalogs, of wealthy preppers stockpiling genetic diversity, of agricultural corporations that would pay dearly for varieties they could patent and control. "Lots of reasons. None of them good."

Dawn would arrive in an hour. Chen's shift would end.

"We could confront him," Mika suggested.

"And say what? We have evidence but no proof of motive. No idea where the real seeds went." Elena made her decision. "We document everything. Photograph every compromised packet. Build a case so airtight he can't slip through."

They worked through the sunrise they couldn't see, three levels underground. Mika operated the camera while Elena's steady hands displayed each specimen. The teenager had started the night as a volunteer; now they were something more. Collaborators. Fellow keepers.

At seven a.m., they had documentation of 147 counterfeit samples. Elena sent the files to the board of directors, the police, and the federal agriculture department simultaneously.

"What if he comes back before they arrive?" Mika asked.

Elena pulled out her thermos of coffee, poured two cups. "Then we're here. Someone needs to guard this."

Mika took the offered cup, smiled tiredly. "That's what we are, isn't it? Guards. Just for something that doesn't look like it needs guarding."

"Everything essential looks humble," Elena said, "until it's gone."

They sat together in the cold light, drinking bad coffee, waiting. Two guards spanning fifty years, protecting the future one small seed at a time.
