Marian Kosky had worked for the Department of Water Infrastructure for nineteen years, and in that time she had found the usual things underground: raccoons, a bicycle, a surprising number of wedding rings. She had never found a garden.

The cistern beneath Municipal Parking Lot 14 was built in 1923 to hold four hundred thousand gallons of emergency water for a downtown that no longer existed. It was decommissioned in 1961, sealed in 1974, and forgotten sometime after that. Marian went down in March only because the city needed a structural survey before the demolition crews arrived. The transit hub was scheduled. Renderings had been released. There was a ribbon somewhere, waiting to be cut.

She expected standing water and rust. Instead, when she switched off her headlamp to change the battery, the brick vaults glowed a faint, sickly green. It was the color of a watch dial at three in the morning.

She switched the lamp back on and saw the plants.

They were pale, mostly. Some were white as boiled celery. Tangled stems climbed the cracked mortar toward nothing in particular. Clusters of waxy, translucent flowers bowed their heads in the shallow seepage pools. A colony of something that looked like asparagus had apparently given up on being asparagus decades ago and committed fully to being strange.

Marian took four hundred photographs, a water sample, and the rest of the afternoon off.

---

The botanist from the state university was named Dr. Feld. He arrived with the posture of a man who had been summoned to look at a great many "miracles" that turned out to be mold.

"Plants need light," he said at the top of the ladder.

"I'm aware," Marian said. "I have a houseplant."

Forty minutes later, Dr. Feld was kneeling in four inches of mineral water and making a noise she would later describe in her report as "involuntary."

The explanation took six weeks and three graduate students. It was less magical and more interesting than anyone had hoped. In the 1920s, a seed wholesaler had occupied the building above the cistern. Its basement flooded repeatedly, and the overflow drained through a storm grate directly into the vaults. Most of the seeds rotted. Some didn't. The survivors had worked out an arrangement with the fungus colonizing the old timber supports. That fungus was a bioluminescent *Armillaria* strain. It fed on the wood and the dissolved minerals leaching through the limestone, and it shared its sugars with any plant willing to plug into its network. The botanists called this mycoheterotrophy. Marian called it a timeshare.

The glow wasn't feeding anything. It was just the fungus showing off.

---

The public meeting was held in a middle-school cafeteria. Present were the Deputy Director of Planning, two city council members, Dr. Feld, the Riverside Community Garden Association, and one man who had come to complain about parking and refused to leave.

The Deputy Director was polite in the way of someone with a federal grant deadline. "We appreciate the novelty," he said. "But the city can't halt a two-hundred-million-dollar project for some albino vegetables."

The gardeners bristled. One woman began to say something about Mother Earth. Marian gently put a binder in front of her.

"I'm not asking you to halt it," Marian said. "I'm asking you to read page thirty-one."

Page thirty-one held the hub's own engineering requirements. The new station needed underground stormwater detention to meet the state runoff code. The plan called for a new concrete tank costing about eleven million dollars.

"You already have one," Marian said. "It's a hundred years old and it's structurally sound. I know because I surveyed it. Shift the platform sixty feet east and the cistern becomes your detention basin. You keep the existing seepage, so the ecosystem stays stable."

Dr. Feld then walked them through pages thirty-two through ninety. He showed the genetic sequencing, which identified three bean varieties and a squash absent from every commercial catalog since 1931. He covered the fungal strain, which was already drawing interest from a pharmaceutical lab. He presented a projected research-grant figure, which made one council member sit up straighter.

"So the weird basement plants," said the parking man slowly, "save us money?"

"About nine million dollars," Marian said. "Net."

The Deputy Director looked at page thirty-one for a long time. Then he looked at it some more.

---

The transit hub opened two years later, sixty feet east of its original rendering. Beneath the northbound platform, behind a locked hatch, the cistern quietly collects the rain and keeps its pale, patient garden. Graduate students go down on Tuesdays. The Riverside gardeners have grown the 1931 beans in actual sunlight. They report that the beans are delicious and somewhat confused.

Marian still inspects the vaults twice a year. She switches off her lamp each time and lets the green glow come up around her. Then she writes in her report, accurately and without romance, that everything is holding.
