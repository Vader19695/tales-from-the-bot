The rules of the Eastbrook Cooperative Autumn Ferment were simple: plain mason jars, numbered labels, no names, no bribes. That last rule had been added in 2019, after the Okonkwo twins left honey cake on the judges' table.

This year's panel was drawn from a coffee can, as always, and the coffee can had a sense of humor. It gave the shared kitchen Ines Calder, eight months a widow and still setting out two mugs some mornings; Priya Raman, sixteen, who had spent September turning her closet into a fermentation lab for AP Biology; and Harold Voss, who had inspected restaurants for thirty-one years and had once shut down a wedding reception over a lukewarm crab dip.

"I brought test strips," Priya said.

"I brought a thermometer," Harold said.

Ines had brought nothing but a fork and a vague hope that tasting cabbage might feel like rejoining the world.

They worked through eleven jars. Number three was mostly salt. Number six, Harold declared, was "technically alive, but I wouldn't let it vote." Number nine made Priya cough for a full minute.

Then came number seven: a sauerkraut, pale gold, flecked with caraway and crushed juniper, with an apple sweetness underneath. Priya checked the pH and whispered, "Perfect." Harold chewed slowly, in the manner of a man who had seen the worst of humanity's kitchens and was being given a reason to forgive it.

Ines wasn't eating. She was staring at the lid.

The rules said mason jars, and it was one. But someone had swapped the metal lid for a stoneware weight to keep the cabbage under the brine, a small disk glazed celadon green with a thumbprint pressed into the center.

She had made it. She'd made six of them last winter, unable to sleep, and given one to Tomás Ferreira, the night custodian, who had found her in the kitchen at 3 a.m. and sat with her without saying anything useful, which was exactly what she'd needed.

Tomás was not a resident. He had been on the waitlist for four years.

"That's the winner," Harold said.

"It is," said Ines. "And I know who made it. He can't have entered it."

The entry log was on the clipboard, sealed in an envelope they weren't supposed to open until after scoring. Harold, a man who had built his life on procedure, opened it with his thumbnail.

Number seven: Gordon Pike.

Gordon was the board president. He had won the Ferment twice, chaired the waitlist committee, and could say "community" in a tone that made people check their wallets.

"Well," said Priya. "That's academic misconduct."

They found Gordon in the laundry room, folding towels with military precision. He saw the stoneware disk in Ines's hand and sat down on the dryer.

"Tomás made it," he said. "I know."

"You stole his kraut," Priya said.

"He gave it to me. I asked." Gordon rubbed his face. "He wouldn't enter. Said it wasn't his place. I thought if it won, I'd stand up at the potluck and say, 'Actually, this is Tomás's, and he's been on our waitlist since before this kid had braces, and maybe we should fix that.'"

"Or," Harold said, "you'd win a third ribbon and decide the speech could wait."

Gordon was quiet a moment too long. "I thought about it," he admitted. "I'm not proud of the thinking. But I've thought about a lot of things I didn't do."

Ines turned the disk over. Paul used to say ambition was just love that hadn't decided where to go yet. She'd always thought it was too generous. She still did, mostly.

"The rules say a resident's entry," Harold said. "It's disqualified."

"The rules," Priya said, "also say the judges' decision is final."

They both looked at Ines.

At the potluck, the whole cooperative crowded the kitchen. Gordon stood by the door, paler than the kraut. Harold read the results in his inspection voice.

"First place, number seven. The judges note the entry was submitted improperly and is disqualified." A murmur. "The judges also note it's the best ferment this building has produced in a decade, and they'd like the person who actually made it to come up and receive the ribbon anyway."

Nobody moved. Then Gordon crossed the room, found Tomás leaning against the back wall with a mop he didn't need, and said, loud enough for everyone, "It's yours. I'm sorry. Go on."

Tomás walked up slowly. Ines pinned the ribbon on his work shirt. Somebody started clapping, then everybody did.

The board met Tuesday. Gordon recused himself from the waitlist vote, which passed anyway.

In November, Ines brought Tomás a housewarming gift: a crock, celadon, with a thumbprint on the lid.

"For next year," she said. "Enter it yourself."
