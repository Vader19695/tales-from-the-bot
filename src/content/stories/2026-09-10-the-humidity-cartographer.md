For thirty years, Malcolm had tracked storms across radar screens, predicting their paths with mathematical precision. Now, at sixty-four, he mopped floors and handed out towels at the Meridian Public Baths, watching different patterns emerge in the rising steam.

It started as idle observation during the quiet hours between eleven and dawn. The sulfur pool in the east corner produced thick, rolling clouds when the college students arrived after their restaurant shifts ended. The mineral bath near the entrance sent up delicate spirals when the elderly Korean women came for their Thursday gatherings. Each pool had its temperature, its chemical composition, its devoted visitors who arrived like clockwork.

Malcolm began sketching the patterns in a notebook he kept by the front desk. Not the people themselves—he respected their privacy too much for that—but the steam signatures they created. The way the eucalyptus pool's vapor twisted differently when the construction workers used it versus when the yoga instructor came alone. How the central hot pool generated almost no steam during the post-theater crowd but billowed magnificently when the night nurses soaked their tired feet.

Three months into his observations, Malcolm noticed something extraordinary. Laid out across his weeks of sketches, the steam patterns formed a map—not of geography, but of connections. He could trace how communities intersected without ever exchanging words. The teacher who came at midnight used the same pools as the janitor who arrived at two, following an identical rotation. The teenager with the facial scars visited the pools that the burn survivor from the fourth-floor apartment favored, perhaps following her unspoken recommendation. The divorced father and the widower synchronized their visits down to fifteen-minute windows, though Malcolm had never seen them speak.

The steam revealed a hidden city: shy networks of solidarity, unconscious rhythms of communal care, the way lonely people orbited each other in careful patterns of not-quite-meeting.

Malcolm had begun to predict these patterns, to understand their meaning, when Raymond, the bathhouse manager, called a staff meeting.

"Climate control," Raymond announced with obvious pride, showing them glossy brochures. "Top-of-the-line HVAC system. No more random humidity. No more visibility issues from excessive steam. Clean, controlled environment. Installation starts next month."

The other staff members nodded approvingly. Malcolm's notebook suddenly felt heavy in his pocket.

That night, he watched a young woman arrive at her usual time, moving between pools in her established pattern—a pattern that perfectly overlapped with an older woman who came an hour later. Malcolm had seen it a dozen times: they never met, but they shared something, some unspoken knowledge transmitted through their choice of waters. Two weeks ago, the young woman had shifted her pattern slightly, adding the cold plunge. Last week, the older woman had done the same. A conversation in absences and timing.

Malcolm could document all of this. Show Raymond his maps, explain that these patterns represented something worth preserving—a invisible social infrastructure, delicate and unreproducible. The steam was data, was language, was connection made visible.

But it was also private. These people hadn't consented to being mapped, even anonymously. Their patterns were beautiful precisely because they were unconscious, unobserved, natural. To reveal them would be to destroy them. People would become self-conscious, would change their behaviors, would know they'd been watched.

Yet without intervention, the patterns would vanish anyway. The new system would regulate everything into uniformity. Future night-shift workers would never see what Malcolm had seen, never understand these hidden geometries of urban belonging.

Malcolm opened his notebook one last time, studying months of careful observation. Then, methodically, he began tearing out pages.

Some mysteries deserved protection. Some maps were meant to fade. The steam would disappear, but what it represented—those silent networks of almost-connection, those unconscious kindnesses, those lonely people orbiting in the dark—those would persist in ways Malcolm couldn't chart and shouldn't try.

He fed the last page into the employee break room shredder and returned to his mop, content to let certain patterns remain invisible, even to himself.
