Margaret Chen had maintained the municipal seed bank for twelve years without incident. The converted cistern beneath parking level B3 kept a constant fourteen degrees Celsius, and the stone walls, three feet thick, muffled the rumble of traffic overhead. She arrived each morning at seven-thirty, descended the iron spiral staircase, and logged specimens with methodical precision.

The cardboard boxes appeared on Tuesday.

Ten boxes, standard file size, stacked beside her desk. No delivery slip. No intake form. The security log showed nothing—the overnight guard swore he'd seen no one, heard nothing. Margaret stood before them for several minutes, calculating violations of protocol. Anonymous donations required verification. Unprovenanced seeds could harbor disease, invasive genetics, administrative liability.

She should have reported them immediately.

Instead, she made tea and opened the first box.

Inside, packets made from folded newsprint, each secured with careful creases. No tape, no staples. The labels were handwritten in cramped numerals: 04/17/1983. 11/02/1994. 06/28/2007. Dates only. No species names, no collection locations, no donor identification.

Margaret selected a packet dated 15/09/1989 and unfolded it at her examination table. The seeds inside were flat, kidney-shaped, mottled brown. She recognized them instantly—*Phaseolus lunatus*, Lima beans. But not the commercial varieties currently approved for urban allotments. These matched the heritage strain documented in the 1977 regional agricultural survey, the one that had vanished when the wholesale markets consolidated.

She opened another. 22/03/1996. Tomato seeds, the tiny wild type. *Solanum pimpinellifolium*. She had seen specimens in the university herbarium, collected from the riverside lots before the flood barrier construction.

A third packet: 08/11/2001. Seeds she didn't immediately recognize. She crushed one carefully between tweezers—the distinctive scent of anise rose from the fragments. *Pimpinella anisum*, common anise. The notation in her database indicated local extinction circa 1998, due to soil contamination remediation.

Margaret sat very still.

Forty years of dates. She did the arithmetic: assuming standard viability protocols, saving seeds annually, someone had been maintaining these plants continuously across four decades. In a city where community garden plots were rationed, subject to zoning review, limited to approved species lists she herself had helped compile.

She found the note in the sixth box, tucked between packets dated to 2019. Brown paper, same careful hand: "Balconies, rooftops, window boxes, railway embankments, cemetery margins, vacant lots. We kept them going. Time to pass them on."

No signature.

Margaret returned to her desk and opened the official database. The donation should be logged. Forms filed. She should contact the agricultural extension office, the municipal compliance department. Unlicensed cultivation carried fines. These seeds represented systematic circumvention of approved protocols.

Her fingers hovered over the keyboard.

In twelve years, she had processed 14,847 accessions. She had documented three cases of seed failure in climate-controlled storage. She had produced quarterly reports demonstrating efficient management of the city's botanical heritage, as defined by the current strategic plan, which prioritized commercial viability and standardization.

She looked at the packets spread before her. Someone had saved these in secret. Someone had grown plants in marginal spaces, collected seeds in brown paper, labeled them with quiet precision, kept faith across forty years that these varieties mattered. That when the planning documents and strategic frameworks changed again, these seeds would still exist.

Margaret began opening her cataloging software, then stopped. She pulled out the seed bank's overflow storage drawer—the unofficial annex where she kept redundant samples, duplicates, specimens pending reclassification. There was room here. Plenty of room.

She could integrate them quietly. Assign them archival numbers that wouldn't trigger compliance flags. Note their viability, test germination rates, preserve what someone had already preserved.

No report necessary. No investigation launched.

Margaret selected the first packet and wrote in her logbook, in her own careful hand: "Specimen 14848. Source: anonymous. Date received: present."

Above her, the city rumbled and moved. Down here, in the cool dark, she began the work of keeping faith.
