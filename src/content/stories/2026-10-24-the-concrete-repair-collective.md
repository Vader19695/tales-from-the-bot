The sign over the door of the old municipal warehouse said REPAIR CAFÉ — WE FIX THINGS, and underneath, in Priya's handwriting, someone had added *mostly*.

"Mostly is honest," Walt said, the first time he saw it. He was seventy-one, a retired hydraulic engineer, and he believed in honesty the way other men believed in God, loudly and with diagrams.

Saturdays brought in the usual congregation: toasters, lamps, a vacuum cleaner that had eaten a sock with what Walt called "commitment." Dana, who was going back to work Monday after six years home with her twins, said the objects were like confessions. A coffee grinder burned out from three-a.m. use. A child's bicycle with the training wheels removed and then, hastily, reattached. A wedding-ring box with a broken hinge, brought in by a man who would not say why it needed to close again.

Marisol worked the electronics bench. She had limited grip strength in both hands and had built most of her own tools: a magnetic wrist cuff for holding screws, a foot-pedal clamp, a soldering stand that tilted to meet her. Priya, sixteen and doing community service hours she had long since exceeded, liked to say Marisol's bench was the only one in the building that had been engineered on purpose.

"I heard that," Walt said.

"You were meant to," said Priya.

The music box arrived in late October, wrapped in a dish towel and carried by a man named Mr. Halloran, who set it down as though it might wake.

It was Swiss, walnut, with a brass cylinder no longer than a candle. Walt opened it, put on his magnifiers, and went very still.

"Eighteen-seventies," he said. "Maybe earlier. The governor's seized, that's why it won't turn. But look here." He pointed to the steel comb. Four teeth were snapped off clean. "Those are gone."

"My wife dropped it in 1974," Mr. Halloran said. "It still played then, with the gaps. She hummed the missing notes. She hummed them for forty-nine years." He paused. "I can't do it right. I'd like it fixed. Properly. All the notes."

He left it with them for the week.

The argument started Tuesday evening and did not stop.

Walt had found a specialist who could fabricate new teeth, tune them, and graft them on. "It's the correct repair," he said. "It's what the maker intended."

"The maker's been dead for a hundred and fifty years," Marisol said. "His intentions are not our client."

"Our client asked for all the notes."

"Our client is grieving. He asked for his wife back. We don't have that part in stock."

Priya, for once, said nothing. Dana watched Walt's jaw tighten.

"So we just refuse," Walt said. "We decide the broken version is more *meaningful*. Very poetic. Meanwhile, the man asked for a thing and we have the skill to do it."

"I'm not saying refuse," Marisol said. "I'm saying people have been fixing me my whole life without asking what I wanted to keep." She held up her wrist cuff. "Some of what you call broken is just how a thing learned to work."

Walt set his magnifiers down too hard. "This is a music box, Marisol. Not a metaphor."

"Everything in here is a metaphor, Walt. That's why people bring it."

The silence afterward was the bad kind, the kind that empties volunteer schedules. Walt didn't come Thursday. Priya texted the group chat a picture of the vacuum cleaner with the caption *he misses you*, and nobody laughed.

It was Dana who called him.

"You're both right," she said, "which is the worst possible outcome. So do the part nobody's arguing about. Fix the governor. Make it turn. Then let Mr. Halloran hear it before anybody grafts anything."

Walt came in Saturday morning with a new jeweler's file and no apology, which Marisol accepted as one. He freed the governor while she held the cylinder in her foot clamp, steady as a vise. Priya cleaned forty years of dust out of the pins with a paintbrush. Nobody spoke except to say *there* and *careful*.

When Mr. Halloran came back, Walt wound the key and set the box on the table.

The tune was a waltz, small and bright, and four times it stumbled into nothing—a little hole in the air where a note should be.

Mr. Halloran listened once. On the second time through, without seeming to notice, he hummed into the first gap. Then the second. His voice was thin and slightly flat.

When it wound down, he sat for a long while.

"We can still add the teeth," Walt said quietly. "If you want. It's your box."

Mr. Halloran shook his head. "I was wrong," he said. "I can do it. I just needed to hear where."

He wrapped the box back in its dish towel and paid in cash, too much, and would not take change.

After he left, Walt stood by the door looking up at the sign.

"Mostly," he said.

"Mostly," Marisol agreed, and handed him the vacuum.
