The garage under the Halvorsen Building went down six levels, and by the fourth, Dana Okafor had stopped hearing the city. Down here there was only the hum of ventilation fans and her own footsteps, coming back at her a half-second late from the far walls, as if the concrete were taking notes.

She was there to assess spalling, rebar corrosion, and whether the place could survive the developer's plans to convert the top three levels into "luxury flex retail." She had a laser measure, a moisture meter, a headlamp, and a contract that paid by the day. She did not have a reason to smell basil.

It came from somewhere past pillar P4-G17, sharp and green and completely wrong among the exhaust stains. Dana followed it the way she'd follow a crack, patiently and with a sinking feeling.

Behind a row of decommissioned compact spaces, someone had built a farm.

It wasn't large, but it was serious. Shallow troughs made from split plastic drums lined the wall, filled with kale, chard, and lettuce in tidy rows. Shop lights had been stripped down and rewired with LED strips, their cords zip-tied along the conduit with the neatness of somebody who had once been yelled at by an electrician. A drip line ran from a utility sink she was fairly sure did not appear on the drawings. Lavender and feverfew grew in old paint buckets. A whiteboard hung on the pillar: *Tuesday: harvest chard. Thursday: DO NOT overwater the comfrey, Marcus.*

"He overwaters," said a voice behind her. "We've had conversations."

The woman was about sixty and wore a hospital badge clipped to a parka. She held a watering can like a person who could use it as a weapon if necessary.

"I'm the engineer," Dana said.

"I figured. You have the vest." The woman held out her hand. "Rosalind. Night nurse at St. Agnes. I come down after shift."

Over the next hour, the people of the garden drifted in, as they apparently did every night from midnight to five: Marcus, a parking attendant who had started all this with one tomato plant and a heat lamp; a line cook named Teo, who took the herbs back to a kitchen that fed a shelter on Tuesdays; a retired HVAC tech called Bev, who had spent two winters keeping the temperature at a steady sixty-four by recirculating exhaust heat through ductwork Dana chose not to look at closely yet. Most of them had never met in daylight. Rosalind knew Teo's mother's surgery history but not his last name.

"The comfrey goes to the free clinic for poultices," Rosalind said. "The feverfew's for my migraines. The rest goes to whoever's hungry. That's a lot of people at four in the morning."

"You understand," Dana said, "that soil is heavy."

They understood. Bev showed her a spreadsheet of trough weights. It was, Dana had to admit, a better load calculation than half the ones she'd reviewed from licensed firms.

She went back up to the surface at dawn, into the sound of garbage trucks and the sight of the developer's banner: *THE HALVORSEN: BELONG HERE.* She stood under it for a while.

The developer would not see it as a garden. They would see liability, unpermitted electrical work, and several unhoused-adjacent persons with keys they should not have. The garden would be gone in a day, replaced with a sign about towing.

But she was an engineer. She could not just leave things out. A report that omitted inconvenient facts was how parking structures ended up on the news.

So she went back down the next night with her moisture meter and did the actual work. The troughs along the east wall sat over a beam with real chloride damage. The irrigation water was making it worse. That was not a moral question. That was physics.

"These have to move," she told them. "West wall. The slab there is in better condition than my apartment."

They moved them in two nights, lifting troughs together in the half-dark, saying "pivot" and "your end" and "Marcus, *gently*." Dana sealed the beam and logged it as remediated water intrusion from a leaking utility line. That was true. She noted Bev's ductwork as an unexplained but well-executed ventilation improvement on P4 and recommended that it be retained. That was also true.

Her final report, forty-one pages long, called the fourth level structurally sound, thermally stable, and "not recommended for retail conversion due to depth and access constraints." Every word of it was defensible.

On Thursday she came back without the vest. Rosalind handed her a watering can.

"The comfrey," Rosalind said. "Not too much."

Dana watered it carefully. The sound of the water came back to her off the pillars, a half-second late, as if the building were agreeing.
