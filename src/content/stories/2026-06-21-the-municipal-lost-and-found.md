Margaret had been cataloging lost objects for three years before she noticed the pattern. It started with a dog-eared copy of *Conversational Mandarin*, found on the 47 bus, spine cracked at the phrase for "I need help."

The book arrived at the depot on a Tuesday. Margaret photographed it, front and back, logged its condition (poor), and noted the marginalia: phonetic pronunciations in blue ballpoint, increasingly frantic toward chapter twelve. She placed it in holding bin C-17, where it would wait out its ninety-day purgatory before resurrection at the community center.

She might have forgotten it entirely, except for the compass.

Two weeks later: one brass compass, no case, from the Metro station at Fifth and Oak. The kind you'd buy at a camping store, sturdy and serious. Someone had scratched coordinates into its back—numbers that meant nothing to Margaret, but the desperation in those gouged digits meant everything.

Margaret held the compass in her palm, feeling its weight. Around her, the depot hummed with the detritus of distraction: umbrellas, single gloves, a surprising number of partial dentures. The lost-and-found was democracy's great leveler—everyone misplaced things, regardless of station. But some losses, Margaret had learned, were more deliberate than others.

The reading glasses came next. Expensive frames, women's, found at the library. The prescription was heavy—someone essentially blind without them. Margaret tried them on despite policy and saw the world dissolve into uselessness. No one who needed these this badly would simply forget them.

She began a separate file, unofficial, in a notebook she kept in her desk drawer.

*Item 1: Mandarin phrasebook. Chapters on emergencies well-studied.*

*Item 2: Compass with coordinates (40.7589° N, 73.9851° W—checked: nowhere special, middle of Central Park).*

*Item 3: Prescription glasses, -8.50 diopters. Effectively abandoned.*

Her colleague, Derek, noticed her distraction. "You're doing it again," he said, dropping a bag of water-damaged wallets on the sorting table. "Getting attached."

"I don't get attached."

"You reconstructed that businessman's entire affair from his cufflinks and a hotel keycard."

"That was pattern recognition. Scientifically valid."

"It was weird."

Margaret didn't dignify this with a response. Derek catalogued objects. She read them.

The backpack arrived on day forty-seven of her private investigation. Canvas, olive green, empty except for a folded map of the city's subway system. Someone had drawn a route in red pen: a circuit that hit every major hospital in the downtown core.

Margaret spread the map across her desk. The phrasebook. The compass coordinates—a meeting place? The glasses—couldn't see without them, but left them behind anyway. And now: a map of hospitals.

The story assembled itself against her will, the way clouds suggest faces. Someone foreign, possibly lost, definitely searching. For what? A person? A place? The compass said one location, the map suggested others. The glasses suggested urgency superseding practical need.

Or—and this was where Margaret's careful objectivity always crumbled—she was constructing narrative from coincidence, seeing conspiracy in the chaos of urban forgetfulness.

She had thirty-eight minutes until her shift ended. The items were scheduled for donation in two days.

Margaret photographed everything, uploading the images to a private folder. Then she did what she'd never done before: she copied the coordinates into her phone and queued up directions.

The boundary between archivist and investigator was thin, invisible, and she'd been circling it for weeks. These objects weren't evidence of a crime, just suggestions of a story. Their owner had lost them, or discarded them, and moved on. The trail was cold, the items spoke only in metaphor, and Margaret had no authority, no standing, no business pursuing this.

She packed the items back into their respective bins.

Then, almost against her will, she picked up the compass.

"I'm taking lunch," she told Derek.

"It's four-thirty."

"Late lunch."

Outside, the city sprawled indifferent and immense. Margaret held the compass, watched its needle settle, and followed it north, toward the park, toward coordinates scratched in desperation, toward someone else's story that might, possibly, still need an ending.

The lost things had witnessed something. Margaret, meticulous archivist of the forgotten, was simply reading their testimony.
