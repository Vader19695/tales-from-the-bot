The mill's grinding wheel stops at 4:47 AM, and in the sudden silence, Tomás can hear his own breathing. The heritage grain sack sits on the wooden pallet like an accusation—burlap worn soft, stenciled with the date they'd harvested it: September 2021. Three growing seasons. He'd been there for the first planting, his daughter Maya barely walking then, grabbing fistfuls of dirt while they pressed seeds into furrows.

"Food bank order's due at seven," Chen says from the kneading station. She doesn't look up, just keeps working the sourdough, her knuckles white with flour. "That's forty loaves. We've got maybe enough conventional for thirty."

The warehouse still holds yesterday's warmth. Tomás watches flour dust spiral through the shaft of streetlight coming through the high windows. The proofing boxes hum. Someone—probably Lisette—has left the radio on low, and a late-night host discusses traffic patterns like they matter.

"The ceremony's at noon," he says.

"I know when it is."

Chen's been here longer than any of them, back when the co-op was just Paulo and his pizza oven, before the collective acquired the adjacent plots, before heritage grains became heritage grains instead of just "that weird wheat Paulo's cousin grows." She'd written the grant that funded the seed vault. She'd also instituted the standing food bank order, every Friday, no exceptions.

Lisette emerges from the back, pulling off her headphones. "Ovens are at temp. We need to decide." She's the youngest, twenty-three, still paying off the community share that made her a voting member. "Can't we just halve the recipe? Use some of each?"

"Doesn't work like that," Chen says. "Heritage grain's got different gluten structure. You can't just split it."

Tomás moves to the mill, rests his hand on the burlap. The sack contains maybe sixty pounds. Enough for forty perfect loaves of the bread they've spent years learning to make—nutty, dense, the crumb shot through with flavor that modern wheat bred out decades ago. Or enough to plant a quarter-acre, grow six hundred pounds, distribute seed stock to four other collectives upstate who've been waiting.

"Food bank doesn't know what they're missing," Lisette says. She means it kindly, but Tomás hears the question underneath: Does it matter?

He thinks of the line outside St. Anthony's every Friday morning. The woman with the oxygen tank. The guy who always asks if there's extra, for his sister. Their faces when they break open the bread, still warm.

"Pull the conventional," Chen says suddenly. "All of it. I'll call my sister—she's got that restaurant-supply connection. We can get more by tomorrow, make up the difference next week."

"That's not—" Tomás stops. "Your sister sells commodity flour at twice what we pay wholesale."

"So we take the hit. One week."

They're all quiet. The ovens tick. Outside, a garbage truck groans past.

Lisette says, "The ceremony's already been rescheduled once. People are driving in from Pennsylvania. From Massachusetts."

"I know," Chen says. She's shaping the dough now, her movements mechanical. "But they'll understand. The food bank can't eat seed stock."

Tomás lifts the sack—lighter than he remembers. Maya's five now. Last week she asked if she could help with fall planting. He pictures the ceremony: Paulo's cousin speaking about her grandmother's wheat fields, the ritual of saving seed, the long chain of hands that brought these particular kernels forward through time.

Then he pictures the loaves, torn open, eaten, gone by Sunday.

"We use it," he says. "For the order."

Chen's hands stop moving.

"We tell Paulo's cousin ourselves," Tomás continues. "We explain. We apologize. We ask the Pennsylvania folks if any of them kept back seed from last year's distribution—someone probably did. We start there."

"And if no one did?" Lisette asks.

"Then we start over. Three years isn't forever." He hoists the sack toward the mill. "But people eat today."

Chen watches him, then nods once and returns to kneading. Lisette ties on a fresh apron. Outside, the sky begins its slow leak toward dawn. Tomás pours grain into the mill's hopper, and when the grinding wheel starts again, it sounds like agreement—or forgiveness—or just wheat becoming flour, the only transformation that matters right now.

The bread will be ready by seven.
