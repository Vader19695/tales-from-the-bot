The break room at Municipal Water Treatment Plant #3 smelled like burnt coffee and industrial soap. Rita spread the printouts across the chipped Formica table while Marcus stood guard at the door. Tom sat across from her, his reading glasses reflecting the fluorescent lights.

"February twelfth," Rita said, pointing to a column of numbers. "Chlorine levels logged at 2.1 ppm. But look at the digital backup—1.7."

Tom leaned closer. At seventy-two, he'd spent forty years as an industrial chemist before his company shuttered and he took this job to supplement his pension. "That's below safe threshold."

"It's not just one day." Rita flipped through pages, her dancer's fingers still graceful despite six months of valve-turning and gauge-reading. She'd retrained after her hip gave out, trading tutus for steel-toed boots. "Six months of discrepancies. Someone's been changing the paper logs after the fact."

Marcus finally spoke, his voice like gravel. "The phosphate levels too. March through June." In fifteen years as maintenance supervisor, he'd said less than most people said in fifteen days. When he did speak, people listened.

They sat in silence, the hum of pumps and filters providing a white noise soundtrack to their discovery. Rita had found the first anomaly two weeks ago while cross-referencing records for her certification training. She'd told herself it was an error. Then she found another. And another.

"Could be incompetence," Tom offered, but his tone said he didn't believe it.

"Too consistent," Rita said. "And look—the changes always make things look *better* than they were. Safer. Cleaner."

Marcus pulled out his phone, scrolled through photos. "Found these barrels behind the north filtration building last Thursday. They were gone by Monday." He showed them: unmarked fifty-gallon drums, rusty and anonymous.

Tom removed his glasses, rubbed his eyes. "We should tell Patterson."

Patterson was their plant manager, a smooth-talking administrator who'd transferred from Public Works six months ago. Six months. The same timeframe as the altered logs.

"Patterson signed off on all these logs," Rita said quietly.

The break room air felt suddenly thick. Through the window, they could see the city's lights spreading across the valley—rows of houses, apartment buildings, the hospital on the hill. Two hundred thousand people drinking water that someone had lied about.

"We could be wrong," Rita said, but she was testing the words, not believing them.

"Could be," Marcus agreed. But then: "My granddaughter lives on Maple Street. She's three."

Tom folded his hands, the gesture Rita recognized from their training sessions—it meant he was working through a problem. "If we go to city council, they'll want proof. We'll need to admit we accessed records without authorization. They'll question our expertise. Rita, you're barely certified. I'm a retiree they hired for pennies. Marcus, when did you last file a formal report?"

"Never."

"Exactly. We're expendable. We report this, they'll bury us with it."

Rita thought of her apartment, her student loans for the certification program, her mother's care facility bills. "I can't lose this job."

"None of us can," Tom said.

They sat with that truth. Outside, a delivery truck pulled up to the loading dock. Normal operations. Business as usual. The city trusting them to keep the water clean, never knowing that trust had been betrayed.

Marcus stood, walked to the window. "My daughter wanted me to retire last year. Said I'd done enough." He turned to face them. "I don't think I have."

Rita met his eyes, then Tom's. She thought of her dancing career, how she'd known the exact moment to stop—when staying would have meant compromising everything she believed her art should be.

"My certification supervisor at the state EPA," she said slowly. "She gave me her card. Said to call if I ever saw anything irregular."

Tom nodded. "I have contacts at the university. The engineering department still owes me for consulting work."

"I know a reporter," Marcus said. "Used to date my sister."

They looked at each other across the table, three people who'd never expected to be heroes, who'd taken this work because they needed it, who now faced losing everything for doing what they'd been hired to do all along: keep the water clean.

"We'll need copies of everything," Tom said.

Rita was already gathering the printouts. "I'll make them tonight."

"Then we go together," Marcus said. "All three of us. They can't disappear us all."

Outside, the city slept, unaware. Inside, the third shift kept working.
