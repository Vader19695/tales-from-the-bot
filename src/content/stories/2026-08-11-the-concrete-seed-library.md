I found Dr. Brennan's seed archive on a Tuesday, wedged between form 27-B and my third cup of break room coffee.

The work order said "structural assessment, sub-level three," which in municipal contractor language meant: poke around the forgotten parts of Parking Garage Seven and confirm nothing's actively collapsing. I'd been doing these inspections for eight years. The job was mostly rodent droppings and leaking pipes.

Storage unit 3-J wasn't on my clipboard.

The door had a combination lock—already a violation—and hummed with electricity that definitely wasn't code-compliant. I should have photographed it and moved on. Instead, I tried the handle. Unlocked.

Inside, the air hit differently. Cool. Deliberate. Not the dank basement cold I'd expected, but the careful temperature of something being preserved. Rows of climate-controlled cabinets lined the walls, each drawer labeled in meticulous handwriting: "Cherokee Purple Tomato, 2019." "Mortgage Lifter, 2021." "Moon and Stars Watermelon."

"You're early."

I turned. The man in the doorway was seventy-something, wearing a cardigan despite the controlled environment. His eyes held the calm resignation of someone who'd been expecting this moment.

"Dr. Brennan?" I checked my clipboard pointlessly. "This space isn't registered for—"

"I know what it isn't registered for." He stepped inside, closing the door behind him. "How much time do I have?"

"For what?"

"To convince you this matters."

I should have left then. Should have called my supervisor, initiated the violation process. Instead, I watched him open a drawer and remove a small glass vial filled with seeds.

"Brandywine tomato," he said. "My grandmother grew these in Pennsylvania. You can't buy them anymore. The grocery distributors chose varieties that ship well, that stack uniformly, that ripen on command under warehouse lights. They didn't choose the ones that taste like summer tastes when you're eight years old."

"Dr. Brennan, I'm just here to—"

"Three thousand two hundred and seventeen varieties." He wasn't listening to me, or perhaps he was listening to something more important than my objections. "Beans that sustained communities through the Depression. Squash seeds carried by refugees across three continents. Corn that doesn't require pesticides corporations own the patents for."

He moved between cabinets like a librarian, pulling drawers open. Each one held hundreds of carefully labeled envelopes and vials, dated, catalogued, annotated with growing conditions and histories.

"The city leases this garage to a development company next month," I said, finding my professional voice. "They're converting it to luxury condos."

"I know."

"You'll need to relocate—"

"Where?" The question wasn't aggressive, just genuinely curious. "University programs have been cut. The agricultural extension office closed last year. Community gardens don't have climate control or fire suppression systems." He gestured at the sophisticated equipment. "This represents three years of my pension. I don't have another three years to rebuild."

I looked at my clipboard. At the forms that would trigger demolition, displacement, the tidy erasure of this unauthorized archive. I thought about the developer's renderings I'd seen—all glass balconies and rooftop yoga studios. Profitable. Legal. Progress.

"How many seeds can you move in two weeks?" I heard myself ask.

Dr. Brennan studied my face. "With help? Maybe forty percent."

I pulled out my phone, not to call my supervisor, but to photograph the catalog system. "Show me how your labeling works. And we'll need to document which varieties are most irreplaceable."

"You're not reporting this?"

"I'm reporting that sub-level three requires additional assessment." I met his eyes. "The structural evaluation is complex. Could take weeks to complete properly. Bureaucracy moves slowly, Dr. Brennan. Sometimes that's a feature, not a bug."

He nodded slowly, understanding blooming into something like hope.

"After work, I have a garage," I continued. "Unfinished. Terrible insulation. But my brother-in-law's an electrician."

It wasn't a perfect solution. It wasn't even a particularly good one. But as Dr. Brennan opened another drawer—Mississippi Pink Beans, saved by a family through slavery and Jim Crow—I understood that public good sometimes looks like borrowed time and an unfinished garage.

Sometimes it looks like choosing which paperwork to file slowly.

I picked up the first box of seed catalogs. "Let's start moving."
