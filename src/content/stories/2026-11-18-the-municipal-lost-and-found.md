The lost-and-found depot occupied the basement of Harlow City Hall, between the boiler room and a storage closet nobody had opened since the Carter administration. Edith Vance had volunteered there three days a week for six years, ever since retiring from the public library. She had made the depot what the library had never quite allowed her to be: a keeper of small, unofficial histories.

She catalogued everything. A brass trumpet mouthpiece, 1974, found in the bleachers at Lincoln Field. One white kid glove, left hand, 1962. A wedding ring engraved *R.M. Always*, which she had returned last spring to a widower who wept so quietly at the counter that she pretended to file something until he finished. The unclaimed objects stayed on the shelves in labeled boxes, and Edith had come to believe they spoke in a language of their own. A single mitten meant hurry. A pair of reading glasses meant someone had been sitting still, thinking, and then stood up into a different thought entirely.

The cardboard box arrived on a Tuesday in October, delivered by a county worker clearing out the old Greyhound station before demolition. Most of it was predictable: umbrellas, a paperback with a bus ticket for a bookmark, a hairbrush. At the bottom lay a notebook made of construction paper, folded and stapled, its cover decorated in crayon with a bird that might have been a robin.

Inside, in pencil, in a hand that pressed too hard and wobbled anyway:

*This is the book of me and Wren. March 1987.*

Edith sat down.

Wren, it emerged over twenty-two pages, was a girl who lived in the walls of the author's house and could only be seen when the lights were off. Wren liked grape popsicles. Wren was not afraid of thunder. Wren knew why Dad was sleeping on the couch and said it wasn't anybody's fault, especially not a kid's. Wren said that people who leave sometimes leave because they are lost, not because you are.

Edith read it twice. Then she went home and did not sleep well.

She told herself to leave it alone. Not every object wanted to be returned; some had been set down on purpose, and a librarian learned to respect a closed book. But the journal offered clues the way children offer secrets, half-hoping to be caught. A spelling list taped to page nine bore the header *Mrs. Okafor, Grade 2*. Page fourteen mentioned a dog named Biscuit and a mother who worked "the night shift at the Starlite." The Starlite Diner had closed in 1991, but Edith had eaten pie there every Friday of her thirties.

Old yearbooks lived in the library annex. Mrs. Okafor's second-grade class of 1987 had nineteen children. Only one, Jonah Teague, had a mother listed in the Starlite's surviving employee records, which Edith found, after considerable charm, in the attic of the owner's daughter.

Jonah Teague now taught high school chemistry two towns over. Edith wrote him a letter, rewrote it four times, and finally sent three sentences: *I believe I have something of yours from 1987. You are under no obligation. It is safe either way.*

He came on a Thursday, a tall man in his forties with careful hands and a chemistry teacher's habit of observing before speaking. Edith set the journal on the counter between them and stepped back.

He turned the pages slowly. At the last page he stopped, and Edith watched his face do something complicated.

She had read that page too, of course. *Wren, we are moving to Aunt Carol's tomorrow. You can't come because you live in this house. So I am leaving our book at the bus place where the lost things go. When you get lost you can find it and know I didn't forget you.*

"My father took the bus out of town that spring," Jonah said finally. "I thought, if the bus station was where people went to be lost, maybe that's where Wren would end up too. I was seven." He laughed, a little ruined. "I left it in the lost-and-found bin while my mother bought our tickets. I made her promise not to look."

"Did your father ever come back?"

"No." He closed the journal gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child. "But I turned out all right. Better than all right. I think I needed somebody to tell me it wasn't my fault, so I invented her."

Edith waited. Restraint, she had learned, was its own kind of kindness.

He slid the journal back across the counter. "Would you keep it? It's where she'd look."

After he left, Edith took a fresh index card and wrote in her neatest hand:

*Handmade journal, construction paper, 1987. Found: Greyhound station. Status: Claimed. Owner: Wren.*

She shelved it beside the white glove and the trumpet mouthpiece, among the things still waiting, and turned off the light.
