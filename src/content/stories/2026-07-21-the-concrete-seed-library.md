The parking garage on Millworth Street had been dying for years before it became something else entirely. Level Three, abandoned since the steel mill closed, smelled of motor oil and rain until the night Dr. Eleanor Voss carried in her first bucket of soil.

She was seventy-three and had nothing to lose but time. Her university greenhouse had been sold off, her research on heritage crops deemed "non-essential." So she reclaimed a corner of the concrete tomb, installed grow lights powered by an extension cord snaking from a maintenance outlet, and planted seeds she'd saved from thirty years of fieldwork.

Eleanor worked alone for three months before she found the note wedged between her tomato pots: "Beautiful. Can I help? —M"

She left a reply. The next week, she arrived to find new shelving units—professional grade, perfectly level—and another note: "Built these. More coming. —Marcus."

Marcus Webb was a contractor watching his business evaporate as the city hollowed out. He'd driven past the garage a hundred times making deliveries for the food app that now paid his bills, and one night he'd noticed the faint glow from Level Three. Instead of reporting it, he investigated. Now he spent his earnings on lumber and hardware, building something that mattered.

The third member found them by accident. Jade Chen, seventeen and perpetually hungry in a house where groceries were scarce, had followed the smell of fresh basil. She stood in the entrance to their expanding garden, backpack slumping off one shoulder, and said, "This is impossible."

"Only improbable," Eleanor replied, handing her a tomato.

Jade became their evening guardian, arriving after school to water and weed, staying until the last bus home. She catalogued everything in a battered notebook: thirty-seven varieties of vegetables, eighteen herbs, a small library of seed packets stored in a repurposed filing cabinet. She never asked the others their names. They never asked hers. The garden was their shared secret, their mutual act of defiance against a city that had forgotten them.

The fourth member was discovered, not introduced. Eleanor arrived one November evening to find a stranger crouched among the lettuce—a food delivery driver named Anton, who'd been using Level Three to escape between orders. "I won't tell," he stammered.

"We know," Eleanor said. "There's an extra trowel by the squash."

They developed a rhythm, orbiting each other across the weeks. Marcus left architectural sketches for expanded beds. Jade posted watering schedules. Anton donated restaurant surplus—day-old bread, wilted produce—that became compost. Eleanor taught them all, through carefully written notes, about companion planting and seed saving. They transformed seven parking spaces into a thriving ecosystem, never speaking more than necessary, never meeting all at once.

The notice appeared in March: "DEMOLITION SCHEDULED—JUNE 15. LUXURY APARTMENTS COMING SOON."

They gathered for the first time together, standing beneath the grow lights at 2 a.m., four strangers who'd built something green in the gray.

"We could go public," Jade said. "People would care. Right?"

"They might," Eleanor acknowledged. "Or they might not."

"I need this job," Anton said quietly. "Can't afford trouble with the city."

Marcus studied his calloused hands. "We all need something."

Eleanor walked to the seed cabinet, running her fingers over the labeled drawers. Centuries of genetic diversity, saved from extinction. She thought of her career, reduced to ashes and funding cuts. She thought of these three people she barely knew but somehow understood completely.

"We've been cultivating two things here," she said. "Seeds and the knowledge to use them."

By morning, the grow lights were gone. The planters were emptied, their soil distributed into dozens of bags. The seeds were divided into four collections, carefully documented.

One week later, a hundred small gardens appeared across the city—window boxes, backyard plots, community lots. Four people, working separately, giving away seeds and instructions, teaching neighbors what they'd learned in secret.

The garage fell in June. By July, green things were growing everywhere, tended by strangers who'd never know where the seeds came from.

Some gardens, Eleanor thought, aren't meant to stay hidden forever. Some just need to scatter their seeds first.
