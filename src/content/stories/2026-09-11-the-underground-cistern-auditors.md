The ladder descended forty feet into darkness that smelled of limestone and time. Maria went first, her headlamp cutting a pale circle through the cool air. Behind her came Derek, then Sam, each carrying clipboards wrapped in plastic and moisture meters that had seen better days.

"Watch the seventeenth rung," Maria called up. "It's loose."

"Everything's loose," Derek muttered. "This whole structure should have been condemned in 1987."

They'd come to catalog what remained of Cistern 7, one of the last Victorian-era water chambers still intact beneath the Riverside neighborhood. Next month, the city would pump it full of concrete—standard procedure for abandoned infrastructure that might someday collapse and swallow a street.

Maria's boots hit the bottom with a splash. Six inches of standing water, as expected. The ceiling arched twelve feet overhead, supported by brick columns that had held firm since McKinley was president. She swept her light across the space and froze.

"What the hell?" Sam landed beside her.

The cistern wasn't abandoned. Not exactly.

Someone had built a platform above the waterline—pressure-treated lumber, carefully constructed. On it sat mismatched chairs, a couple of card tables, even a small bookshelf wrapped in plastic sheeting. Mason jars lined a ledge, holding candles burned down to various heights. A sign, hand-painted, read: "RIVERSIDE UNDERGROUND SOCIETY - PLEASE LEAVE AS YOU FOUND IT."

Derek whistled low. "How long you think this has been here?"

Maria approached the bookshelf. The titles ranged from battered paperback mysteries to a well-thumbed copy of *Leaves of Grass*. A logbook sat on top. She opened it, angling her headlamp to read.

*November 12 - Chess club. Marty finally beat someone. (It was Phil, so half credit.)*

*December 3 - Book discussion got heated. Note: no more Ayn Rand.*

*January 18 - Just needed somewhere quiet. Thanks for existing, cistern.*

The entries went back three years. Different handwriting, different people. Quiet confessions of loneliness, reports of small gatherings, gratitude for a place where neighbors could be neighbors without commercial transaction or digital mediation.

Sam was examining the platform's construction. "This is quality work. Whoever built this knew what they were doing. Even accounted for seasonal water level changes."

Derek flipped through the logbook over Maria's shoulder. "We have to report this."

"Obviously," Maria said.

"I mean, it's a safety violation. Unauthorized access to city infrastructure. The liability alone—"

"I said obviously." But her voice lacked conviction.

She walked the perimeter, running her hand along the brick. The cistern's structural integrity was actually remarkable. The columns showed minimal efflorescence, no significant cracking. Someone had even repointed sections of mortar—probably whoever had built the platform.

"Doing the city's work for free," she murmured.

Sam joined her. "You know what happens when we file the report. They'll seal all the access points first thing. Might even accelerate the concrete pour."

"That's the plan regardless," Derek said. "We're just cataloging before decommission."

"The plan assumes the structure is degraded and dangerous." Sam tapped his moisture meter against a column. "But it's not. It's sound. Could last another hundred years."

Maria knew what he was suggesting. Their report would determine priority. A structure in immediate danger got sealed within weeks. Something stable could sit in the queue for years—long enough for budget reshuffling, for personnel changes, for the slow bureaucratic forgetting that preserved so many things neither officially protected nor actively destroyed.

Derek crossed his arms. "So we lie?"

"We'd be precise," Sam said. "Careful. We'd note the stable sections, the areas needing monitoring. All true."

"And omit the speakeasy."

"The what?" Maria looked at him.

Derek gestured around. "That's what this is. A speakeasy for ordinary human connection. For whatever people can't find up there anymore."

They stood in silence, headlamps crisscrossing the dark water. From above came the muffled sound of traffic, the city grinding through another day.

Maria made her decision. "We document the structure, not the contents. Someone's furniture isn't our department. And this structure"—she tapped her pen against her clipboard—"shows remarkable integrity for its age. Recommend continued monitoring at thirty-six month intervals."

Derek studied her face, then nodded slowly. "Thirty-six months. That's very thorough of us."

"We're very thorough people," Sam agreed.

They began their measurements, carefully recording the truth of brick and water, and saying nothing at all about the small republic of connection that flickered in the margins, forty feet below the lonely streets.
