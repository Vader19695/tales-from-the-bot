Dr. Sarah Chen had been reading feet for twelve years, and she could tell you more about a person from their calluses than most therapists learned in fifty-minute sessions.

The ballet dancer with the accountant's posture. The marathon runner who favored his left side because he carried his daughter on his right hip. The woman whose pinky toes had gone numb from standing in heels at open houses, selling condos she couldn't afford to live in herself.

"You're spending too much time on concrete," Sarah told the young man across from her, his foot cradled in her gloved hands like something precious and broken.

He laughed. "I'm a line cook."

"I know," she said. Because of course she did. The pressure pattern on his metatarsals told the story of someone who stood in one spot, shifting weight, reaching for things. The burn scar near his ankle confirmed it.

The clinic where she worked was wedged between a check-cashing place and a pharmacy in Washington Heights. Her patients were mostly people whose feet bore the full weight of their survival. She saw deliverymen, home health aides, security guards, stock clerks. People who couldn't afford to stop moving.

She learned to read their maps. The overnight nurses had a particular callus constellation—forefoot pressure from the constant walking punctuated by heel wear from standing at bedsides. New parents developed temporary asymmetries, always shifting the baby to the same side. The people waiting in government office queues for six, seven, eight hours had a distinctive bilateral pattern, weight distributed with the patience of those who had no choice.

Then there was Mr. Kowalski.

He'd been coming every eight weeks for two years. Seventy-three, Polish accent still thick as brick. He never said much beyond pleasantries, but his feet told her everything. He was a night janitor—she knew from the wear pattern consistent with pushing industrial equipment. He walked to work—the left heel showed extra wear, suggesting he favored that side going uphill. And recently, something had changed. The right forefoot showed new pressure, like he'd started carrying something heavy on his left side.

"How's the grandson?" she'd asked at his last appointment, guessing.

His face had lit up. "You remember! My daughter, she is working two jobs now. I watch him Tuesdays and Thursdays."

The baby weight, she thought. He carries him in a sling on his left hip.

But then Mr. Kowalski missed his appointment. And the next one. Sarah rescheduled him twice, but he didn't show. She told herself it was normal. People moved, changed insurance, died. Life happened.

Still, she couldn't stop thinking about those feet. The story they'd been telling.

On her lunch break, she did something she'd never done before. She looked him up in the system, found his address. She told herself she'd just walk by. Make sure he was okay. It was practically on her route home anyway.

The building was a walk-up in Inwood. She stood outside for ten minutes, feeling ridiculous, before climbing to the third floor. She knocked.

A woman in scrubs answered, looking exhausted. Mr. Kowalski's daughter.

"I'm Dr. Chen," Sarah said. "Your father's podiatrist. He missed some appointments, and I—"

"Oh God, thank you for coming." The woman's eyes filled. "He fell three months ago. Broke his hip. He's been too proud to call and cancel, kept saying he'd reschedule when he could walk better."

Sarah found herself inside, being led to a small bedroom where Mr. Kowalski sat in a chair, his grandson sleeping in a bassinet nearby.

"Doctor!" His surprise was genuine. "You didn't have to—"

"Yes, I did," Sarah said, and surprised herself by meaning it. "Your feet have been walking a very long way. They deserve someone who pays attention."

She knelt down, the old gesture automatic and dignified. His feet were swollen, neglected. The map had changed—atrophy on the right side, compensatory pressure on the left.

"Let me see what story they're telling now," she said.

And there, in that cramped apartment with afternoon light slanting through cheap blinds, Sarah began the slow work of reading someone back into their life, one careful touch at a time.
