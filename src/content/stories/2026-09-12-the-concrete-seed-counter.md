The fluorescent lights buzzed in Section D-7 of what used to be the third level of the Riverside Parking Structure. Margaret Chen set down her magnifying glass and rubbed her eyes. Sixty-three packets today, each one labeled in the same cramped handwriting: *Tomato—Broadway & 5th, 1987.*

"That's the fourth one from Broadway and Fifth," she said.

Tyler didn't look up from his spreadsheet. "So someone really liked that intersection."

"In 1987, nothing was at Broadway and Fifth except the plastics factory." Margaret had lived in this city for forty-two years before retiring from the university. "Trust me, nobody was growing tomatoes there."

"Well, someone was." Destiny held up another packet. "Squash—Broadway & 4th, 1987. Same handwriting. Look at that capital S."

Tyler sighed and added another row to his database. Three weeks into this project, and they'd only processed 2,800 packets. At this rate, the community garden distribution wouldn't happen until September, and his boss was already breathing down his neck about timelines.

Margaret took the squash packet from Destiny and held it beside the tomato. "The handwriting's identical. Same ink. Same paper stock." She flipped them over. On the back, tiny pencil sketches: a water tower on one, a fence post on the other.

"Maybe it's just someone's personal collection," Tyler said. "Their gardening diary or whatever."

"Ten thousand seeds' worth?" Destiny had moved to the next box, pulling packets seemingly at random. "Here's another—Broadway & 6th, 1988. Beans this time."

Margaret stood and walked to the whiteboard they'd requisitioned from the old parking attendant booth. She drew a crude map of downtown, marking the intersections. "Read me more addresses."

For the next hour, they called out locations while Margaret plotted points. Broadway featured heavily, but so did Harrison Street, Tenth Avenue, and the entire Riverside district. The dates ranged from 1985 to 1999, then stopped.

"That's when they tore down the Riverside neighborhood," Margaret said quietly. "Eminent domain. They built the convention center."

Tyler finally looked up from his laptop. "These are from places that don't exist anymore?"

"The places exist. The buildings don't." Margaret studied the map. "Broadway and Fifth—that whole block was row houses before the factory. Before that, probably the 1920s, it was the Italian district. My professor did a study..."

She trailed off, moving to a box they'd marked as "processed." She found packet 0847: *Basil—Harrison & 3rd, 1986.* On the back, a sketch of a doorway with a distinct arch. "I know this door. It was on Harrison. Green paint, brass knocker shaped like a lion. I walked past it every day when I worked at the university library."

Destiny leaned over her shoulder. "What happened to it?"

"Demolished in '98. They built luxury condos."

Tyler had abandoned his spreadsheet and was now looking through packets himself. "This is weird, right? This is objectively weird?"

"Someone made a map," Margaret said. "A living map. They collected seeds from every garden in the blocks that were going to be destroyed."

"But why send them here now?" Destiny asked. "Why anonymous?"

Margaret returned to the whiteboard, counting the marked intersections. "Because we can give them back. Not to those exact locations, but to the community gardens. These seeds—assuming they're still viable—they're genetic descendants of the city that was."

"Assuming they're still viable is doing a lot of work in that sentence," Tyler said. "Seeds don't last forever."

"Some do." Margaret picked up a packet labeled *Beans—Tenth & Maple, 1995.* "Beans can last ten to fifteen years under proper storage. Tomatoes, squash, up to a decade. And if this person knew enough to catalog all this, they probably knew how to store them."

"So we still have to verify all ten thousand," Tyler said. "Germination tests."

"Which means months more work," Destiny added.

They stood in silence, the fluorescent lights humming overhead, surrounded by boxes of seeds from gardens that no longer existed, cataloged by someone who wanted them remembered.

Finally, Tyler closed his laptop. "I'll request a deadline extension. Tell them we need to do proper germination testing for quality assurance."

Margaret smiled. "I'll draft a testing protocol."

"I'll keep cataloging," Destiny said. "Someone should finish the map."

They returned to their stations in the converted parking garage, three mismatched people doing work that mattered, though no one would call it urgent. Above them, the city continued changing. Below, in the fluorescent-lit dark, they preserved what had been lost.
