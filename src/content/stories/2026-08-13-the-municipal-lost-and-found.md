Margaret Chen stood before the warehouse with her clipboard and her orthopedic shoes, feeling oddly like a general surveying a battlefield she'd already lost. The Lost & Found depot stretched before her—three stories of industrial brutalism that had, for forty-two years, housed the city's orphaned belongings according to her beautifully Byzantine cataloguing system.

In two weeks, it would be a parking structure. Progress, they called it.

"Mrs. Chen?" A young man with a municipal ID badge approached. "I'm Derek. They said you wanted to do a final inventory?"

"Ms. Chen," she corrected automatically. "And yes. Someone should document what's being lost."

Derek looked uncomfortable. "Most of it's already been disposed of. We're just down to the unclaimed items from the last decade."

"Only a decade," Margaret said drily. "How manageable."

The interior smelled of dust and time. Margaret's system had divided the space by object category, then by date, then by a color-coding scheme that had made perfect sense in 1982. As she walked the familiar aisles, her footsteps echoing in the cavernous space, she felt something she couldn't quite name. Not nostalgia. Something sharper.

She began in Section 7-B: Paper Goods.

The postcards caught her attention immediately—not because they were together, but because they shouldn't have been. They'd been found across the city over six years, different locations, different dates. Someone had placed them adjacent to each other. Derek, probably, clearing shelves without understanding the system.

Margaret picked up the first one. Prague, 1984. "Still looking," written in cramped handwriting. The next: Rome, 1986. "Still running." Then Amsterdam, Barcelona, Tokyo. "Still dreaming." "Still lost." "Still yours."

She frowned. Coincidence, certainly. Yet something made her slip them into her cardigan pocket.

In Section 12-A: Metallics, she found the keys. Seventeen of them, spread across five years of acquisitions. Separately, they'd been unremarkable. But someone—the same careless someone—had dumped them together, and now Margaret noticed what she shouldn't have: each key had a tiny mark filed into its head. Sequential marks. Numbering them.

"This is ridiculous," she muttered to herself. But she collected them anyway.

The sketches were in Section 3-C: Artwork and Personal Papers. Technical drawings, really. Found over four years at various bus stops. Separately, they'd seemed like engineering homework. Together, they formed something else: plans for a door. Not a metaphorical door—a specific door with specific measurements, specific hardware. Seventeen locks.

Margaret sat down on a crate marked "UMBRELLAS, 2019" and spread everything before her. Her hands were shaking slightly, which annoyed her. She was seventy-three years old and had spent her career cataloguing randomness. She knew better than to see patterns where none existed.

But.

The postcards' dates aligned with the sketches. The sketches specified exactly seventeen locks. Seventeen keys had been found—lost, really—in a sequence.

"Find anything interesting?" Derek appeared with coffee in a paper cup.

"A mystery," Margaret said. "Or a hoax. Or senility."

Derek peered at her collection. "Weird. Think it means something?"

"I think," Margaret said slowly, "that someone spent years leaving a trail. Whether anyone followed it—whether there's anything at the end of it—that's the question."

She looked at the sketches again. The door they described would have been somewhere in the old city, in a building that might not exist anymore. The postcards suggested someone traveling, searching, waiting. The keys suggested they'd given up. Left them to be found, piece by piece, by strangers who would never understand.

Or by one meticulous archivist who couldn't help but see the architecture in lost things.

"Can I take these?" she asked Derek. "For the record."

He shrugged. "Sure. It's all going to the dump anyway."

Margaret packed everything carefully into her bag. She probably wouldn't search for the door. She probably wouldn't spend her retirement following a mystery that existed only in her pattern-seeking mind. She probably wouldn't check the old city maps tonight.

Probably.

Outside, the afternoon sun cast long shadows across the parking lot that would replace her life's work. Margaret adjusted her cardigan, felt the weight of found things in her bag, and smiled despite herself.

After all, she'd always been good at finding what was lost.
