The building on Calloway Street had five days left to live, and Jaylen Ortiz was the one who put a crowbar through its heart.

He hadn't meant to. Doreen Pike, facilities manager for thirty-one years, had told him to pull the old corkboard off the basement wall so the salvage crew could take the frame. The corkboard came away. So did the drywall behind it, crumbling like stale cake, and behind that was a steel door no taller than his shoulder.

"That's not on any plan," Doreen said. She had every plan memorized. She had memorized the building the way some people memorize a spouse.

The door opened onto a narrow room, cold even with the power long dead to that wing. Steel cabinets lined the walls. Jaylen slid out a drawer and found rows of paper envelopes, each labeled in fountain pen: *Cherokee Purple tomato. Donated by Mrs. Ruth Kessler, 14 Linden Ave. 1974.*

"Get Teodor," Doreen said.

---

Teodor Lisowski, the city archivist, arrived wheezing from four flights of stairs and stood in the doorway a long time without speaking. Then he found the ledger on the bottom shelf: *Municipal Heritage Seed Library, Est. 1973.* Page after page of names and addresses, the seeds people had carried from Sicily and Oaxaca and Mississippi, handed over to a city that had promised to keep them.

"Linden Avenue," Teodor said softly. "That's under the interstate now. Half these streets are gone."

Marcus Bell, the night guard, came down at the end of his shift because he'd heard voices. He took the ledger from Teodor without asking, ran his finger down a page, and stopped.

"Hattie Bell," he read. "Okra. Clinton Street." He laughed, a small broken sound. "That's my grandmother. She used to say the city stole her garden when they built the projects. Guess she gave some of it away first."

---

On Tuesday, Teodor made the call he was required to make. He came back gray-faced.

"Everything in the building is city property. Anything not on the salvage inventory goes with the demolition. I asked them to amend the inventory." He took off his glasses. "They said Friday is Friday."

"So that's it," Jaylen said.

Nobody answered. Doreen stared at the wall she had painted three times. Marcus turned an envelope over in his hands.

"I've spent my whole life putting things where they belong," Teodor said finally. "Catalogued. Accessioned. Protected. And the rules I follow say these seeds belong in a landfill." He put his glasses back on. "I find I don't care for the rules this week."

"It's theft," Marcus said. "I'm the guard. I'd be guarding the door you walk out of."

"Then don't look," Doreen said.

He looked at her for a long time. "I've been looking at this building for nineteen years. Maybe I've earned a blind spot."

Jaylen spoke last. "Judge said I don't respect public property. Said I only know how to wreck things." He pulled a drawer open, gently this time. "I'm in."

---

They worked in the evenings. Teodor brought archival boxes. Doreen brought lunch coolers, dozens of them, from a church basement she'd once fixed the boiler for. Marcus logged each night as *quiet*.

Not every hour was hurried. On Wednesday they sat on overturned buckets while Teodor read names aloud, and someone would say *I knew a Delgado on Fourth Street* or *my mother had those beans*, and the cold room filled with people who had been gone for fifty years. Jaylen found a packet of sunflowers from a girl who'd been nine in 1974, her handwriting enormous and crooked. He held it for a while before packing it away.

"Why'd you keep this place running so long?" he asked Doreen. "Everybody says it's ugly."

"It is ugly." She patted the concrete. "But it held. That's worth something. Holding."

---

On Friday morning, the last cooler went out in the trunk of Doreen's Buick while Marcus stood at the loading dock studying a crack in the ceiling. At noon, the four of them stood across the street and watched the first swing of the ball. Concrete gave way in great gray slabs. Doreen didn't cry. She just nodded, once, like a woman saying goodbye at a station.

---

In April, behind a library on Clinton Street, a plot of borrowed ground was turned over by a facilities manager, an archivist, a security guard, and a boy whose community service hours had ended in January.

Teodor had tracked down eleven families from the ledger. Some came. Marcus knelt in the dirt and pressed his grandmother's okra into the soil, one seed at a time.

Jaylen planted the sunflowers last. On the little wooden marker, he wrote the girl's name, and the year, and beneath it, in his own careful hand:

*Kept.*
