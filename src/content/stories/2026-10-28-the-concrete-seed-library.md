Agnes Wieczorek had spent forty years telling undergraduates that a seed is a promise. She had not expected, at seventy-three, to discover that promises could be forged.

The Eastbank Municipal Seed Library occupied the second level of a parking garage that had outlived the factory it once served. The city had donated the space because no one else wanted it, which Agnes considered the highest form of civic generosity. She had hung grow lights where the sodium lamps used to buzz, lined the concrete pillars with card catalogs rescued from a closed branch library, and painted over the faded sign that read COMPACT CARS ONLY with SMALL THINGS GROW HERE.

On a Tuesday in April, she opened the drawer marked CHEROKEE PURPLE and tipped a packet into her palm.

The seeds were wrong. Too uniform, too clean, faintly pink with fungicide coating. Heirloom tomato seeds look like tiny, hairy lentils. These looked like they had been manufactured by someone who had only heard tomatoes described.

She checked twelve more drawers. Seven were counterfeit.

"Well," she said to the empty garage. "Somebody's been gardening."

---

Dana Reyes found out the way everyone eventually would: by planting.

She had three kids, a night shift at the distribution center, and a backyard the size of a parking space, which felt appropriate. For two summers, the seed library had kept her family in tomatoes, beans, and a variety of squash called Lakota that her youngest insisted on naming individually. Last year's harvest had been the difference between groceries and choosing which bill to skip.

This spring, her Moon and Stars watermelon had come up as something that was very clearly not Moon and Stars. Its leaves were plain. Its vines were sulky.

She brought one down to P2 in a yogurt container.

"I'm not trying to complain," Dana said, setting it on Agnes's desk. "But I think my watermelon is lying to me."

Agnes looked at the seedling for a long moment. "It's not the watermelon's fault," she said. "It's been misinformed."

---

Ray Okonkwo had been hired to seal the cracks on P3, the lowest level, where groundwater seeped in every spring like it was paying rent. The city was considering flooding the bottom floor permanently and capping it. Ray's job was to make sure that if they did, the water stayed where it was told.

On his third morning, he followed the hum of something electrical to a forgotten maintenance room. Inside, wedged behind a rusted breaker panel, was a dorm-sized refrigerator plugged into a socket that should not have worked. Inside the refrigerator were mason jars, dozens of them, each labeled in careful block capitals: CHEROKEE PURPLE. MOON AND STARS. LAKOTA.

Ray was not a gardener. But he knew a hiding place when he saw one, and he knew that nobody hides things they don't love.

He took one jar upstairs.

---

They sat at Agnes's desk, the three of them, studying the jar the way detectives in old films study a cigarette butt.

"Whoever it is has keys," Agnes said. "And knows exactly which varieties are rare. And has terrible handwriting for a criminal. Far too legible."

"Who comes down here at night?" Ray asked.

Dana answered before Agnes could. "Walter."

Walter Pruitt had been the garage custodian for twenty-two years, back when there were cars to clean up after. The city had kept him on mostly by forgetting to fire him. He swept P2 every evening, nodded at Agnes, and never checked anything out.

They found him at nine that night, refilling the jars.

He didn't run. He just sat down heavily on an overturned bucket.

"There was a memo," he said. "Last fall. They're flooding P3, maybe P2 after. Thirty days' notice, it said. I figured you knew and weren't saying, so folks wouldn't panic. I figured somebody ought to save the good ones." He gestured at the refrigerator. "I swapped them out slow so the drawers wouldn't look empty. Didn't think anybody'd plant the store ones before I moved everything to my sister's basement."

"Walter," Agnes said gently, "that memo was for P3. Only P3. They tested the water table. P2 is staying."

Walter stared at her. Then at the jars. "Oh," he said. "Well. That's embarrassing."

"It's devoted," Agnes corrected. "Which is often the same thing."

---

They spent the following Saturday re-sorting the collection, all four of them, plus Dana's kids, who were assigned to labeling and took it with the gravity of surgeons. Ray installed a proper outlet for the refrigerator, which Agnes decided to keep as a backup vault. Walter was appointed Assistant Curator, an unpaid title he accepted with visible pride.

Agnes posted a notice by the entrance explaining everything: the counterfeits, the jars, the misunderstanding. Below it she added a line in her own hand:

*If your plant came up wrong, bring it back. We'll make it right.*

By June, Dana's second planting of Moon and Stars had sprawled across her yard, each dark rind speckled with small yellow suns. She brought the first melon down to P2 and split it on Agnes's desk, five ways, beneath the grow lights, two floors beneath a city that had forgotten them entirely.

It was, everyone agreed, extremely honest fruit.
