When the rains stopped coming to Millerton, the city council did what bureaucrats do best: they formed a committee. The Committee for Water Resource Assessment dispatched Vera Kowalski and her team into the old cisterns with clipboards, headlamps, and a vague mandate to "assess the situation."

"There's no situation to assess," Vera told her supervisor over a crackling radio on their first descent. "It's empty. The whole thing."

"Check anyway," came the reply.

So they checked.

That's when Marcus, the youngest of the crew, found the first map.

It was drawn in blue chalk on the eastern wall of Chamber 7, which according to their official schematics didn't connect to anything. The chalk map suggested otherwise. It showed Chamber 7 linking to three additional rooms, labeled in neat block letters: 7A, 7B, 7C.

"Should be nothing past this wall," Vera said, consulting her tablet.

Marcus knocked on the bricks. The sound echoed hollowly.

They found the gap behind a loose panel—a narrow passage leading exactly where the chalk map promised. All three chambers were there, dry as bone, empty except for more blue chalk marks indicating dimensions, structural notes, and arrows pointing deeper.

"Someone's been down here," Marcus said, which Vera felt was stating the obvious.

Over the next two weeks, they found seventeen unmapped chambers. Every one had been marked first in chalk, as if someone were leaving breadcrumbs for them to follow. The handwriting never varied—precise, almost architectural in its confidence.

"We should report this," said Diane, their structural engineer, after they discovered a vaulted cathedral-space that shouldn't exist beneath the Commerce District. "Someone's been trespassing in municipal infrastructure."

"Someone who knows more about our infrastructure than we do," Marcus pointed out.

The maps became more detailed as they ventured deeper. Measurements appeared in margins. Notes about load-bearing walls. Warnings about unstable floors, which proved accurate when Marcus nearly stepped through rotted boards in Chamber 23. The chalk had warned him: "WATCH YOUR STEP HERE."

"It's like they're teaching us," he said, steadying himself against Vera.

"Or leading us," Diane muttered.

Then they found the impossible map.

It appeared overnight in Chamber 31, a crossroads where four passages met. This map showed their route back to the surface—accurate in every detail. But it also showed a fifth passage heading northwest, terminating in a large chamber labeled "RESERVOIR—NOT YET."

They stood before the solid wall where the passage should be.

"'Not yet' what?" Marcus asked.

Vera ran her hand along the bricks. Different color. Different texture. "This section is newer. Maybe forty years old?"

"The rest is pre-war construction," Diane confirmed. "1890s."

"So someone walled this off," Marcus said. "And our chalk friend knows about it?"

Vera studied the map. In the corner, new handwriting had appeared: "YOU'LL NEED THIS WHEN THE CREEK DRIES. SORRY I WON'T BE THERE TO SHOW YOU MYSELF. —R.M. 1983"

"Forty years ago," Marcus whispered.

They called for excavation equipment. The city council, desperate as the drought entered its third year, approved it immediately. It took three days to break through safely, following the chalk map's structural guidance to the letter.

The chamber beyond was vast—larger than anything in the official records. And it was wet.

Not the stagnant puddles they'd found elsewhere, but clear water, ankle-deep across an enormous floor, fed by an underground spring that trickled from cracks in the northern wall. Not enough to save the city alone, but enough to matter. Enough to buy time.

On the far wall, in that same blue chalk, a final message:

"TOLD YOU. WHEN YOU FIX THE PIPES IN CHAMBERS 12 AND 18, THIS WILL FILL PROPERLY. THE SPRING KNOWS WHAT IT'S DOING EVEN WHEN WE DON'T. TRUST THE WATER. —R.M."

Marcus was already pulling up old municipal records on his phone. "Regina Marsh. City engineer, 1978 to 1984. Died in the collapse of—"

"Chamber 18," Vera finished, reading the same article over his shoulder. "Trying to map the unmapped sections."

They stood in silence, water lapping at their boots.

"Should we report this?" Diane asked finally.

Vera looked at the blue chalk, forty years old and still showing them the way forward.

"We report the reservoir," she said. "As for who mapped it?" She picked up a piece of blue chalk someone had left on a dry ledge. "Some knowledge doesn't need explaining. Just following."

She added their names to Regina's wall, and they began the climb toward daylight.
