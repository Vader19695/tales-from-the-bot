The cistern under the old schoolhouse hadn't held water in three years, but Ines still knocked on the wall before she went down. Three knocks, the way her grandmother had done to warn the frogs. There were no frogs now. There was a ladder, a string of battery lights, and forty-one ammunition boxes stacked on pallets where the waterline used to be.

"You knock," said the new volunteer, Devika, watching from the hatch.

"Everyone knocks."

Devika knocked.

Below, the air was ten degrees cooler and smelled of limestone and beeswax. Ines checked the hair hygrometer nailed to the post: thirty-eight percent. Good. She wrote it in the ledger, under Paolo's reading from Tuesday and Rafa's from Saturday, a column of numbers in six different hands going back to the first dry spring.

The boxes held the archive. Seed notes: which beans the Ochoa family had grown through the '89 drought, how deep to plant the blue corn, when to soak sorghum. Medical recipes: willow bark, oral rehydration in exact spoonfuls of salt and sugar, how to clean a wound when the clinic in town had closed. How to rebuild a check dam. How to read clouds. All of it copied by hand onto rag paper, because rag paper lasts, and sealed in boxes with packets of silica gel that the volunteers dried in their ovens every month.

The rule of the shift was simple. Check the humidity. Rotate one box. Read one page aloud.

Ines opened box seventeen and handed Devika a sheet. "Out loud."

Devika read, haltingly: "*Tepary beans. Plant after first real rain, not first rain. First rain lies.*" She looked up. "Who wrote this?"

"Señora Beltrán. She died last winter."

Devika set the page down carefully. Then she said what Ines had known she would say since the meeting on Sunday, when she'd shown everyone her phone and her portable scanner and the word *cloud*.

"If this room floods—"

"It's a cistern. We'd celebrate."

"If it burns. If the roof caves. If somebody comes looking for anything worth selling and doesn't know what this is. Everything Señora Beltrán knew is in one hole in the ground." Devika's voice stayed level. "I can scan a box a day. It'd be on servers in three countries."

Ines rotated box seventeen to the bottom of the stack and box eighteen to the top. Her hands knew the weight.

"Servers in three countries," she said, "owned by people who've never planted tepary beans. Who could close the account. Who could change the price. When the power fails here—and it fails, mija, every August—what does a server do for a child with dysentery?"

"Nothing. The paper does that. I'm not asking to replace the paper."

"You're asking to make it not matter."

That was the true thing, and it sat between them in the cool dark. Ines had been coming down this ladder for three years. When people had asked her, in the worst months, what the point was, she had pointed at the hatch. *There. That's the point.* A place you could go. A ledger you could sign. If the knowledge lived everywhere, it lived nowhere you could put your hand on.

Devika picked up the hygrometer log and turned the pages back, back, to the first entry.

"Who started this?"

"Many families. Each one brought what they had. Recipes in kitchen drawers, notes in Bibles. We copied them." Ines heard herself and stopped.

"So it was spread out before," Devika said gently. "That's how it survived long enough to get here."

Ines sat down on box nine. Above them, a truck passed, and dust sifted through the hatch light like flour.

"The cloud is just another kitchen drawer," Devika said. "A big, strange one. I'd want more drawers too. Hand copies. One at the church, one with the Ochoas, one in my sister's house in the city."

"Copying by hand takes months."

"We have shifts."

Ines looked at the column of numbers, six hands, three years. She thought of how many people would have to touch each page. How many would read it aloud.

"Paper first," she said finally. "Every box copied by hand before anything is scanned. And the scans get printed and sent to the drawers, too. Nothing lives in only one kind of place."

"Agreed."

"And whoever scans reads one page aloud. Every time."

Devika smiled. "Agreed."

They climbed out into the white afternoon. The sky was hard and empty. Devika closed the hatch, then paused and knocked three times on the iron lid, for the frogs, for whatever was coming. Ines wrote the date in the ledger and handed her the pen.
