The first time Maya noticed the tomatoes, she thought she was having a stroke.

She'd been a municipal water department inspector for eleven years, and in that time she'd seen rats the size of house cats, a surprisingly intact 1987 Buick, and once, memorably, a wedding dress still on its hanger. But never tomatoes. Especially not thriving, red, suspiciously well-watered tomatoes growing in terra cotta pots on top of Cistern Seven.

Cistern Seven sat beneath the Riverside district, holding 2.3 million gallons of the city's drinking water. Its concrete roof was accessible only through a locked maintenance hatch and a rusted ladder that required a tetanus shot just to look at. Maya had the only key on her ring.

Or so she'd thought.

The tomatoes were joined by basil the next week. Then zucchini. Then—and this was when Maya started keeping a list—three types of lettuce, strawberries, beans climbing up makeshift trellises, and what appeared to be an experimental pepper situation.

She should have reported it immediately. Protocol demanded it. Instead, she found herself visiting Cistern Seven every Thursday, ostensibly to check the water quality monitors, actually to see what had sprouted.

The garden expanded with patient precision. Whoever tended it understood weight distribution, drainage, sun exposure. They'd installed a rain collection system using salvaged gutters. The whole operation demonstrated more competence than the city's actual parks department.

Maya started arriving earlier. She documented everything with her phone, telling herself it was evidence. She never saw the gardener.

Until week seven, when she found the old woman sitting cross-legged between the tomatoes, deadheading marigolds.

"You're early," the woman said, not looking up.

Maya's hand moved toward her radio. "This is city property. You're trespassing."

"So are you, technically. Your jurisdiction is water quality, not rooftop management." The woman smiled, weathered face crinkling. "I checked."

"How did you—"

"Get up here? Same way you did. Lock picking isn't that hard when you've got time and arthritis medication." She gestured at the garden. "Sit. You've been coming for weeks. Might as well talk."

Maya didn't sit. "I have to report this."

"Of course you do." The woman—her name was Ruth, it turned out—returned to her deadheading. "Did you read the proposal? For the Riverside redevelopment?"

Maya hadn't. Ruth produced a battered folder from behind the zucchini.

The plan was neat, inevitable: demolish Cistern Seven, build a new facility outside city limits, rezone Riverside for luxury condos. The neighborhood above—rent-controlled apartments housing two hundred families—would be cleared. All perfectly legal. All completely devastating.

"They announce it next month," Ruth said. "This garden won't stop it. I know that. But my granddaughter lives in 4C. She has a window box with herbs. When they demolish this, her building goes too." She touched a tomato plant gently. "I wanted something beautiful on top of the thing that's going to destroy her home. Seemed fair."

Maya looked at the garden—vibrant, illegal, doomed. "This won't change anything."

"No," Ruth agreed. "But you might."

"Me?"

"Water department has final say on decommissioning. You could find problems. Contamination concerns. Structural issues requiring years of study." Ruth's smile was sharp. "I've been reading the municipal code. It's very dense. Lots of room for... interpretation."

Maya thought about her job. Her pension. The clear bright line between right and wrong she'd relied on for eleven years.

She looked at the strawberries, already flowering.

"I'd need proof," she said slowly. "Regular documentation of this unauthorized garden. Evidence of water waste, since someone's been using the maintenance tap." She pulled out her phone. "Would require weekly visits. Maybe more."

Ruth nodded solemnly. "Very thorough."

"And if someone were to, say, expand this operation—add more plants, really make it a problem—that would require extensive investigation."

"Understood."

Maya pocketed her phone. "I take my coffee black. If you're going to be here when I visit."

"There's a drip system by the peppers," Ruth said. "Figured you might stay awhile eventually."

Maya finally sat, dirt smudging her uniform pants. Below them, 2.3 million gallons of water sat in darkness. Above them, two hundred families slept in homes they didn't know they were losing.

And between—a garden, growing.
