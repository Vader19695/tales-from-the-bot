The alarm was not loud. Ilse had always thought it would be. It was a single amber light above the vault door and a soft repeating tone, like a microwave finished with its work.

She read the panel twice. Primary compressor: offline. Backup generator: fault. Vault temperature: −14.2°C and climbing.

The seed bank occupied four hundred meters of a railway tunnel abandoned in 1961. The rock kept the outer galleries at a steady eleven degrees, which had seemed like an advantage when the foundation bought it. Now it meant the vault had somewhere to go.

By the time the volunteers arrived, she had the numbers. The regional facility in Hallden could take them, but the river road was closing at four for flood control, and the refrigerated van had to be on it by then. Six hours. The portable coolers, packed with dry ice, held perhaps nine hundred accessions. The vault held twenty-two thousand.

"So we take the important ones," said Priya, a graduate student who came on Saturdays to update the database.

"They're all important," Ilse said. "That's the problem with the word."

Tomas, who had farmed the valley for fifty years and now volunteered because, he said, his knees preferred shelves to furrows, pulled on the insulated gloves without a word.

Ilse stood at the terminal and filtered. The first cut was easy, or easy in the way that amputation is easy once you have decided to do it. Anything duplicated at Svalbard or another major bank stayed. That removed eighteen thousand accessions in one keystroke. The commercial wheat, the standard barley lines, the tomato cultivars every seed catalogue carried. They would be lost here and survive elsewhere.

Four thousand left. Nine hundred slots.

"Wheat feeds people," Priya said, reading over her shoulder. "Shouldn't we prioritize staples? Even the local landraces?"

"Some of them," Ilse said. "But think about what we're actually saving. Not calories. Options." She pulled up a wild emmer relative, collected on a limestone slope that had since become a quarry. "This doesn't feed anyone. It carries drought tolerance nobody has bred out yet. If it's gone, it's gone from the earth."

Priya nodded slowly, then went to the cold room door with the first list.

They worked in shifts, ten minutes inside, ten minutes out, because the vault was still cold enough to numb fingers. Ilse handled every packet herself before it went into a cooler. Trilaminate foil, heat-sealed, labeled with accession number, collector, date, site. She checked the seals. Two of the oldest packets from the 1990s had lost their vacuum. She set them aside. A compromised seal meant moisture, and moisture meant the seeds inside had likely aged ten years in two. A slot was a slot.

At hour three, the vault read −6°C. Tomas came out holding a packet as though it might bruise.

"Phaseolus," he said. "The speckled runner. That's mine. My mother's, before that."

Ilse checked the ledger. Single accession. No duplicates. Germination test from 2019: eighty-one percent.

"It's on the list," she said.

He didn't thank her. He just put it in the cooler himself and closed the lid carefully.

What she didn't tell him was what she'd taken off the list to make room. A winter squash, accession 4471, collected from a woman named Agnes Ruud in 2003, who had grown it on the same hillside for sixty years and died the following spring. Viability in 2018 was thirty-four percent. Thirty-four percent of eleven seeds. Possibly three would grow. Possibly none. The speckled runner had a better chance of being a living thing in twenty years.

That was the arithmetic. Ilse believed in the arithmetic. She also knew that arithmetic was only a way of choosing which grief to carry.

At hour five, the coolers were sealed and logged. Priya and Tomas loaded them into the van, and the driver checked the dry ice and signed the manifest. Ilse watched the taillights go down the access road toward the river, then turned back into the tunnel.

Priya lingered. "You don't have to stay."

"Someone should record the curve," Ilse said. "When this is reviewed, they'll want to know how fast it warmed. What we lost, and when. Otherwise the next archivist learns nothing."

After Priya left, Ilse sat at the terminal in her coat. The vault passed zero at 5:40. Somewhere in the stacks, frost was turning to water on twenty-one thousand foil packets, most of them safe in other countries, some of them safe nowhere.

She opened accession 4471 and, in the status field, typed *Not evacuated. Presumed lost.* Then, after a moment, she added the collector's name in full, and the hillside, and the year, so that if anyone ever went looking, they would at least know what had been there.
