The cistern spoke in echoes. Every drip, every footfall, every scratch of pen on parchment bounced off limestone walls and returned transformed. Mira had learned its language over thirty-seven years, knew which sounds meant honest work and which meant trouble.

Today, she heard trouble.

She stood at her desk in the antechamber, oil lamp casting shadows across the ledgers she'd maintained since she was seventeen. The newest clerk, Yasmin, had left her station early—unusual, but not unprecedented. What made Mira's pulse quicken was the open drawer, the scattered papers, and the volume marked "Distribution Records: Northside Flow."

Mira picked up the ledger. Her fingers knew its weight, its texture. But her eyes found numbers they'd never truly seen before.

She carried it to her workbench and pulled out the corresponding infrastructure maps. The cistern amplified her breathing as she traced the waterways with one finger. According to her records, the Northside tunnels—serving the poorest quarter—received forty thousand gallons daily. But the engineering maps showed pipes half that capacity.

Her hands began to shake.

She grabbed another ledger. Southside Gardens, where the merchants lived. Records showed thirty thousand gallons. The pipes could carry eighty.

Someone had been lying. For how long? She flipped backward through years of entries, her own neat handwriting documenting fiction. The cistern groaned around her, water moving through channels she'd helped maintain, serving people she'd never thought to count.

Mira descended the spiral stairs to the main chamber, her footsteps ringing like bells. The vast reservoir stretched before her, supported by dozens of stone columns that disappeared into darkness above and below. She walked the perimeter, checking the gauge markers she'd read ten thousand times.

Everything matched her records. Of course it did. She'd been meticulous.

But the outflow valves—she'd never questioned them. The master controller adjusted distribution each day before she arrived. She'd simply recorded the results, faithful as sunrise.

"You're here late."

Mira spun. Master Controller Daven stood in the archway, his silver badge catching lamplight. He'd hired her. Trained her. Brought her sweet tea on her daughter's wedding day.

"I found irregularities," she said. The cistern caught her words and threw them back: *irregularities, irregularities.*

"Irregularities." Daven descended the steps slowly. "Those records are complex, Mira. Easy to misread."

"The pipes don't lie." Her voice surprised her with its steadiness.

"No. They don't." He reached the bottom step. "But reports can be... simplified. The poor neighborhoods have illegal taps, siphoning water we can't track. We compensate by restricting official flow. It balances out."

"Does it?" Mira thought of the Northside, where her sister had raised six children in two rooms. Where women walked miles to the public fountains. "Show me the balance."

"Mira." His voice softened. "You have a pension coming. Grandchildren who visit you here. Why risk—"

"Show me."

The cistern held its breath.

Daven's jaw tightened. "You think the Governor will care about your moral crisis? The merchants pay taxes. The Northside pays nothing. This is how cities survive."

"This is theft." The word echoed: *theft, theft, theft.*

"This is your job." Daven stepped closer. "Which you'll lose. Along with everything you've earned. Who'll believe an old cistern keeper over the city's water authority?"

Mira looked up at the columns, the arches, the perfect engineering that brought life to a desert. The cistern had taught her that water flows where it's directed. That systems persist because people maintain them.

She'd been maintaining the wrong system.

"The engineers will believe me," she said. "The ones who built these pipes. And the neighborhood councils. And anyone who can subtract." She moved past Daven toward the stairs. "You're right that I might lose everything. But I've already lost something worse."

"What's that?"

She paused at the archway, looking back at the vast chamber that had been her life's work.

"The ability to unhear this place. Every echo's been telling me the truth. I just wasn't listening."

The cistern's voice followed her up the stairs—her footsteps, her breathing, the sound of one woman choosing to make noise in the dark. By dawn, she'd make sure the whole city heard it.
