Lin spread the plastic tablecloth across her folding table, smoothing the corners with practiced hands. Around her, the night market pulsed with its usual chaos—vendors hawking vegetables, phone accessories, steaming bowls of noodles. She'd chosen this spot deliberately: busy enough to blend in, quiet enough that people could linger without blocking traffic.

From her worn leather satchel, she withdrew the maps.

They looked like ordinary street grids at first glance. But anyone who studied them would notice the peculiar annotations. Red dots marked 24-hour water fountains. Blue circles indicated warm alcoves where heat from restaurant kitchens bled through vents. Green squares showed benches the police didn't patrol after midnight. Small stars designated shops where sympathetic owners left out day-old bread.

Lin had spent thirty years surveying for the city, measuring property lines and plotting drainage systems. In retirement, she'd discovered what all those official maps had missed: the invisible infrastructure that kept the city's most vulnerable alive.

She sold the maps for whatever people could pay. Sometimes nothing. Sometimes a few coins. Once, a woman had traded her a pair of warm socks.

Tonight, the cold bit harder than usual. November in the city meant frost warnings and shelter lotteries. Lin watched her breath cloud in the fluorescent glow of string lights overhead.

"How much?"

The girl couldn't have been more than sixteen. Sharp cheekbones, eyes too old for her face. A backpack that bulged with everything she owned.

"Depends," Lin said. "Which neighborhood?"

"Downtown. Near the bus station."

Lin pulled out the downtown map, the most detailed in her collection. She'd walked those streets weekly, noting every change. The girl's eyes widened as she studied it.

"What's this?" She pointed to a purple triangle.

"Library bathroom. Individual stalls with locks. Security guard looks the other way if you're quiet. Good place to clean up."

The girl's throat worked as she swallowed. "And this?"

"Saint Catherine's. They put out coffee and donuts at six a.m., no questions asked."

"How much?" the girl asked again, her voice barely audible.

"How much do you have?"

The girl's face closed off. "Never mind." She turned to leave.

"Wait." Lin pulled out a pencil. "The annotations are two weeks old. This restaurant"—she marked an X—"just changed owners. No more food left out. But this one"—she added a new star—"the baker now leaves bread in a box by the back door. Tuesday and Friday mornings."

She rolled up the map and held it out. "Free updates for life if you check back weekly."

The girl stared at her. "Why?"

Lin thought of all the official maps she'd drawn, how they'd shown everything except what mattered. Property boundaries but not where people slept. Infrastructure but not which taps still ran. She'd spent her career making the city legible to those who owned it. Now she was mapping it for those who simply needed to survive it.

"Someone should know," Lin said simply.

The girl took the map with trembling fingers. "My name's Ava."

"Lin." She pulled out a second sheet—blank except for a grid. "Winter's coming. I need help keeping these current. You notice things other people don't. That alley that stays warm? The security guard who's kind? You tell me, I'll add it. These maps only work if they're true."

Ava's eyes shone. "Like a job?"

"Like a collaboration." Lin hesitated, then made a decision she'd been avoiding for months. "I'm setting up a website. Making these maps available to more people. I could use someone who knows what information actually matters."

She'd stayed hidden long enough. Her quiet life felt hollow compared to this: sitting in a night market, watching a girl study a map like it was a treasure, knowing that maybe—just maybe—the right information delivered to the right person at the right time could mean everything.

"I'll come back Friday," Ava said.

"I'll be here," Lin promised.

Around them, the market hummed with a thousand transactions. But Lin would remember this one—the night her maps stopped being a secret and became what they were always meant to be: a guide home, drawn by those who knew the territory of survival by heart.
