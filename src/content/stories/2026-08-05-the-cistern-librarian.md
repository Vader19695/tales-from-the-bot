The cistern smelled like minerals and time. Maria descended the iron ladder twenty meters into the earth, her headlamp cutting through darkness that had existed since before the city above ran dry. The water department sent her down every three months to check for structural integrity, though "structural integrity" was optimistic language for walls built in 1847.

She found the first case wedged behind a support column on her second inspection round.

It was a pelican case, the kind photographers used, sealed with desiccant packs and latched against the damp. Inside: a notebook filled with handwriting so precise it looked typeset. *The Left Hand of Darkness* by Ursula K. Le Guin, copied word for word, page after page, in blue ballpoint pen.

Maria sat on the limestone ledge, her boots dangling over what used to be eight million gallons of water. She turned pages, careful with her work gloves. Whoever had written this had noted the original publication date, ISBN, and even printer specifications. The handwriting never wavered, never cramped. It was the work of someone who understood that copying was its own form of reading.

She found eighteen more cases over the next hour. They were arranged chronologically along the eastern wall, each labeled with a number and date. *Parable of the Sower*. *The Dispossessed*. *A Wizard of Earthsea*. All science fiction. All out of print, or trapped behind defunct DRM, or published by houses that had folded when the digital markets collapsed.

Maria should have reported it. The cistern was city property, and whoever was accessing it had either stolen keys or picked the locks. Instead, she photographed the labeling system and started checking the access logs.

---

Three weeks later, she waited by the Hoffman Street entrance at 4 AM with two thermoses of coffee and a copy of *The Lathe of Heaven* that she'd paid sixty dollars for at an estate sale.

The woman who emerged from the pre-dawn darkness was seventy if she was a day, wearing a backpack that looked like it weighed twenty pounds. She stopped when she saw Maria, neither surprised nor afraid.

"I figured someone would notice eventually," the woman said. "I've been getting sloppy with the timeline."

"Eleanor Vance?" Maria checked the name against the key-card access log. "Retired civil engineer."

"Guilty. You going to arrest me?"

"I'm going to drink coffee and ask what the hell you're doing."

Eleanor accepted a thermos. They sat on the concrete barrier, watching delivery trucks navigate the empty streets. Above them, the city was rationing water to four hours a day. Below them, the empty cistern held something else now.

"I started when the first big server farms went down," Eleanor said. "2031. Everyone said the cloud was forever, but forever ended on a Tuesday. I had three hundred books on my reader, and suddenly they were just gone—licensing errors, they said. Like the books had never existed."

"So you started copying them."

"By hand. It seemed important." Eleanor's fingers were callused on the right side, where the pen rested. "Physical books cost what, thirty-forty dollars now? And half of them are print-on-demand garbage that falls apart. But pen and paper—that's two dollars and time."

"It's also insane," Maria said, without heat.

"Probably." Eleanor smiled into her coffee. "But the cistern's climate-controlled, structurally sound, and nobody goes down there but you. Better than my apartment, which floods every spring."

Maria thought about the scratch of pen on paper, hour after hour, words transferred by hand in the darkness. The weight of all that water that used to press down, replaced now by the weight of preservation. The smell of stone that had held the city's water, now holding its memory.

"I brought you something," Maria said, and handed over *The Lathe of Heaven*.

Eleanor turned it over, checked the copyright page, nodded. "Three months for this one, probably. Maybe four."

"The west wall is drier," Maria said. "Better for long-term storage. And I'll stop logging your access if you let me read them sometimes."

Eleanor considered. Above them, the city was waking up to rationing and heat. Below, the archive waited.

"Deal," Eleanor said. "But you bring the coffee."

"Deal."

They shook hands, and Maria went home to sleep before her shift. That evening, she bought twelve blue pens and a box of archival notebooks. The work, she was learning, had a way of multiplying.
