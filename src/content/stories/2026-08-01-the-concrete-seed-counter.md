The call came at 2:47 AM. Maria pulled her phone from the nightstand, still half-dreaming of the grant proposal she'd been writing.

"You need to come see this," said Derek, the night security guard. His voice carried a tremor she'd never heard before.

The municipal seed bank occupied three levels of what had been Parking Structure D before the floods made the upper city untenable. Maria arrived twenty minutes later, descending the ramp past painted lines that once guided cars, now guiding humidity-controlled air through a repository of ten thousand seed varieties.

Derek met her at the vault entrance. "Drawer 47-C. The Haskell County corn."

In the sterile examination room, the opened drawer sat under bright LED lights. Inside the vacuum-sealed glass envelope, kernels that had remained dormant for sixty-three years showed pale green shoots pressing against their container.

"The seal's intact," Maria said, checking the monitoring strip. "No breach, no moisture intrusion. Temperature steady at negative eighteen Celsius."

"I noticed it during rounds," Derek said. "Thought the light was playing tricks."

By morning, two more people crowded the small room. Dr. Yao, seventy-four, had established the seed bank's original protocols. She examined the sprouted corn through magnifying glasses, her weathered hands steady.

"Impossible," she said, for the third time.

Priya, the intern, pulled up digital records on her tablet. "Last viability test was eight years ago. Eighty-seven percent germination rate under controlled conditions. But those conditions include thawing, moisture, and specific light exposure. None of which happened here."

"Check the others," Maria said.

They worked systematically through the archive. By noon, they'd found three more cases: a variety of drought-resistant sorghum from Mali, heritage tomatoes from a defunct monastery garden in New Mexico, and curiously, a collection of wild flax seeds gathered from a highway median in 2019.

"There's no pattern," Priya said, staring at the map she'd plotted. "Different climates, different families, different acquisition dates."

"There's always a pattern," Dr. Yao said quietly. "We're just not seeing it yet."

Derek made coffee while they worked. He'd taken this job after his landscaping business failed, finding unexpected peace in the underground silence. Now he watched these women treat sprouting seeds like a crime scene, and understood why people devoted lives to preservation.

"What if it's not about the seeds themselves?" he said. "What if it's about why they're here?"

They looked at him.

"I mean, the reason they were saved. The corn—I pulled the documentation. Last variety grown by a woman named Edie Haskell before her family farm was sold. The sorghum came from a village displaced by drought. The monastery closed in the '70s."

Priya scrolled through files. "The flax... collected by a volunteer project before that highway was widened. It was the last natural corridor in the area."

Maria felt something shift in her understanding. "Final specimens."

"Seeds preserved not because they were thriving," Dr. Yao said slowly, "but because they were ending."

They spent the afternoon testing environmental sensors, cross-referencing atmospheric data. The facility's monitoring systems had captured minute electrical field fluctuations—barely measurable variations in the earth's magnetic signature that had intensified over three weeks.

"Ground conductivity changes," Maria said, reading the technical summary. "Likely from shifting water tables due to the drought."

"Could seeds sense that?" Priya asked.

Dr. Yao removed her glasses, cleaned them thoughtfully. "Seeds contain everything needed to detect their environment. They measure moisture, temperature, even day length through their seed coats. Some species sense pressure changes, chemical signals. We don't fully understand the mechanisms."

"So they're responding to something we can barely measure," Derek said.

"Responding to a world in flux," Dr. Yao corrected. "These particular seeds—the last of their kind—might be more sensitive to environmental stress. Evolution's final roll of the dice."

The inspection team arrived the following Tuesday. Maria showed them the sprouted specimens, now carefully isolated and studied. She explained their hypothesis: that seeds preserved from extinct populations might retain heightened sensitivity to environmental signals, a last-chance biological mechanism.

The inspectors noted it in their report as an unusual anomaly requiring further study.

That evening, Maria stood alone in the vault. Ten thousand varieties surrounded her—some common, some rare, all waiting. The sprouted seeds had been carefully re-preserved, their brief green reach now documented, studied, explained.

But she thought about what Derek had said. About endings and why we save things. About seeds that somehow knew, even in frozen darkness, that the world was changing.

She touched the cool metal drawer, a gesture that felt like both promise and apology, then climbed back toward the surface.
