Kenji Mori's office at the Eastside Bus Depot had once been a supply closet, and it had never fully accepted the promotion. A mop bracket still jutted from the wall above his monitor. He hung his umbrella on it.

Every morning he audited passenger complaints. Most of them sorted themselves: *bus late* (true), *driver rude* (sometimes true), *seat sticky* (always true, and best not investigated). He stamped them, coded them, and sent them into a database that, as far as he could tell, existed mainly to prove that complaints had been received.

Then there was Mr. Pruitt.

Mr. Pruitt complained about Route 14, the 6:47 a.m. departure, every Tuesday and Thursday with the regularity of a heartbeat. *Bus waits at Alder Street for an old man who is always late. Ninety seconds minimum. Unacceptable.* Attached, helpfully, was the time the old man tapped his fare card, which allowed Kenji to pull the card's record.

The card belonged to Walter Ostrowski, age seventy-nine.

Walter Ostrowski had died three years ago.

A reasonable man would have coded it *Data Error: Card Record*, a category that existed for exactly this purpose and was, frankly, underused. Kenji looked at the code for a long time. Then he printed the card history, which showed Walter Ostrowski riding the 6:47 every weekday for three years, and took it with him to lunch.

---

He began with the driver, Bev Delgado, who had driven Route 14 for eleven years and regarded Kenji the way one regards a pigeon that has wandered indoors.

"Do you wait at Alder Street?"

"I adhere to the schedule."

"For ninety seconds?"

"The schedule," Bev said, "has a certain flexibility of spirit."

"The card belongs to a dead man."

Bev ate a french fry with great deliberation. "Then I'd say he's doing very well for himself."

---

Kenji, who had not ridden a bus at 6:47 a.m. since university, set his alarm.

The man at Alder Street was not seventy-nine and not dead. He was perhaps eighty-five, in a tweed cap, and he arrived at a gentle shuffle while the bus idled and Mr. Pruitt—identifiable by his audible sighing—checked his watch twice. The old man tapped the card. The reader beeped. Bev said, "Morning, Ernesto," and Ernesto said, "Morning, my dear," and sat in the front seat as though it had been carved for him.

Kenji sat behind a young woman in scrubs. "Excuse me. Do you know that gentleman?"

She looked at Kenji's depot lanyard and her face closed like a shop at five. "Who's asking?"

"Complaints," Kenji admitted.

"Then no."

But at the next stop a teenager with a skateboard got on, fist-bumped Ernesto, and asked if he'd taken his pills. At Fifth Street a man in a suit handed Ernesto a newspaper folded to the crossword. At Marlowe Avenue, Ernesto stood up, confused for a moment, and the woman in scrubs was already beside him, saying, "This one, Ernesto. The bakery's right there," and walking him off the bus and across the street to a shop where someone waved through the window.

She came back. The bus had waited. Of course it had.

---

It took Kenji two weeks, four interviews, and a coffee he paid for himself to assemble the case.

Walter Ostrowski and Ernesto Ruiz had worked together at the Marlowe Avenue bakery for forty years. When Walter died, Ernesto's memory was already loosening, and his family couldn't navigate the depot's reduced-fare application, which was eleven pages long and required a notarized signature from a doctor who no longer took new patients. Walter's widow had simply handed Ernesto her husband's card. Ernesto still went to the bakery every morning. The current owners let him sit by the ovens and fold boxes.

The card was topped up anonymously, in small amounts, from at least nine different kiosks. The teenager made sure he'd taken his pills. The nurse got him off at the right stop. Bev waited ninety seconds. Nobody had organized any of it. Nobody had told anyone.

The system had recorded it all, faithfully, as a dead man riding the bus.

---

Kenji sat in his closet and opened Mr. Pruitt's latest complaint. The cursor blinked beside the dropdown menu. *Data Error. Driver Misconduct. Fare Evasion.*

He thought about the eleven-page form. He thought about Bev's flexibility of spirit.

He selected *Other* and typed into the comment box: *Investigated. Delay attributable to passenger loading. Within acceptable tolerance.* That was, he decided, entirely true.

Then he printed the reduced-fare application, all eleven pages, and began filling in the parts he could.

The next Tuesday, Kenji caught the 6:47 at Alder Street. Mr. Pruitt was already sighing. Ernesto arrived at his usual pace, tapped the card, and the reader beeped, and Bev said good morning.

Kenji stopped at the kiosk on the way to work. He put twenty dollars on Walter Ostrowski's card.

Nine kiosks, he thought. Now ten.
