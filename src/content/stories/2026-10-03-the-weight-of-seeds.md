The seed bank occupied the bottom forty feet of a concrete grain silo outside Kessel Falls, and Marta Engel had learned to love the way it held sound. Paper envelopes whispered. Glass jars clicked against steel shelving and the click circled the walls before it died. On Thursdays she came in at eight, signed the clipboard, and pulled the next drawer in the germination queue.

That morning it was beans. *Albrecht Tongue of Fire*, accession 1987-114, donated by Hilde Albrecht, who had carried the line from a farm near Ulm and grown it in Kessel Falls for fifty-one years. Marta counted twenty seeds onto damp paper towel, rolled them, labeled the roll, and set it in the incubator beside last week's corn.

The corn bothered her.

She had tested *Hollis Red Flint* seven days earlier, and every seedling stood at the same height, the same pale green, the same angle off the towel, like pickets cut from one board. Open-pollinated flint was never like that. It sprawled. It argued with itself. Some sprouts came up purple at the base, some yellow, some slow. Uniformity was the signature of a hybrid, bred from two controlled parents so every child came out identical, and identically unable to breed true.

She went back to the cold room and opened the Hollis jar. The kernels were red, yes, but too even. Under the lamp she found, caught in the crease of the jar's inner bag, a faint dust of pink.

Fungicide coating. Commercial seed.

Marta did not tell anyone that day. She went home, did not sleep, and on Friday came back with a notebook of her own.

The accession logs lived in green clothbound ledgers on the shelf behind Dale Whitcomb's desk. Dale had run the bank for twenty-two years. He had trained her. He brought cinnamon rolls on regrow days and knew the donors' grandchildren by name. Marta waited until he left for the co-op and then took down the ledgers one at a time.

She worked backward. Each regrow cycle, a variety was planted out, harvested, dried, and returned to storage with a new lot number. Each lot had a germination rate, a seed count, a weight per hundred seeds. Weight per hundred was the thing nobody watched. It was the thing she watched now.

*Hollis Red Flint*, lot 4: 31.2 grams. Lot 5: 31.0. Lot 6, entered in 2016: 38.7.

She found it again in *Tongue of Fire*. Again in *Vasquez Blue Squash*, in *Marrow-Fat Pea*, in a sorghum whose donor had died the year Marta joined. Fourteen varieties in all. Every jump fell in the same autumn, the autumn after the dehumidifier failed and the silo sweated for nine days before anyone noticed. The handwriting in those entries was Dale's, careful and slightly too dark, as if he had pressed hard.

She understood it then, and the understanding was worse than malice. The originals had molded. He had lost them, all fourteen, and instead of saying so he had driven to a farm supply store and bought the nearest match. Red corn for red corn. Speckled bean for speckled bean. Then he had kept lying every cycle since, regrowing hybrids whose offspring drifted further from anything Hilde Albrecht would have recognized.

On Monday, Dale found her at the incubator. The bean roll lay open between them. Every sprout looked identical.

"You weighed them," he said.

"You could have told us."

"We were up for the state grant that year." He sat down heavily on the step stool. "If it got out we'd lost founder stock, nobody would trust us with anything again. The donors gave us their whole lives, Marta. I thought a placeholder was better than an empty shelf."

"It's not a placeholder. It's a label on the wrong thing." She heard her voice ring off the concrete. "Somebody plants this thinking it's adapted to our frost dates, our blight. It isn't. And we stopped looking for the real seed because we thought we had it."

He didn't argue. That was how she knew he'd been waiting years for someone to ask.

She wrote the report that night: fourteen varieties, lot numbers, weights, the pink dust photographed beside a ruler. She sent it to the board and the donor families both, so no one could quietly file it. Then she wrote a second letter to every grower who had ever taken seed home from the silo, before 2016, asking one question: *Did you keep any?*

The first reply came in eleven days. A retired schoolteacher in Orrin County mailed a coffee can of beans, mottled and uneven, wrapped in a 1994 church bulletin.

Marta counted twenty onto damp towel. A week later they came up crooked, some fast, some slow, a few streaked red at the stem.

She logged each one by hand.
