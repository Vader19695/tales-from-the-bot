The Municipal Recovered Property Depot shelved items by intake date, then by category, then by bin number. This system had been in place since 1987, and Maren Pike had maintained it for eleven years without incident.

On a Tuesday in March, she arrived at 7:04 a.m. to find it gone.

Aisle 1, which should have held January's umbrellas, now held umbrellas from every month, along with single gloves, phone chargers, and a laminated bus schedule. Aisle 12 held reading glasses beside a dog-eared paperback with a pressed flower inside. Aisle 40, at the far end where the fluorescent tubes buzzed and flickered, held three items on an otherwise empty shelf: a gold wedding band, a crayon drawing of a house with four stick figures, and a half-written letter that began *Dad, I know it's been years, but.*

Maren filed Incident Report 0311-A. Then she called Logistics.

Teodor Vance arrived at nine, carrying coffee and the expression of a man who believed most problems were people. He walked the aisles twice, tapping a pen against his clipboard.

"Night crew," he said.

"There is no night crew. We cut it in 2019."

"Then it's a prank. Someone with a key and too much time."

They pulled the security footage. At 2:16 a.m., Aisle 40 was full of February's luggage. At 2:17 a.m., it held three objects. No figure crossed the frame. The timestamp did not skip.

Teodor watched it four times. "Glitch," he said, with less conviction.

"The footage, or the shelves?"

He didn't answer. Instead, he took a single blue mitten from the morning's intake bin, logged it as Item 44871, and placed it in Aisle 1 himself. "Control sample," he said.

The next morning, the mitten was in Aisle 9, beside a teddy bear missing one eye.

"Okay," Teodor said quietly. "Okay."

---

They spent the following week doing what municipal employees do with the inexplicable: they documented it. Maren built a spreadsheet. Teodor rode the recovery van to Central Station, the Line 4 terminus, and the ferry dock, interviewing the collection staff with a list of questions that grew stranger each day.

*Where was this found? Was the owner seen? Did they look like they were in a hurry?*

At the ferry dock, a cleaner named Gus remembered the wedding ring. "Woman took it off at the railing," he said. "Stood there a long time. Then she put it on the bench and walked away. I figured she'd come back."

At Central Station, a ticket agent remembered the letter. "Older guy. Sat at Gate 6 writing for an hour. Missed three trains. Then he just left it and went home."

Teodor wrote everything down. On the ride back, he stared out the van window at the gray river.

"The low aisles are things people forgot," he said finally. "The high aisles are things people couldn't carry."

Maren nodded. She had reached the same conclusion over the spreadsheet, though she'd been afraid to say it aloud. The depot wasn't broken. It had simply started sorting by a column nobody had thought to include.

---

Policy stated that unclaimed items were auctioned or destroyed after ninety days. The ring had been in the depot for eighty-one.

"We're not detectives," Teodor said, which Maren understood to mean *yes.*

The letter was easiest. It had been written on the back of a pharmacy receipt with a loyalty number. The pharmacy, after a phone call involving the words "municipal" and "urgent," provided an address. Maren mailed the letter in a depot envelope with a note: *Found at Gate 6. We thought you might want to finish it.*

The drawing took longer. The house had a street number in the corner, carefully crayoned: 418. There were eleven 418s in the city. At the seventh, a woman opened the door, saw the drawing, and sat down on her front step.

"My son made this before the divorce," she said. "It was the last one with all four of us."

The ring they never traced. On day eighty-nine, Maren took it down from Aisle 40 and held it under the flickering light.

"Policy says auction," Teodor said.

"Policy says a lot of things."

He was quiet for a while. Then he reached into his jacket and set something on the empty shelf: a man's watch, old, its band cracked.

"My father's," he said. "I left it on a train in 2006. Someone turned it in. I picked it up here and never wore it again." He shrugged. "Seemed like it belonged back here."

The next morning, the watch was gone from Aisle 40. They found it in Aisle 3, among the umbrellas and reading glasses—things that were merely misplaced, and might still be carried.

Teodor put it on.

Maren filed Incident Report 0311-B: *System functioning as intended.*
