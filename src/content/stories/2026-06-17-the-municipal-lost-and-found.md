Margaret had supervised the city's bus depot for thirty-two years, but retirement left her with too many empty hours and a stubborn habit of waking at five a.m. The lost-and-found warehouse felt like a natural place to volunteer—a concrete cavern on the industrial edge of downtown where forgotten things waited in numbered rows.

She worked Tuesdays and Thursdays, logging items into the database while Jerome, the young city employee assigned to the warehouse, processed disposal orders and fielded phone calls from people hoping to reclaim their umbrellas, their phones, their dignity.

The brown coat appeared on a Tuesday in March, pulled from the back of a crosstown bus. It was unremarkable—worn corduroy, missing a button, pockets sagging with lint. Margaret was checking those pockets, as protocol required, when her fingers found the folded papers.

Six hand-drawn maps, each one rendered in careful ballpoint pen on lined notebook paper. Her city, sketched in sections. The park near Westfield Station. The plaza by City Hall. The old carousel at Riverside. Each map bore a symbol—a star, a heart, a tree—and a timestamp: "Saturday, 2:00 p.m." "Saturday, 3:15 p.m." "Saturday, 4:30 p.m."

"Jerome," Margaret called across the warehouse. "Come look at this."

He ambled over, headphones around his neck, perpetually skeptical of anything that disrupted his routine. At twenty-four, he treated the warehouse like purgatory before something better came along.

"Maps," he said, glancing at them. "So?"

"They're connected. See? They form a path through the city. Like instructions."

Jerome shrugged. "Probably some urban exploration thing. Or geocaching."

But Margaret couldn't let it go. That night, she spread the maps across her kitchen table, tracing the route with her finger. The attention to detail bothered her in the best way—the careful notation of landmarks, the precise times. Someone had planned this with love.

By Thursday, she'd convinced Jerome to take their lunch break on the road. "One hour," he'd said. "I'm not getting in trouble for this."

They started at Westfield Station, where the first map's star marked a bench near the fountain. Margaret stood there, turning slowly, trying to see what the mapmaker had seen. Jerome checked his phone, impatient.

"There," Margaret said, pointing to the bench's armrest. Scratched into the paint, almost invisible: a star.

Jerome's eyebrows rose.

They found a heart carved into the base of the City Hall statue, a tree symbol spray-painted faintly on the Riverside carousel's fence. At each location, Jerome's skepticism softened into something like wonder.

The coat had a name sewn into its collar, faded but readable: Dorothy Chen. Jerome ran it through the database. No claims. Margaret tried the phone directory, old-school. Three D. Chens. The third call connected.

"That was my mother's coat," a woman's voice said, tight with emotion. "She passed away in January. I... I didn't know she'd left it somewhere."

Margaret explained about the maps.

There was a long silence. Then, quietly: "She was planning a scavenger hunt. For my daughter, Emma. They'd had a falling out—my fault, mostly. I kept them apart. Mom kept calling, trying to arrange a visit, a day together. She must have been planning this as a surprise."

The woman's voice cracked. "Emma's ten. She doesn't really remember her grandmother now."

"We found all the locations," Margaret said. "Every marker is still there."

Another silence, different this time.

"Could you... would you send me photographs? Of the route? Maybe we could still—"

"Better," Margaret said, glancing at Jerome, who nodded slowly. "We could show you in person."

That Saturday, Margaret and Jerome met Dorothy Chen's daughter and granddaughter at Westfield Station. Emma carried the maps, following her grandmother's careful instructions through the city, discovering each hidden symbol, collecting the wonder someone had tried to leave for her.

At the carousel, the last stop, Emma pressed her small hand against the tree symbol. "She wanted to take me here?"

"She did," her mother said, crying now. "She really did."

Margaret and Jerome stood back, watching three generations—one absent, two present, one possible—connected by ballpoint ink and lost corduroy.

"Not a bad way to spend a Saturday," Jerome said quietly.

Margaret smiled. "No. Not bad at all."
