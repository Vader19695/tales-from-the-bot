The Municipal Dead Letter Annex occupied the basement of City Hall the way a cough occupies a lung: unwelcome, persistent, and impossible to evict. Built in 1974, it had been designed by someone who believed fluorescent light was a form of nutrition. Doris Pell had been eating it for thirty-one years.

Tomorrow she would stop. Tomorrow the keys went to Marcus Tibbs, twenty-four, who labeled his labels and once filed a complaint about the complaint form.

She was clearing out Cabinet Fourteen, the one that stuck, when the bottom drawer came away in her hands and revealed a second bottom beneath it. Inside was a green ledger, the kind nobody had manufactured since Nixon, and on its cover, in the cramped, upright hand she remembered from every sticky note of her first decade: *A. Vole. Private.*

Arthur Vole had run the Annex before her. He was a small, damp-eyed man who hummed show tunes in the wrong key and retired the year she was hired. She had assumed he'd left nothing behind but the smell of pipe tobacco in the sorting table's drawers.

She sat down on the stepladder and opened the ledger.

*March 1962. Letter addressed "To my son, wherever the Army sent you." No return. Sent to: Pvt. R. Okafor, Fort Dix, who wrote to the paper that he had no family. Reply received: "Whoever you are, thank you."*

*June 1966. Letter: "Dear Ellen, I was wrong about everything." Sent to: Ellen Marsh, 18 Cutter St. Not the right Ellen. Possibly a better one. Reply received: she married the man in 1968.*

*October 1970. Letter to "The girl in the yellow coat on the No. 9 bus." Sent to: Lorraine Busby, who owns a yellow coat. Reply: a strongly worded note. Fair.*

Page after page. Hundreds of them. Letters the regulations said must be held ninety days, then destroyed, and which Arthur had instead read, weighed, and readdressed according to nothing but a hunch, a newspaper clipping, or, in one entry, "the way the handwriting leaned."

Doris was, by temperament and training, a woman of rules. She believed the postal service was one of civilization's few unbroken promises: you write a name, and the name receives it. Arthur had broken that promise roughly four hundred times. He had also, by his own tally, prompted two marriages, one reconciliation between brothers, a successful lawsuit, and what he described as "a regrettable incident at a Rotary luncheon."

There was a federal statute against this. She could recite its number. She did, quietly, to the filing cabinets, who were unmoved.

She kept reading because she could not stop, and because the last entry was dated 1993, the year she'd started, and she wanted to know what he'd done with his final weeks.

*September 1993. Letter, unsigned: "You are not as alone as you think. Someone notices you." Intended recipient unknown. Sent to: the new girl, D. Pell, 4B Larch Court, who eats lunch at her desk and apologizes to the photocopier.*

Doris put the ledger down.

She remembered that letter. She had been twenty-nine, newly divorced, living in an apartment whose radiator knocked like a debt collector. She had kept the letter in her coat pocket until the paper went soft as cloth. She had decided, privately, that it was from her late grandmother via some cosmic loophole, and it had been enough to get her through that winter, and then through the next.

It had been a stranger's letter. Arthur had simply guessed she needed it.

The fluorescent tubes buzzed their single note. Somewhere above, the City Council was voting on parking meters.

Here was the problem, laid out on a sorting table like everything else in her life. If she gave Marcus the ledger, one of two things would happen. He would report it, and Arthur's name, he was eighty-nine now, living in a home in Dunmore, would end up in a memo. Or Marcus would not report it, and would start doing it himself, and Marcus could not be trusted to guess what anyone needed. Last week he'd given her a stapler for her birthday.

But then, Arthur had not been trustworthy either. He had sent a stranger's apology to the wrong Ellen. He had triggered a Rotary incident. He had been wrong a great many times and right often enough to make the wrongness feel like a fair price, though Doris noted that he had never asked the people paying it.

And she had been the one he was right about.

At five o'clock, Marcus came down the stairs with a clipboard and a face full of earnest anticipation.

"Handover checklist," he said. "Keys?"

She gave him the ring. Twelve keys, tagged in her handwriting.

"Anything I should know?"

Doris considered him: his neat tie, his terrible stapler judgment, the way he'd once spent forty minutes trying to find the owner of a postcard that just said *Wish you were here.*

"Cabinet Fourteen sticks," she said. "Be patient with the bottom drawer."

He wrote it down. *Cab. 14 — sticks. Patience.*

She picked up her coat, felt the empty pocket where a letter used to live, and climbed the stairs into whatever light the world had left.
