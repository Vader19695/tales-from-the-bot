Margaret Chen had seen worse cracks in her forty years as a civil engineer, but the split running through Mrs. Kowalski's garden bed still made her wince. She ran her finger along the repair work—smooth, properly mixed, reinforced with what looked like salvaged rebar.

"Who fixed this?" Margaret asked, squinting in the morning sun.

Mrs. Kowalski adjusted her glasses. "No idea. Happened overnight. Three weeks ago."

Margaret had been volunteering at the Riverside Municipal Repair Café for six months, mostly fixing toasters and teaching people how to patch drywall. The café operated out of a former DMV office—all fluorescent lights and linoleum—where residents brought broken things instead of throwing them away. Unglamorous, yes. But useful.

Now someone was conducting guerrilla infrastructure repairs.

She found two more fixes that afternoon: a sidewalk panel near the elementary school, and a retaining wall behind the library. All executed with surprising competence. All completely illegal.

At Tuesday's repair café session, Margaret watched her regular volunteers with new suspicion. There was Jamie, the graffiti artist doing community service hours, who'd proven unexpectedly good with tile work. And Reyna, the single mother of two who came in exhausted from her nursing shifts but stayed anyway, learning plumbing with fierce concentration.

Margaret noticed concrete dust under Jamie's fingernails.

"Walk with me," she told them both after closing.

They followed her to Mrs. Kowalski's garden bed without a word. Jamie cracked first.

"Look, nobody was going to fix it. Mrs. K grows vegetables there. She's eighty-three."

"The sidewalk near the school was a tripping hazard," Reyna added quietly. "I reported it four times. A kid broke his arm there."

Margaret examined the work again. "Where'd you learn to mix concrete?"

"YouTube," Jamie said. "And my uncle did construction."

"The rebar?"

"Salvaged from the demolished warehouse on Fifth."

"At night," Margaret said. It wasn't a question.

They both nodded.

Margaret felt the familiar tug between what was proper and what was necessary—a tension she'd navigated her entire career. "The city has protocols. Engineers review structural work. There's liability, permits—"

"There's also a two-year backlog," Reyna interrupted. "And Mrs. Kowalski can't afford private contractors."

That's when the white sedan pulled up. City inspector.

Margaret's stomach dropped as Douglas Tremaine emerged, clipboard in hand. They'd worked together on the flood barriers project fifteen years ago. He'd been insufferable then too.

"Margaret Chen. Still fixing the world?" He gestured at the garden bed. "We've had reports of unauthorized municipal repairs. Fines start at five thousand dollars per violation."

Jamie went pale. Reyna's jaw tightened.

"I did these," Margaret said.

Douglas raised an eyebrow. "You?"

"Semi-retired. Got bored. You know how it is." She met his gaze steadily. "I assume you'll want to inspect the work before issuing citations?"

He crouched, examining the concrete. His expression shifted from smugness to concentration to something resembling respect.

"This is... actually good work."

"I'm a licensed civil engineer with forty years' experience. I know how to pour concrete, Douglas."

He stood, brushing off his knees. "Still unauthorized."

"Then authorize it," Margaret said. "Create a program. Let the repair café handle minor infrastructure fixes. I'll supervise, sign off on everything. The city saves money, residents get safe sidewalks. Unless you'd rather keep watching people get hurt while permits languish?"

Douglas tapped his clipboard. "City Council would never—"

"Try them. You might be surprised what people approve when you frame it as citizen engagement and budget savings." She smiled thinly. "I'll write the proposal myself. Include all the safety protocols you want."

Douglas studied the three of them: a retired engineer, a graffiti artist, a tired nurse. An unlikely infrastructure crew.

"One month," he finally said. "Get me something convincing, or I'm back with fines."

After he left, Reyna exhaled shakily. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet," Margaret said. "Now we have to actually convince the Council. And you two—" she pointed at Jamie and Reyna—"are learning proper load calculations. If we're doing this, we're doing it right."

Jamie grinned. "Does this mean—"

"It means we're going to fix things. Legally. Paperwork and all." Margaret looked at the garden bed, the good concrete work gleaming in the afternoon light. "Someone's got to."
