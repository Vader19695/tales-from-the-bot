For thirty-four years, Margaret Okafor had found the discrepancies other people missed. A transposed digit, a vendor that didn't exist, a petty-cash drawer forever forty dollars light. Retirement, she discovered, did not switch the habit off. It only took away the spreadsheets.

So when she began volunteering at the Linden Street Community Orchard, she brought a clipboard.

The orchard was mostly apple and pear, but along the southern fence ran eleven raised beds of heirloom tomatoes, tended by a rotating cast of neighbors. Nobody had ever written down what was growing there. Margaret found this intolerable.

She started in June with a simple table: bed number, plant label, fruit shape, skin color, flesh color, locule count, average weight. By July she had added columns for leaf type (regular or potato-leaf), stem pubescence, shoulder coloring, and a taste rating she scored from one to five across sweetness, acidity, and what she privately called *depth*. She cut each specimen at the equator and photographed the cross-section against a gray card.

Brandywine: oblate, pink, potato-leaf, six to eight locules, 340 grams, sweetness 4, acidity 3, depth 5. Cherokee Purple: dusky rose with green shoulders, depth 5. Green Zebra: tart, striped, acidity 5, depth 2. She was not sentimental about Green Zebra.

Then she reached Bed Nine.

The label read only *H.R.P.* in faded marker. The plants were regular-leaf, indeterminate, and heavily fruited with something she couldn't place: deeply ribbed, almost pleated, like a paper lantern, with a pale pink skin that blushed to coral at the blossom end. Inside, the flesh was nearly hollow, four thick-walled chambers around a small seed core. Sweetness 3, acidity 4, and a finish of something like cloves. Depth 5.

She spent two evenings with seed catalogs and online databases before she found it in a scanned 1979 newsletter from a regional seed savers' exchange: *Halloran's Ribbed Pink. Stuffing type, Pennsylvania origin. Offered by E. Halloran, Scranton. Listed as lost, 1986.*

Lost. She read the word three times. An extinct thing was sitting in Bed Nine, being nibbled by slugs.

The bed belonged to Walter Brisco, who was eighty-one and came every morning at seven with a thermos and a folding stool. He had the slow, deliberate hands of a man who had once worked with machinery. Margaret approached him the way she had once approached a nervous comptroller: politely, with documentation.

"Mr. Brisco, I believe these are Halloran's Ribbed Pink."

He looked at the printout for a long time. "Didn't think anybody'd know that name anymore."

"The seed exchange thinks they're extinct."

"They would." He unscrewed his thermos. "Eddie stopped sending them out in eighty-five. Stopped doing much of anything, after."

Eddie Halloran, it emerged, had worked the line beside Walter at a tool-and-die shop in Scranton for twelve years. Eddie grew tomatoes on a rented lot and bred this one himself, crossing an Italian stuffer with something his grandmother had carried from County Mayo. He gave Walter a paper envelope of seeds in 1983.

"Then we had a falling-out," Walter said. "Stupid thing. Money I owed him, and pride on both sides. I moved out here for work. Meant to call. Kept meaning to." He turned a tomato in his palm, running his thumb along its ribs. "Heard he passed in eighty-six. Lot of him went with him. Not this, though."

Every year for forty years, Walter had planted the seeds, chosen the best fruit, fermented the pulp in a jar on his windowsill, dried the seeds on coffee filters, and labeled the envelope in pencil. He kept them separate from other tomatoes to prevent crossing. He had never sold or shared them, never told anyone.

"Felt like an apology I could still make," he said. "Every spring. Seemed like the least I could do."

Margaret, who had always believed that what mattered was what got written down, found she had nothing on her clipboard for this.

That night, she added a column to her spreadsheet. She labeled it *Provenance*, then sat a while before typing in the cell for Bed Nine: *E. Halloran, bred c. 1975. Maintained by W. Brisco, 1983–present. Forty consecutive seasons.*

In September, with Walter's permission, she mailed two hundred seeds to the seed savers' exchange, along with her measurements, her photographs, and a one-page history. Walter signed the cover letter in careful block capitals. The listing ran in the spring catalog: *Halloran's Ribbed Pink — rediscovered.*

The following June, Bed Nine had a new wooden sign. Walter had insisted on the wording.

**HALLORAN'S RIBBED PINK. EDDIE'S TOMATO.**

Margaret recorded it in the ledger, under *Label*. It was, she decided, the most accurate entry she had ever made.
