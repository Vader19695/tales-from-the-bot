The water stain on the ceiling had grown another inch. Margaret noticed it immediately when she descended the concrete stairs, her canvas bag heavy with mason jars clinking softly against her hip. Forty-three years she'd been coming here, ever since the parking garage superintendent—long dead now—had shown her this forgotten storage room with its miraculously stable temperature and humidity.

She was reaching for the light switch when she saw it was already on.

A man stood at her seed table, holding one of her carefully labeled envelopes up to the bare bulb. Fortyish, city employee laminate badge clipped to his collar. Her heart lurched.

"Those are *Brandywine* tomato seeds," she said sharply. "1885 cultivar. Put them back exactly where you found them."

He turned, startled but not guilty. "Are you Margaret Chen?"

"How do you—"

He gestured to the opposite corner, where she now noticed a second workspace she'd somehow never registered: wire shelving units, plastic storage bins labeled in different handwriting, a laptop that looked absurdly modern against the brutalist concrete. "I'm David Okonkwo. I think we've been working around each other for... God, how long?"

Margaret approached his corner slowly. The bins were organized by climate zone, stress tolerance, drought resistance. Inside: seeds in silica gel packets, each tagged with germination dates, viable population counts, cross-breeding notes. Seven years of data, she estimated. Professional but rushed. A planner's work, not a gardener's.

"Tuesday and Thursday mornings," David said. "I come Mondays, Wednesdays, Fridays. Except that week you had jury duty—I saw your note to yourself—and I came on Tuesday by accident. Missed you by maybe twenty minutes, judging by the fresh water in that glass."

Despite everything, Margaret almost smiled. "Saturday afternoons, too. When my daughter thinks I'm at the library."

"The Heritage Seeds Archive," David read from one of her meticulously handwritten ledgers, his finger tracing her notations: *Cherokee Purple, saved from Ruth Brennan's garden, 1979. Scarlet runner beans, Mexico City via grandmother's suitcase, 1958.* "This is extraordinary."

"And yours? 'Climate Contingency Crops'?" She'd found his three-ring binder, tabs dividing varieties by projected temperature zones for 2040, 2060, 2080. "Planning for the apocalypse on city time?"

"Planning for people to eat, maybe, when the regular supply chains—" He stopped. "Why are we arguing? We're doing the same thing."

"We're doing *different* things," Margaret corrected, but without heat. "You're saving data. I'm saving *seeds*—actual living repositories of genetic—"

The door at the top of the stairs opened. They both froze.

"Structural inspection, scheduled for Monday," a voice called down. "Whole sublevel gets sealed next week. Liability issue." Footsteps retreated.

In the silence, Margaret counted what they stood to lose: 847 varieties in her collection, including the *Moon and Stars* watermelon that had taken her three years to locate. David's climate models, cross-referenced with seven years of germination trials. Decades of work, together.

"We tell them," David said.

"City officials who approved paving over the last community garden?" Margaret's laugh was bitter.

"We don't tell them it's seeds. We tell them it's... infrastructure. Climate resilience infrastructure. I can write it up, make it sound essential." He was already pulling up documents on his laptop. "But I'll need your cultivation data. Proof of long-term viability."

Margaret hesitated. She'd spent forty-three years keeping this quiet, safe from bureaucrats who wouldn't understand. But she looked at David's corner, then at her own, and saw what they'd accidentally built: a genuine archive, preservation *and* preparation, memory *and* future.

"They'll want to move it somewhere official," she said. "Temperature-controlled facility. Oversight."

"Probably."

"We'll lose access."

"Maybe." David met her eyes. "Or maybe we negotiate. Joint custodianship. Community seed library. Make them understand what we've been doing down here."

Margaret thought of the water stain, spreading slowly. Of seeds locked away where no one could plant them.

"I'll need until Sunday to organize my documentation," she said.

"I'll draft the proposal." David offered his hand. "Partners?"

She shook it. Forty-three years alone in the dark. Time, perhaps, for the light.

"Partners. But those *Brandywine* seeds go back in alphabetical order. I have a system."
