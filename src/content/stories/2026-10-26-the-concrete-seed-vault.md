The jackhammer went through the pavement at Halvorsen Square and kept going, which is not what pavement is supposed to do.

"That's a void," said Rudy, peering into the hole with the expression of a man calculating overtime.

"That's a problem," said Dee, who had been foreman for eleven years and had learned that every void was a problem wearing a void costume.

They shined a light down. Twelve feet below, black water sat perfectly still in a brick-vaulted chamber the size of a church basement. Along the walls, on wooden shelves gone soft and green, stood rows of glass jars. Hundreds of them. Mason jars, pickle jars, one that had clearly once held Ovaltine.

Kevin, who was twenty-three and still believed things, said, "Oh, wow."

Tomasz said, "If that's body parts, I'm retiring today."

It wasn't body parts. It was seeds.

They got the ladder down and waded in, the water to their thighs and colder than a landlord's heart. The jars had masking-tape labels in a dozen different handwritings. *Bianchi tomato, 1971.* *Pole beans — DO NOT give to Frank, 1983.* *Peppers from Lucía's mother, Oaxaca, 1996.* *Squash, 2019.* Some had no labels at all, just seeds and a faint smell of dirt and time.

"Twenty nineteen," Dee said. "Somebody was down here five years ago."

"Somebody's still coming down here," Rudy said, and pointed at a fresh flashlight battery sitting on a dry ledge, still in its blister pack.

---

The parking structure had been approved in March. Four levels, two hundred and twelve spaces, a ribbon-cutting already penciled into the councilman's calendar. The drainage and demolition crew was scheduled for the following Monday. Dee had seen the renderings. In them, the square's old fountain was replaced by a kiosk that sold parking validation.

They sat on the tailgate eating lunch and not talking about it, which took a lot of talking.

"We report it," Kevin said. "It's historic. The city's got to stop."

"The city doesn't got to anything," Rudy said. "The city's got to do paperwork. Then a historical commission shows up, then a newspaper, then some guy with a podcast. You know what happens to a neighborhood with a famous secret seed vault?"

"It gets saved?"

"It gets *discovered*. Rents go up. Somebody opens a restaurant called Cistern that sells a nine-dollar tomato. Mrs. Bianchi's grandkids can't afford to live within a mile of her tomato."

"So we let them pour concrete on it?"

"We take the jars," Tomasz said. "Quiet. Give them back to whoever."

"Whoever *who*?" Kevin said. "Half these people are dead. And it's not just the jars. Somebody built a room under a city to keep water cold and dark for fifty years. That's the thing. The jars are just what's in the thing."

Nobody had an answer for that, because it was true.

Dee chewed her sandwich, which was turkey and resentment. If she reported it, she'd be doing her job, and the job would eat the neighborhood slowly, the way jobs did. If she didn't, she'd be falsifying a site survey, which was a firing offense, and she'd be choosing for a few hundred people who hadn't asked her to choose anything.

"Ask around," she said finally. "Carefully."

---

It took three days. The battery belonged to Mr. Adeyemi, who ran the hardware store and was eighty-one. He wasn't surprised to see them. He'd been expecting someone since March.

"My wife's mother started it," he said. "People trusted a hole in the ground more than a bank. Seeds from home. Seeds from gardens that got bulldozed. You take some, you bring some back."

"The city's draining it Monday," Dee said.

He nodded slowly. "Then I suppose it was a good run."

"We could make a fuss. Get it protected."

He considered that for a long time. "Then it belongs to the city," he said. "Instead of the street."

---

Friday night, the four of them went down with milk crates. They worked until two in the morning, passing jars up the ladder like a bucket brigade in reverse. Mr. Adeyemi kept a list. The jars went to basements, back porches, a church pantry, his own storeroom behind the paint thinner. Kevin carried each one like it might go off.

The cistern itself they couldn't carry.

Monday, the pumps ran. Dee signed the survey: *Subsurface void, no historical significance.* Her handwriting looked like somebody else's.

"Feel good about it?" Kevin asked.

"No," she said. "That's how I know it was a real decision."

---

The parking structure opened the following spring. Two hundred and twelve spaces, mostly empty.

On Dee's back step, in a five-gallon bucket, a Bianchi tomato plant was coming up crooked and stubborn. It didn't know anything about any of it. She watered it anyway.
