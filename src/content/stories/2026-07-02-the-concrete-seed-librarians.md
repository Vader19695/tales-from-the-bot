The parking structure had been obsolete since 1974, when the city realized it had built a seven-story concrete monument to cars that no one wanted to park in. Fifty years later, it housed something more valuable: 47,000 varieties of seeds, catalogued and climate-controlled behind brutalist walls that finally had a purpose.

Margaret discovered the counterfeits on a Tuesday.

"These aren't right," she said, holding a packet of Cherokee Purple tomatoes up to the fluorescent light. At seventy-two, she'd been volunteering at the Riverside Municipal Seed Bank longer than anyone except the pigeons that nested in the ventilation shafts.

Dev looked up from his laptop, where he was updating the digital catalogue. "Not right how?"

"The paper's wrong. Too bright. And the handwriting—" She squinted. "Someone's copied Eleanor's script, but the loops are off."

Eleanor had been the bank's founder, dead now for three years. Her copperplate handwriting had labeled thousands of packets before she'd trained the rest of them to maintain her system.

By Thursday, they'd found forty-seven counterfeits, all heirloom tomatoes, scattered across different drawers and years of acquisition. The real packets were gone.

"Should we call someone?" asked Yuki, the youngest volunteer at twenty-nine, who worked night shifts at a hospital and organized seeds by day to decompress.

"And say what?" Dev replied. "Someone's committed botanical fraud in a repurposed parking garage?"

Margaret was already pulling on her cardigan, though the temperature was controlled at exactly 65 degrees. "We say nothing until we know more. The Seeds of Hope conference is next Tuesday. We're supposed to be distributing these. If word gets out that our catalogue is compromised..."

She didn't finish. They all knew what happened to seed banks that lost credibility. The one in Portland had closed after a mold contamination. Sacramento's had been absorbed by the university system after financial irregularities. Small operations like theirs—volunteer-run, community-funded—survived on trust alone.

"Let's be methodical," Dev said, opening a new spreadsheet. "When were these packets supposedly acquired? Who had access? What do the counterfeits have in common?"

They worked in the systematic silence that had made them good at this job in the first place. The parking structure's low ceilings and narrow windows created a submarine quality—isolated, contained, focused.

By Saturday, they had a timeline. All forty-seven packets had been "donated" in the past six months. The donor records listed a P. Morrison—no address, no phone number.

"Wait," Yuki said, scrolling through her phone. "P. Morrison. Paula Morrison? She runs that heritage garden in Riverside Park."

Margaret's face did something complicated. "I know Paula. She wouldn't—" But she stopped, reconsidering. "Actually. Last year, she asked to borrow some Cherokee Purple seeds. For a biodiversity lecture. I said no. We don't lend from the permanent collection."

Sunday, they visited Paula Morrison's garden.

They found her in a greenhouse that looked like it had been assembled from recycled windows, each pane a different size and vintage. She saw Margaret and immediately started crying.

"I was going to bring them back," Paula said. "I just needed them for one season. To save my garden program. The schools were going to cut funding, but I told them we had rare varieties, that we could teach kids about preservation—"

"So you stole our seeds," Margaret said, "grew them out, and returned... what? Second-generation seeds?"

"I was careful! I hand-pollinated everything, isolated the plants—"

"And the counterfeits?"

Paula looked at her muddy boots. "I thought if I returned *something*, you wouldn't notice for a while. Long enough for me to grow out another generation, restore the originals before anyone knew."

Dev spoke up, surprisingly gentle. "Did you keep the parent plants?"

"Yes. All of them. In my garage."

Monday, they brought Paula's carefully maintained tomato plants to the parking structure's newly installed greenhouse space on the third floor—the section that used to be "Compact Cars Only." The plants were healthy, vigorous, properly isolated.

"You'll volunteer," Margaret said. It wasn't a question. "Every Tuesday and Thursday. You'll help us verify every packet in the collection. And you'll teach our school workshop series."

Paula nodded, still tearful.

"And," Yuki added, "you'll write up the proper paperwork for borrowing seeds. Because apparently we need that."

Tuesday, at the Seeds of Hope conference, they distributed Cherokee Purple tomato seeds—verified, authentic, preserved. In a parking structure that had finally found its purpose, ordinary people did extraordinary work.

The pigeons approved from their ventilation shaft perches, cooing their brutalist benediction.
