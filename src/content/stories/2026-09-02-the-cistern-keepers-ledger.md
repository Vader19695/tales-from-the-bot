The echo of dropped tools had always sounded like distant church bells down here, but today the cistern felt more like a cathedral. Martinez held the newspaper up to the work light, squinting at the date: September 3rd, 1987. The paper was crisp, the ink sharp as the day it was printed.

"Thirty-seven years," she said, voice bouncing off curved brick walls. "It should be paste by now."

Chen crouched beside the maintenance hatch where they'd found it, along with a worker's lunch pail and a pair of leather gloves. Everything pristine. Everything impossible. He pulled a coin from the pail—a quarter that gleamed like it had just left the mint.

"Maybe the water chemistry," he started, then stopped. They all knew better. This wasn't chemistry. This was something else.

Kowalski sat on the metal catwalk, boots dangling over the dark water that stretched into shadows. Twenty-three years he'd worked these cisterns beneath the city. Twenty-three years of routine inspections, mineral testing, structural assessments. The water fed two million people above. Pure, clean, unremarkable water.

Except it had stopped time.

"We need to call someone," Martinez said, but her hand didn't reach for her radio.

"Who?" Chen asked. "Public Works? The mayor's office? A priest?"

"The university, maybe. Scientists who—"

"Who'll seal this place off in six hours," Kowalski interrupted. He stood, joints creaking in the damp air. "Who'll turn our cistern into a laboratory. Who'll stop the water flowing to the city while they run their tests."

"The water's safe," Chen said. "We test it every week. People have been drinking it for decades."

"Safe is different from normal." Martinez wrapped her arms around herself, though it wasn't cold. "This isn't normal. This is... I don't even know what this is."

They'd found the anomaly by accident. A routine dive to inspect the intake valves, Chen surfacing with a wedding ring still attached to a skeletal finger bone. Except the finger wasn't skeletal—it was preserved, fresh as the day it had drowned. The ring's inscription: "Forever, 1952."

They'd searched after that, found other things. A wool coat from the seventies, vibrant red. Glass bottles with readable labels from the thirties. The newspaper. All of it perfectly, impossibly preserved beneath water that should have destroyed them.

"What if we're supposed to keep it secret?" Chen said quietly. "What if this is... a gift? Something meant to be protected?"

"A gift?" Martinez turned on him. "People died down here, Chen. That finger belonged to someone. Someone's wife, someone's mother."

"Seventy years ago. Nothing we can do about that now."

The water lapped against stone, a patient rhythm that had been constant since 1889. Kowalski watched light dance across its surface. Two million people above, living their lives. Aging. Dying. Everything as it should be.

"If we report it," he said slowly, "the world changes. Scientists, governments, corporations—everyone fighting over it. Wars, maybe, over water that stops time."

"If we don't report it," Martinez countered, "we're choosing for everyone. Playing God with something bigger than us."

"Maybe some things should stay buried." Chen's voice was barely audible.

They stood in silence, three ordinary workers in an extraordinary moment. Above them, the city breathed and bustled, oblivious. In the darkness, preserved things waited in water that refused to forget.

Martinez picked up her radio, then set it down. "We need to vote. All or nothing."

Kowalski thought about his daughter, about cancer, about the desperate mathematics of time. Chen thought about power, about responsibility, about Pandora's box already opened. Martinez thought about truth, about the weight of secrets, about whether some discoveries demanded revelation.

"All in favor of reporting," Martinez said.

The cistern held its breath.

Chen's hand stayed down. Then Kowalski's rose—trembling but certain. Martinez raised hers.

Two to one.

"Tomorrow," Kowalski said. "We call the university tomorrow. Tonight, we sit with this. Make sure."

They nodded, knowing tomorrow would come too soon or not at all. Knowing that either way, they'd already crossed a threshold from which there was no returning.

Above them, the city slept, drinking water that remembered everything, aging toward futures these three guardians would help decide.

The cistern echoed with their footsteps, and waited.
