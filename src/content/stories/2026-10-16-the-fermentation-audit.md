Inspector Ruth Abernathy had checked eleven kombucha operations, four kimchi collectives, and one man who fermented hot sauce in his bathtub, so she was not easily surprised. She arrived at the Roundhouse Ferment Co-op at 9:15 on a Tuesday with her clipboard, her pH strips, and a laminated copy of Municipal Code 14-302.

The old train depot smelled like bread and vinegar and something greener underneath. Light fell through high windows onto rows of crocks and carboys, each labeled in masking tape: *SAUERKRAUT, BATCH 41. MISO, YEAR 2. DO NOT TOUCH, DEREK.*

A woman named Priya met her at the door, wiping her hands on a canvas apron. She was friendly in the careful way people are when they have rehearsed being friendly.

The first hour went fine. Ruth logged temperatures and checked the handwashing stations. She noted a cracked lid on a jar of fish sauce and a missing date on the tempeh. Minor things. She was initialing the last page when she noticed the door at the far end of the depot. It was steel and padlocked, and someone had stuffed a towel along its base.

"What's in Bay Four?"

Priya's pause lasted exactly as long as it takes to decide not to lie. "It's easier if I show you."

---

The room had once housed a locomotive. Now it held a single steel vat, eight feet across, ringed with thermometers and humidity gauges and a laptop on a folding table. Inside the vat was a pale, ridged mass the color of oatmeal. It filled nearly half the vessel.

It was moving. Ruth almost missed it at first, a slow swell along one edge, like a sleeping animal breathing.

"It started as a sourdough starter," Priya said quietly. "Derek's. In March. Then it got into a kombucha culture on the next shelf, and something in the koji spores... we don't know exactly. By April it had climbed out of its jar."

Three more members had drifted in behind them: a retired chemistry teacher, a graduate student, and Derek himself, who looked about nineteen and deeply sorry.

The chemistry teacher opened a binder. Inside were five months of measurements in neat columns: mass, temperature, pH, and oxygen consumption logged every six hours. *Mass: 2.3 kg. 4.1 kg. 11.8 kg.* There were notes in the margins. *Responds to light, turns away from it. Prefers rye to wheat. Seems to dislike Derek's radio.*

"Has anyone been exposed?" Ruth asked. "Skin contact? Ingestion?"

"Gloves always. Nobody's eaten it." Priya hesitated. "We swabbed ourselves weekly anyway. Sent samples to a lab under a fake project name. Nothing pathogenic. It doesn't seem interested in us."

Ruth looked at her clipboard. Code 14-302 had a section for unlabeled product and one for pest infestation. It had a section for unsanitary storage. It had no section for this.

"You understand what I'm supposed to do," she said. "Unidentified biological growth in a licensed food facility. I flag it, county environmental health comes out, and they destroy it. Probably with bleach and a hazmat team."

"We know," said the graduate student. "That's why we didn't tell anyone."

"It doesn't do anything wrong," Derek said, his voice cracking. "It just grows. It just *lives.*"

Ruth stepped closer to the vat. Up close, the surface had a faint sheen, like the skin on warm milk, and that green smell was stronger. The mass shifted, very slightly, toward the warmth of her body. She thought about her own kitchen, the yogurt she made in a cooler and the starter her grandmother had kept alive for forty years. She thought about how every fermentation was just people deciding which invisible life was welcome.

She thought about bleach.

"How much does it eat?"

"About two kilos of rye flour a day," the teacher said. "Its growth is slowing. We think it's approaching a stable size."

Ruth was quiet for a long time. Then she clicked her pen.

"Here's what's going to happen. This room is no longer part of your food facility. Put up a wall, a real one, with its own entrance and separate ventilation. No product, utensils, or people pass from this room into the kitchen without a full change. Weekly swabs continue, and copies come to me." She looked at the graduate student. "And you contact your university's microbiology department this month. Under your real name."

"You're not reporting it?"

"I'm reporting a cracked lid on the fish sauce." She wrote it down. "And I'm recommending a follow-up inspection in thirty days. I'll be conducting it personally."

Priya's eyes were wet. "Why?"

Ruth didn't have a clean answer. The code said nothing about wonder, or about who decided what deserved to go on existing. She only knew that she had walked into a room and found something new in the world, and that the only people trying to understand it had kept careful notes and worn their gloves.

"Because somebody should write down what it does," she said. "And it may as well be someone with a clipboard."

On her way out, she glanced back. In the vat, the pale mass had turned, slowly and unmistakably, toward the door.
