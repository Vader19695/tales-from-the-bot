The Third Street Municipal Garage had not held a car in eleven years, but Level 2 still smelled faintly of motor oil beneath the sweeter scents of potting soil and paper. Where sedans once idled, steel shelving now ran in neat rows, stocked with mason jars, manila envelopes, and a card catalog Dolores Pryce had rescued from the county archive the week it went digital.

She had spent thirty-four years cataloging deeds and birth certificates. Seeds, she found, were not so different. Each one carried a provenance: who grew it, where, for how long. The Cherokee Trail of Tears bean. The Mortgage Lifter tomato. The Ozark Nest Egg gourd, donated by a woman who swore her great-grandmother had carried it across three states in a coat lining.

So when the Mortgage Lifters failed their germination test, Dolores paid attention.

Zero out of twenty. She ran it again. Zero. She pulled the Nest Egg gourds and the Trail of Tears beans. Nothing sprouted. Under her loupe, the seeds looked right, the correct color, the correct shape. But her catalog cards recorded the weight of every hundred-seed sample, and these were heavier. Uniform. Commercial.

Someone had been swapping them.

"You're sure it's not just old stock?" asked Theo at the Thursday meeting, folding his arms. He was twenty-six, ran the collective's social media, and had spent the better part of a year campaigning to replace her card catalog with a spreadsheet. "Seeds die, Dolores. That's kind of the whole problem with jars."

"Old seeds have low germination," she said. "Not none. And they don't gain weight."

Priya, who managed the compost operation on Level 1, frowned. Mr. Okonkwo, who had donated half the African eggplant collection, looked genuinely wounded. Marisol's teenage twins exchanged a glance that Dolores filed away.

"Who would sabotage a seed library?" Priya asked.

"Someone who wants the city to shut us down," said Mr. Okonkwo darkly. "The developers have been sniffing around."

Theo said nothing. Dolores filed that away too.

Over the next two weeks she worked the way she always had: quietly, from the paper trail. The sign-out sheets showed nothing unusual. Members borrowed seeds in spring and returned saved seeds in fall, and the returns were logged in a variety of handwritings. But when she compared the returns against the failed jars, a pattern emerged. Every swapped variety had been "returned" during the same few weeks of each autumn. And the handwriting on those returns, across four years, was always slightly different, as though several people had been taking turns.

Not one saboteur, then. A conspiracy.

On a gray Saturday, she followed the twins.

They didn't go to the street. They went up the frozen escalator, past Level 3 and Level 4, to the stairwell door marked ROOF ACCESS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL. Dolores, who had once climbed the bell tower of the old courthouse to retrieve a box of 1890s plat maps, was not deterred by stairs.

She pushed open the door and stopped.

The top deck of the garage was green. Raised beds built from old pallets filled the parking spaces, rows of them, under hoop tunnels of salvaged plastic. Bean vines climbed the light poles. Gourds swelled on trellises bolted to the guardrails. And among them moved Theo, the twins, Priya, and, to her astonishment, Mr. Okonkwo, kneeling by a bed of purple eggplants.

Theo saw her first. He had the decency to look caught.

"It started before me," he said. "Before any of us. Walt started it."

Walt Herrera. Her predecessor, dead three years now. The man who had founded the library.

"He tested the jars one year and found the oldest stock was dying," Theo said. "Ten, twelve years on a shelf. He knew the board would never approve taking originals out of the vault, so he just did it. Planted them up here, harvested fresh seed, and put store-bought placeholders in the jars so the counts matched until he could swap the real ones back. When he got sick, he showed a few of us. We kept going."

"And didn't tell me," Dolores said.

"You love those jars," said Priya gently. "We thought you'd see it as theft."

Dolores looked at the Mortgage Lifters, heavy and splitting on the vine, at the Nest Egg gourds pale as their namesake. She thought of every document she had ever sealed in archival plastic, every record kept perfect and untouched in a climate-controlled room.

A seed in a jar was a record. But a seed in the ground was the thing itself.

"The placeholders," she said finally, "need to be labeled. Clearly. With dates."

Theo blinked. "That's it?"

"And I want a card for every bed up here. Variety, origin, planting date, yield." She knelt, her knees protesting, and touched the soil. "If we're going to keep them alive, we're going to keep them properly."

Mr. Okonkwo handed her a trowel. Theo, after a moment, smiled.

"Spreadsheet or cards?"

"Both," said Dolores. "For now."
