Dr. Sarah Chen stood ankle-deep in settling tank three, holding a clipboard that was slowly dissolving in the mist, and wondered if this was how careers ended—not with a bang, but with a fungus.

"Run it again," she said to Marcus, her grad student, who was crouched by his laptop on the catwalk above, looking miserable.

"Sarah, we've run it six times. The correlation holds at 94.7%."

She stared at the delicate white threads spreading across the tank's surface like veins on a leaf. Three days ago, they'd spiked in the northwest quadrant. Yesterday, pump station seven—located in the northwest part of the city—had failed. Just like the model predicted.

The model that was based on fungus.

Her supervisor's voice echoed in her head from their meeting that morning: "Chen, I approved your mycological survey because you promised actionable data on treatment efficiency. Not because I wanted you to audition for a Netflix documentary about mushrooms solving crimes."

"They're not mushrooms, they're fungal networks," she'd said.

"I don't care if they're the second coming of Christ. Do you know what our budget looks like?"

She did. Everyone did. The facility was held together by duct tape, hope, and increasingly creative interpretations of EPA regulations.

---

"We need better data," Sarah announced at the team meeting the next morning, setting down coffee from the broken machine that only dispensed lukewarm brown liquid, regardless of which button you pressed.

"We have data," said Priya, her postdoc, pulling up the latest maps. "The mycelial growth patterns correspond to underground pipe networks with 95% accuracy. And look—this cluster here started forming two days ago."

They all leaned in. The cluster sat in the eastern quadrant.

"So we predict..." Marcus prompted.

"Pump station twelve should fail within 48 hours," Sarah said quietly.

"Or," offered James from IT, who'd been dragged into this against his will, "we're engaging in advanced confirmation bias with fungi."

"That's what I'm afraid of," Sarah admitted.

---

She requested a meeting with Robert Kowalski, the city's senior infrastructure engineer. He arrived fifteen minutes late, smelling of cigar smoke and radiating the aura of a man with twelve crises already behind him today.

"Dr. Chen. Your email mentioned fungus."

"Fungal networks," she corrected automatically, then caught herself. "Look, I know how this sounds."

"Do you?"

"We think the mycelial structures in our settling tanks are somehow mapping and responding to stress patterns in the underground infrastructure. They're acting like a biological sensor network."

Kowalski stared at her. "The fungus is psychic."

"The fungus is responding to chemical or physical signals we don't fully understand yet. Pipe degradation, pressure changes, maybe microbial activity indicating stress points. They're integrated with the wastewater system. They're showing us things we can't see otherwise."

"And this helps me how? I've got a $2 million budget shortfall and infrastructure from the Carter administration."

Sarah pulled out her tablet. "Two days ago, we predicted pump station seven would fail. It did. Yesterday, our model indicated pump station twelve. When's the last time that station was serviced?"

Kowalski's face changed. He pulled out his phone, typed rapidly, then looked up. "Eighteen months ago. It's... actually due for inspection."

"So inspect it."

---

They all stood in the facility's break room forty-eight hours later, watching Kowalski's text messages arrive in real time.

*Station 12. Bearing wear. Critical. Shutting down for emergency maintenance.*

Then: *How the hell did you know?*

The room erupted. Marcus hugged Priya. James muttered something about updating his LinkedIn profile to include "fungal IT support."

Sarah's supervisor appeared in the doorway, coffee in hand. "Chen. My office. Now."

She followed, preparing for the worst.

He closed the door. "The mayor's office just called. Apparently, Kowalski's been singing your praises. They want a presentation next week about predictive maintenance." He paused. "They used the phrase 'revolutionary cost savings.'"

"So... we're not fired?"

"You're getting a budget increase." He smiled slightly. "Though for the love of God, don't lead with 'the fungus is psychic.'"

Sarah laughed, surprising herself. "The fungus isn't psychic."

"I know. It's—what was it?"

"Fungal networks," she said. "And they're just doing what they've always done—connecting, communicating, responding to their environment. We just finally learned to listen."

He nodded. "Write the report. And Chen? Get some sleep. You look like something that grows in a settling tank."

"Thanks," she said. "That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me."
