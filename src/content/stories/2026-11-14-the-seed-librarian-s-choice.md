Mira Halvorsen found the first one on a Tuesday, during the decennial viability audit of Row 14.

The packet was standard: trilaminate foil, heat-sealed, accession number printed in the bank's blunt sans-serif. *SB-2287-04. Lathyrus sp., 212 seeds.* But when she pressed it between her gloved fingers to check for seal integrity, something inside gave a soft, fibrous resistance. Not the dry rattle of dormant seed. Something with structure.

She carried it to the antechamber, let it equilibrate for the mandated hour, and cut it open under the lamp.

Radicles. Dozens of them, pale and translucent as fishing line, frozen mid-reach.

At minus eighteen degrees Celsius and five percent moisture content, a seed does not grow. That is the entire premise of the building: a hillside hollowed out and sealed so that time, for the four hundred thousand samples inside, would slow to something close to geological. Seeds dried and frozen this way could wait a century. They did not wake up and stretch.

By Friday, she had found six more.

---

"It's the intake cooler," said Dev, the facilities engineer, scrolling through temperature logs on his tablet. "Has to be. If something sat at four degrees for a few weeks before transfer, wet enough—"

"Our seeds don't go into the cooler wet. They go through the drying room first. Fifteen percent relative humidity, fifteen degrees, minimum two weeks." Mira had the protocol memorized; she had written half of it. "These weren't dried. Someone primed them. Soaked them just long enough to start the clock, then sealed them."

Dev looked up. "You're saying someone did this on purpose."

"I'm saying the accession numbers are wrong."

That was the part she had not told anyone yet. *SB-2287-04* had been a common grass pea from a Cypriot landrace, catalogued in 2009. The seeds in the packet were smaller, mottled violet, with a distinctive hilum she had only ever seen in herbarium photographs. She had sent images to a colleague in Kew. The reply came back in four hours: *Where did you get these?*

A coastal vetch, last recorded in the wild in 1971. A dwarf lupine from serpentine soils now under a reservoir. A wild rice relative whose final stand had been drained for a car park. Seven packets, seven ghosts, each slipped into a slot belonging to something ordinary.

---

The intake logs showed that every swapped packet had passed through the cooler during shifts worked by Anselm Reyes.

He was sixty-eight, two months from retirement, and the only person on staff who still labelled his thermoses in pencil. He was in the germination lab when she found him, counting emerged seedlings on blotter paper with a click-counter.

She set the opened packet on the bench beside him.

He didn't look surprised. He finished his count, wrote *37/50* in the log, and capped his pen.

"My teacher kept them," he said. "Professor Lindqvist. Forty years of collecting, all in a chest freezer in her garage, because the national program wouldn't accept material without full provenance. She died in March. Her nephew was going to clear the house."

"So you put them here."

"I put them where they'd be safe."

"Then why prime them?" Mira heard her own voice sharpen. "If you wanted them preserved, you could have dried them properly. Nobody would have noticed for a decade."

Anselm turned the counter over in his hands. "That's exactly the problem. Nobody would have noticed. They'd have sat in the dark under the wrong names until the foil failed. A seed bank is a promise to grow things someday, Mira. I've watched us keep that promise for forty years by postponing it." He nodded at the radicles. "Policy says compromised viability triggers regeneration. Sprouted seed gets grown out or destroyed. I wanted someone to have to choose."

---

She sat with the choice for three days.

The arguments against were real, and she wrote them down because that was how she thought. No provenance. Unknown pathogen load. Possible hybridization if grown near related accessions. No habitat to return them to—the serpentine hills were underwater, the marsh was asphalt. Regenerating them would mean claiming them, and claiming them would mean someday answering for whatever they became.

The argument for was shorter. It was the radicles themselves, reaching for soil that wasn't there.

On Monday, she filed seven new accession records, numbered in sequence, each annotated in full: *Donor: E. Lindqvist estate. Provenance incomplete. Received irregularly. See incident report.* She did not leave Anselm's name out.

Then she carried the packets to the quarantine greenhouse, filled a tray with sterile loam, and pressed each pale thread into the dark, gently, the way you'd return something you had borrowed for far too long.
