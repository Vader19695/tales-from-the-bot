The Hollis Cold Storage building still smelled faintly of frozen peas, though it had not held a pea for commercial purposes in thirty years. Now it held seeds: eleven thousand packets in labeled drawers, a walk-in freezer full of the irreplaceable, and one retired botanist, Marguerite Feeney, who had volunteered to "help with cataloguing" and had somehow become the person everyone called when anything went wrong.

What had gone wrong was the beans.

"Nothing," said Dale Ostrowski, standing in her office with the aggrieved posture of a man whose garden had embarrassed him in front of his neighbors. "Not a sprout. Mrs. Pell's Purple Bush. I've grown beans since Carter. Beans are what you give children so they believe in themselves."

Marguerite took the leftover seeds home and ran a germination test on damp paper towels. Zero of fifty. She cut one open under her old dissecting scope. The embryo was cooked, a tidy brown ruin. Someone had baked them. Low oven, she guessed. Patient work.

Over the next week she talked to six more gardeners. Priya Raman's Otterburn Runner squash: nothing. The Lindqvist twins' Blue Hubbard: nothing, and the twins had blamed each other so thoroughly that they were no longer speaking. Every failure was a rare variety. Every packet was a perfect copy, down to the handwritten labels in the bank's trademark green ink.

"Whoever did this has access to the label pens," Marguerite told the board treasurer, Harold Brisk, a man who wore cardigans as a moral position.

"Lots of people have access to the pens," Harold said, a little too quickly.

Marguerite had spent forty years watching plants give themselves away. People were easier. She let the silence go on until Harold filled it.

"They were *losing* them," he said at last. "Do you know how many Mrs. Pell's seeds came back last fall? Nine. We lent out two hundred. People plant them next to their hybrid nonsense, they cross-pollinate, they forget to save seed, they go to Arizona for the winter. A seed bank that lends out its last copies isn't a bank. It's a casino."

"So you gave them sterile decoys."

"Temporarily. The originals are safe. Drawer forty-one, back of the walk-in. I was going to bring it up at the meeting."

"When?"

"Eventually," said Harold, which was, she reflected, the motto of every board she had ever served on.

They went to the walk-in together. Drawer forty-one held a neat row of envelopes. Marguerite opened the first. Inside was a folded index card in a different hand: *Seeds are not stamps.*

Harold made a sound like a radiator.

It took her two days to find Theo Marsh, the twenty-four-year-old who ran the Saturday compost workshops and believed, sincerely and at length, that most problems were really soil problems. He was in a vacant lot behind the laundromat on Fifth, kneeling in an astonishing tangle of vines.

"Harold's an archivist," Theo said, not getting up. "I'm not. A variety that only exists in a freezer isn't alive. It's taxidermy. Mrs. Pell didn't freeze her beans, she grew them, and they changed every year she grew them, and that's how they got good."

"You knew he was swapping packets."

"I watched him do it. I figured if he was going to hide them, I'd un-hide them." He gestured at the lot. "Everything's crossing with everything. Half these squash don't have names yet."

"Dale Ostrowski thinks he's lost his touch with beans. The Lindqvists aren't speaking."

Theo had the grace to wince. "That part I didn't think through."

"Neither of you did," Marguerite said. "That's the trouble with principles. They're very tidy until they reach other people."

She walked the rows. The beans were mostly purple, some streaked, some an unfamiliar mottled bronze that Mrs. Pell would not have recognized and might have liked. They were heavy with pods. Whatever they were, they were not dying out.

She thought about Harold's freezer, which kept things exactly as they were, and Theo's lot, which kept nothing exactly as it was, and about the fact that she had spent her own career doing a bit of both and calling it science.

At the next board meeting, she set two jars on the table.

The first held seeds from Harold's few untouched reserves, true Mrs. Pell's, which would go back into the freezer under two signatures instead of one. The second held seeds from the laundromat lot, labeled in green ink: *Mrs. Pell's (Fifth Street Strain), Parentage Uncertain, Vigor Excellent.*

"Both get catalogued," she said. "One for keeping. One for lending. And Harold buys Dale Ostrowski a very good bottle of something, and Theo explains the whole thing to the Lindqvists, together, in person."

Harold opened his mouth. Theo opened his. Marguerite raised a hand.

"Nobody here is wrong," she said. "That's what makes it so exhausting."

In May, Dale's beans came up in four days. He called to tell her, sounding forty years younger, and asked what the bronze ones were.

"Nobody knows yet," Marguerite said. "Save some seed, and we'll find out."
