Margaret arrived at the Bonneville flats at 4:47 PM, exactly as planned. She parked her sedan at the usual coordinates, retrieved her leather-bound journal and measuring tape, and began her quarterly documentation of the geometric salt ponds that stretched toward the horizon like frozen mathematics.

Her mentor, Dr. Ashford, had started this ritual forty years ago. When he retired, he'd given Margaret the journal—138 pages of meticulous measurements, sketches, and notations in his cramped handwriting. "Keep the record," he'd said. "Every three months. It matters."

She'd never asked why. Dr. Ashford didn't invite questions.

Margaret had continued the work for seven years now, adding her own pages. Pond 17-A: 47.3 meters across, hexagonal, salt crystals forming dendrite patterns at northeast edge. The precision soothed her. Numbers were honest. They accumulated into truth.

She was measuring Pond 22-C when she saw them: footprints in the salt crust, crisp and recent. Not her own.

Her chest tightened. In seven years, she'd never encountered another person here. That was the point—the isolation, the purity of unwitnessed work.

The prints led west, toward a ridge she'd always noted but never explored. Margaret glanced at her watch. She was already twelve minutes behind schedule. But the prints disturbed the pattern, introduced a variable she couldn't ignore.

She followed them.

Behind the ridge, sheltered from the wind, sat a modest camp: a tent, a camping stove, and a folding table covered with papers. A man looked up from his work, startled but not alarmed. He was perhaps sixty, with silver hair and sun-weathered hands.

"I didn't think anyone came here," he said.

"I could say the same."

He gestured to the empty expanse. "Plenty of salt for both of us."

Margaret noticed the papers on his table—photocopies of survey maps, old geological reports. And something else: pages that looked disturbingly familiar. Her stomach dropped.

"Is that Dr. Ashford's work?"

The man's expression shifted, became careful. "You knew Gerald?"

"He was my mentor. He gave me the journal. I've been continuing his records."

The man sat back slowly. "I'm Daniel. I worked with Gerald thirty years ago, before you." He paused. "Before the incident."

"What incident?"

Daniel's hands moved to the papers, protective. "The mining company. 1987. They wanted to extract lithium from these flats. Gerald was hired to do the geological survey. He found it—vast deposits. Enough to make them billions."

Margaret's throat felt dry. "He never mentioned—"

"He falsified the report," Daniel said quietly. "Documented everything except the lithium. Made it look geologically worthless. The company walked away."

The words hung between them like heat shimmer.

"I helped him do it," Daniel continued. "We both believed these flats should remain untouched. But I've spent thirty years wondering if we had the right to make that choice. To bury the truth." He gestured to his papers. "I've been coming here, gathering evidence, preparing to release the real findings."

Margaret looked at the copied pages—Dr. Ashford's handwriting, but different sections than what she'd inherited. The hidden data.

"And I've been preserving his cover story," she said slowly. "Recording the surface, never knowing what was underneath."

They stood in silence, two accountants of different truths.

"The mining company still exists," Daniel said. "If I release this, they'll come back. Destroy all of this." He swept his hand toward the pristine flats. "But isn't the truth worth preserving, even if it's destructive?"

Margaret thought of her seven years of careful measurements. The comfort of documentation. But Dr. Ashford hadn't given her the journal for comfort—he'd given her a choice she didn't know she was making.

"Maybe the question isn't whether we tell the truth," she said. "Maybe it's whether this particular truth, right now, creates more honesty or more harm."

Daniel looked at her for a long moment, then at his papers.

Together, they made a fire. The pages curled and blackened, both the false records and the true ones, leaving only the salt and the people who remembered. They would keep coming, she and Daniel, alternating months now. Measuring the surface, protecting the depths, bearing witness to the complicated weight of what they chose to preserve and what they let burn away.

Some ledgers, they agreed, were kept in salt and silence.
