The fluorescent lights flickered when Maya heard footsteps on the concrete ramp. Wrong rhythm. Not Davide's shuffle or Chen's quick clip. Someone new.

"We've got company," she said, not looking up from the seed packets she was cataloging. Armenian cucumber, saved from Mrs. Kasparian's garden before the building sold. Three years of careful selection.

The inspector wore a grey suit and carried a tablet like a shield. "This level is supposed to be vacant. I'm going to need to see permits for—" He stopped, taking in the rows of climate-controlled cabinets, the humidity monitors, the drying racks hung with seed heads. "What is this?"

"Seed library," said Davide, emerging from the back room. "Five thousand varieties. Some over a hundred years old."

The inspector's face hardened. "Do you have permission to operate here?"

"Define 'operate,'" said Maya.

"Don't." Yuki appeared from behind a cabinet, her hands still dusty from cleaning fava beans. "We don't have permits. We know."

The inspector tapped his tablet. "Then you know I'm authorized to—"

"To what?" Chen stood in the doorway, blocking the exit without seeming to block it. "Confiscate seeds? They're not contraband."

"The space is. Unauthorized occupation, potential safety violations, unpermitted alterations to city property." He gestured at the insulated walls they'd installed. "This needs to be dismantled."

Maya set down her packets with careful precision. "How long do we have?"

"I can give you until Friday. Four days."

Rashid finally spoke from his corner workstation. "Some of these seeds are extinct in their countries of origin. Wars. Climate change. Corporate consolidation. We have the only copies."

"I understand it's meaningful work," the inspector said, and he sounded almost genuine. "But the regulations exist for reasons."

"So do we," said Yuki.

After the inspector left, they sat among their cabinets in silence. Five years. Hundreds of conversations with gardeners who'd smuggled seeds in coat linings, who'd kept varieties alive through decades in new countries, who'd trusted them with their inheritance.

"We could pack it up," Davide said finally. "Find somewhere else."

"Where?" Chen's laugh was bitter. "You know what storage costs. We barely afforded the climate units."

Maya was already thinking. "What if we make it legitimate?"

"How?" Yuki asked.

"The inspector said unpermitted. Not illegal. There's a difference." Maya pulled up the city code on her phone. "We need a sponsor. An institution."

"In four days?"

"The community college is three blocks away. They have a horticulture program." Maya was scrolling fast. "If we could get them to adopt this as an educational facility—"

Chen was already nodding. "I know someone on the faculty. We rebuilt her mother's garden last year."

"It's still a long shot," Rashid said, but he was pulling up contact information.

Davide stood. "I'll draft the proposal. Rashid, inventory documentation—everything we have on provenance and cultural significance. Yuki, Chen, can you handle the college connections?"

"What about you?" Yuki asked Maya.

Maya looked at the Armenian cucumbers. When Mrs. Kasparian had given them to her, the old woman's hands had trembled. "My grandmother brought these from Van," she'd said. "In 1915. Sewed them into her dress lining."

"I'll work on the inspector," Maya said. "Buy us more time if I can."

"And if we can't pull it off?" Chen asked.

They looked at each other. Five people who'd met through community garden forums and discovered they wanted the same impossible thing: to preserve what the market wouldn't value, to save what couldn't be monetized.

"Then we pack it up," Maya said. "Take what we can carry. Distribute the rest to our network. Start over somewhere else." She touched the seed packets. "But we don't let them disappear."

"Friday's our deadline," Davide said. "Let's make it count."

They dispersed to their tasks, moving with the efficient urgency of people who'd learned not to waste time or hope, but to use both with precision. The lights hummed. The climate units cycled. In their careful chambers, five thousand varieties waited, patient as seeds always are, for someone to give them ground to grow in.
