Inspector Chen had worked at Municipal Weigh Station 7B for eleven years, long enough to know the difference between a properly calibrated scale and one that had gone rogue. Thursday morning, the readings started acting strange.

The first package—a shoebox wrapped in brown paper—should have weighed perhaps two pounds. The digital display flickered: 47.3 pounds.

"That can't be right," the sender muttered, a woman in a rumpled business suit clutching a coffee. "It's just my daughter's old ballet shoes."

Chen lifted the box. Light as air. He set it down again. 47.3 pounds.

"Why are you sending them?" Chen asked, though this wasn't protocol.

The woman's eyes reddened. "She quit. Blamed me for pushing too hard. This was supposed to be—I don't know—an apology? She's at college now. Won't return my calls."

Chen looked at the scale, then back at the woman. He cleared his throat and stamped the form. "Shipping will cost extra. Heavy package."

By noon, Chen had weighed seventeen items. A birthday cake: 0.2 ounces, sent by a grandmother with a smile like sunlight. A laptop computer: 289 pounds, from a gray-faced man who whispered, "He doesn't know I read his messages." A wedding dress: negative 12 pounds, which should have been impossible—it actually pulled upward against Chen's hands while the bride-to-be laughed into her phone, radiant with anticipation.

Chen's supervisor, Gladys, appeared during his lunch break. She examined the equipment with her scanner, frowning at the displays.

"Calibration's perfect," she said. "Everything checks out on the diagnostics. You must be reading the displays wrong."

"I'm not—"

"Then you're entering package dimensions incorrectly. Be more careful, Chen. This station has performance metrics to maintain." She handed him a new roll of stickers and left.

The afternoon rush brought the real flood: commuters from the subway above, an endless river of people with packages to send. Chen weighed them all. A manuscript: 8 pounds (hope). A jar of preserved lemons: 103 pounds (resentment—something about a mother-in-law's criticism). A child's drawing: 0.01 ounces (pure love, uncomplicated).

At 4:17 PM, a young man approached with a small wooden box.

The scale read: 2,847 pounds.

Chen couldn't lift the box once the man set it down. The scale groaned. The man himself looked hollow, his hands shaking.

"What's inside?" Chen asked quietly.

"My brother's ashes. We hadn't spoken in six years. Stupid argument about money." The man's voice cracked. "He died last month. I'm sending him home to Mom. He should have—I should have—"

Chen watched the number hold steady. 2,847 pounds of guilt, grief, and words unsaid.

"Sir," Chen said slowly, carefully, "before you send this... have you considered taking it yourself?"

"I can't afford the time off work. That's why I—" The man stopped. Looked at Chen. Really looked. "You think I should."

"I think," Chen said, "some packages aren't meant to be shipped. Some weights you have to carry in person."

The man stared at the box for a long moment. Then he picked it up—it couldn't have weighed more than five actual pounds—and cradled it against his chest. "Thank you," he whispered, and walked back toward the subway stairs.

The scale reset to zero.

At 6 PM, Gladys returned with a clipboard. "Corporate's sending a replacement unit Monday. This one's being decommissioned—too many complaints about inaccurate readings."

Chen looked at the line still snaking toward his station. A teenager with a care package. An old man with letters bound in string. A woman holding flowers wrapped in tissue paper.

"I'll need the weekend to transfer files," Chen lied. "Leave it operational until Monday."

Gladys shrugged. "Fine. But come Monday, it's gone."

Chen nodded and turned back to his work. Three days. Seventy-two hours to keep weighing what people carried, to give them a chance to see their invisible burdens made manifest.

He called the next person forward.

"Good evening," he said. "Please place your package on the scale."
