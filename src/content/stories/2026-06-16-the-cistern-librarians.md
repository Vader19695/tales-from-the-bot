The descent into the cistern always felt like entering a church. Mira counted the forty-three steps by heart now, her hand trailing the cool stone wall while her flashlight picked out the familiar graffiti from 1987: *Tommy + Rosa Forever*. The air changed at step thirty, becoming damp and close, smelling of minerals and old paper.

At the bottom, Chen was already checking the water levels, his clipboard balanced on the metal railing. "Down another two inches," he said without looking up. "But the archives are dry. The dehumidifiers are holding."

Mira joined him, peering into the vast darkness beyond their platform. Somewhere in that black expanse, 180,000 gallons waited in reserve—the city's emergency supply, maintained by their four-person volunteer crew. But the cistern's other purpose was what brought her here three times a week: the Archive of Ordinary Things, as Dolores had named it.

Metal filing cabinets lined the platform walls, drawers carefully labeled by decade and category. Inside, protected by plastic sleeves, were thousands of index cards and notebook pages. Mrs. Chen's cure for croup using honey and thyme. The words to "Desert Rain Song" in both English and Spanish. Bobby Martinez's grandmother's tamale recipe, the one she'd brought from Sonora in 1952. Instructions for mending shoes, for storing rainwater, for keeping tomatoes alive in the heat.

"Dolores called," Chen said. "The developer's making his pitch to the council tonight. She wants us there."

Mira opened drawer 1970-1979, pulled out a card at random. *How to save seeds from squash blossoms—wait until the flower dies completely...* The handwriting was shaky but determined, the blue ink faded but legible.

"Are we going?" Chen asked.

Before Mira could answer, footsteps echoed on the stairs. Dolores emerged first, moving slowly on arthritic knees, followed by young James, who'd joined them six months ago after his grandmother died, insisting her recipe for shelf-stable bread be added to the collection.

"I've been thinking," Dolores announced, settling onto the folding chair they kept for her, "about what we're actually protecting here."

"The water," James said promptly. "The city needs—"

"The city has four other cisterns," Dolores interrupted gently. "Newer ones. Better filtration. That developer's not wrong about that part. This one's old. Expensive to maintain properly."

Mira felt something tighten in her chest. "You're saying we should let it go?"

"I'm saying we should be honest." Dolores gestured at the filing cabinets. "It's not the water we care about most, is it? It's these. The things people thought worth remembering."

Chen set down his clipboard. "The developer's proposal includes a digital archive. Scanning everything before they drain it and convert the space to—what was it?"

"Climate-controlled storage for server farms," James said bitterly.

They sat in silence, listening to water drip somewhere in the darkness. Mira thought about the school groups they brought down here, children running their fingers over their great-grandparents' handwriting. The elderly residents who descended those forty-three steps to add one more memory before they forgot it.

"The humidity damages the documents," Chen said slowly. "We lose a few cards every year despite the dehumidifiers. A digital archive would preserve the information indefinitely."

"My grandmother wouldn't have written down her bread recipe to put it in a computer," James said.

"No," Dolores agreed. "She wrote it to put it in someone's hands." She looked at each of them in turn. "What if we proposed something different? Let them have their server farm. But we build a proper archive room upstairs. Climate-controlled, yes. Digital backups, absolutely. But the originals stay accessible. Physical. Real."

Mira felt the tightness ease slightly. "Would they go for that?"

"The developer needs our cooperation to avoid a fight," Dolores said. "We have modest funding, yes, but we also have every family in the barrio who's contributed to these archives. That's votes the council cares about."

Chen was already making notes. "We'd need specifications. Square footage. Access requirements."

"Elevator access," Dolores added firmly.

James pulled out a card, held it toward the light. "We'd be giving up the cistern."

"We'd be doing our job," Mira said quietly, surprising herself. "Adapting. Preserving what matters." She gestured at the darkness around them. "The cistern's just the container. What we're keeping—that's something else."

Dolores smiled. "So. Who's ready to make a counter-proposal?"

They climbed the forty-three steps together, carrying nothing but flashlights and clipboards, leaving the archives sleeping in the dark below—for now.
