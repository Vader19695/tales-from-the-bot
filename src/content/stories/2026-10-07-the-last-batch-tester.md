At 9:40 p.m., Dolores Achterberg held the tray up to the ultraviolet lamp and watched eleven bottles stay clear.

The twelfth glowed a faint, unmistakable blue.

She set the tray down and checked the label twice, though she had written it herself that afternoon: *Larkspur Meadows, hydrant 12-C, dead-end main, 2:15 p.m.* The newest subdivision on the city's east edge. Three hundred and forty homes. Service connection scheduled for six o'clock tomorrow morning.

Fluorescence under UV meant *E. coli*. It meant fecal contamination. It also, according to her own field notes, could not be true. The free chlorine residual at 12-C had read 1.4 milligrams per liter, more than enough to kill anything living in that pipe. The turbidity was clean. The pressure test had held for two hours without a drop.

She pulled up the sample log and ran her finger down the column. Bottle sterility lot: fine. Incubator temperature: 35.0 degrees, steady all day. Field blank: negative. Every protocol she knew said this bottle was impossible.

And yet it glowed.

---

At 10:15 she called Marcus, the night supervisor, who came in from the control room smelling of vending-machine coffee and held the bottle to the lamp himself.

"One out of twelve," he said. "Residual's high. That's a contaminated bottle, Dee. You touched the cap, or the hydrant threads splashed. It happens."

"It might be."

"Rerun takes eighteen hours minimum. You know what happens if we flag it." He didn't say it unkindly. "Families have been living in hotels. The developer's been on the mayor's phone all week. There's a woman out there with a newborn whose lease ran out Friday."

"I know."

"So write it up as a probable sampling error, recommend a confirmatory sample after connection, and let them have their water."

He left the decision on the counter with the tray, which she understood was its own kind of pressure.

---

At 11:30, Priya from the microbiology bench stopped on her way out and listened while Dolores explained.

"Dead-end main," Priya said slowly. "Stagnant water at the far end. Biofilm can shelter bacteria from chlorine. It's rare in new pipe, but not impossible if something got in during construction. A cracked joint. Groundwater intrusion."

"So it could be real."

"It could be real. It could be you." Priya shrugged into her coat. "The test doesn't tell you which. That's the part nobody puts in the manual."

---

After midnight the lab was quiet except for the hum of the incubators. Dolores sat with the bottle and tried, honestly, to remember the hydrant. The wind had been gusting. She had flushed the line for five minutes, flamed the nozzle, uncapped the bottle—had her glove brushed the rim? She could picture herself doing it right. She could also picture herself doing it wrong. Memory, she realized, would give her whichever version she leaned toward.

She thought about the woman with the newborn. She thought about the same newborn, two weeks from now, if the twelfth bottle was telling the truth and nobody listened.

Neither version of tomorrow was hypothetical. Both had faces.

At 2:50 a.m. she opened the reporting form. Under *Result*, she typed *Positive, E. coli, 1 of 12.* Under *Assessment*, she wrote the truth: *Cannot rule out sampling contamination. Cannot rule out contamination of dead-end main. Recommend hold on service pending resample and flushing of 12-C.*

She did not soften it. She did not inflate it. She signed it and sent it to Marcus, the utility director, and the health department, and then she drove out to Larkspur Meadows in the dark and collected three new samples from 12-C herself, with fresh gloves, slowly, in the still air.

---

The connection was postponed. The local news ran footage of moving trucks idling in empty driveways. Marcus didn't speak to her for most of a shift. The developer's office called the director twice.

Thirty hours later, Dolores held the new tray under the lamp.

All three bottles stayed clear.

She stood there a long time. Crews had flushed the main hard before she resampled; maybe they had cleared something real. Maybe there had never been anything to clear. The water would never tell her which, and neither would the families who moved in that afternoon, carrying boxes past the hydrant without a glance.

Priya found her still at the bench. "Negative?"

"Negative."

"So you were wrong."

"Maybe," Dolores said. She labeled the tray, logged the result, and filed it beside the first. "Or maybe that's what being right looks like, in this job. Nothing happens, and you never get to know why."

Outside, across the city, three hundred and forty kitchen faucets turned on, and the water ran clear.
