The first rule of the cistern was that nobody said the word *flood* above a whisper. It felt rude, given the circumstances.

Marguerite Oyelaran, seventy-one, retired reference librarian and self-appointed foreman, was interleaving the pages of a 1924 county ledger with unprinted newsprint when Teo came down the iron ladder two rungs at a time.

"There's a woman," he said. "With a clipboard."

"Everyone has a clipboard, Teo."

"She has a *hard hat*."

Marguerite set down her bone folder. In three years beneath the Calle Sereno pumping station, they had survived rats, a gas leak, and Dolores's brief, disastrous experiment with a dehumidifier bought off a man named Skip. They had never survived a hard hat.

The cistern was a cathedral of brick, ninety feet long, vaulted, built in 1889 and abandoned in 1961 when the city discovered aquifers and stopped believing in rain. It was cool, dark, and steady at fifty-eight degrees, which made it, in a city that hadn't seen real rainfall in eleven years, the best place on earth to dry a book.

And they had so many books to dry. When the water main burst under the old Municipal Records Building, it was the only flood the city had seen in a decade, and it went straight for the land deeds. The city's official response had been to bulldoze the wet boxes into a dumpster. Marguerite's response had been to bring a borrowed truck and her nephew's chest freezers.

Now there were clotheslines strung between the brick piers, pinned with maps. Plywood tables held ledgers fanned open beneath box fans. Freezers hummed in a row, each holding volumes too waterlogged to save yet, suspended like patients awaiting surgery. Dolores had labeled them *Purgatory 1* through *Purgatory 6*.

"We could hide," Teo suggested. "Behind the purgatories."

"There are three thousand items hanging from the ceiling," Dolores said, not looking up from the brittle survey she was blotting. "She'll notice."

They heard boots on the upper grate. A flashlight beam swept the stairwell.

Marguerite had rehearsed this moment for three years, mostly in the shower, mostly losing. The city needed water. A restored cistern could hold four million gallons. People were rationing showers to ninety seconds; children were being sent to school with damp washcloths instead of baths. Against that, what was a ledger?

She knew what a ledger was. It was who owned what, and who had been cheated, and who had lived here before anyone wrote it down. It was the only proof that the Barrio Alto families had held their land before the 1952 "reassessment." It was a hundred thousand small facts nobody would ever know again.

Still, she thought, you cannot drink a fact.

"Well," she said. "Let's not make her climb down in the dark."

She switched on the work lights.

The engineer stopped on the bottom rung and stared. She was perhaps forty, with chalk dust on her sleeves and a tape measure clipped to her belt like a sidearm. Her flashlight drifted from the clotheslines to the freezers to Dolores, who waved with a pair of tweezers.

"I'm Priya Haddad," the woman said slowly. "Water Authority. I was told this was empty."

"It was," said Marguerite. "Then it wasn't. Tea? We have a kettle. We don't have a lot of water for it, but we have a kettle."

Priya did not take tea. She walked the length of the cistern in silence, tapping brick, measuring cracks, occasionally pausing beneath a hanging map like someone at a museum. Teo trailed her with the expression of a man watching his house be appraised by an executioner.

At the far end, she stopped at a table holding a vellum survey, its ink bloomed but legible.

"What's this?"

"An 1891 well survey," Dolores said. "Every private well in the valley. Depths, flow rates. Most were capped when the aquifer came in."

Priya leaned closer. She read for a long time. Then she took off her hard hat, which Marguerite chose to interpret as a sign of respect.

"These wells," Priya said. "We don't have records of half of them. The originals were in—"

"The Records Building," said Marguerite. "Yes."

"Do you have more like this?"

Dolores gestured to the purgatories. "Six freezers' worth."

Priya stood quietly, doing arithmetic of a kind Marguerite recognized: the weighing of one urgency against another.

"The cistern is sound," she said finally. "I'm going to recommend filling it. I'm sorry. That isn't negotiable."

Teo made a small, wounded sound.

"But," Priya continued, "it'll take two months to reline. And there's a vacant storage floor at the Water Authority, climate-controlled, that nobody's using because nobody can afford to cool it. I think I can argue that it's now essential infrastructure." She tapped the survey. "If even ten of these wells are still viable, you've just saved more water than this cistern holds."

Marguerite felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched since the dumpster.

"We'll need a truck," she said.

"And freezer space," said Dolores.

"And for nobody to say *flood*," added Teo.

Priya put her hard hat back on. "I'll take that tea now," she said. "Small cup."

Eight weeks later, water rose in the empty cathedral, quiet and dark. Across town, on the fourth floor, Marguerite pinned the first map to a fresh clothesline.
