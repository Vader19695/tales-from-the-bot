The sign on the warehouse door still said FIXED, NOT TOSSED, though someone had added in pencil underneath: *until the 30th*.

Walt Pruitt, seventy-three, retired after forty years of designing hydraulic valves, was explaining to a toaster why it had betrayed him. "You have one job," he said, probing with a multimeter. "One."

"It has two jobs," said Dolores from the next bench. "Up and down." She'd spent thirty-one years on the line at the Bellwether appliance plant until it moved somewhere with cheaper people. She could rewire a lamp faster than most people could find the switch.

Marcus, sixteen, sat between them, soldering a phone charger under Dolores's supervision. Priya, an illustration student who'd arrived two years ago with a broken sewing machine and never left, was regluing a chair leg and the chair's dignity at the same time.

The lease letter was pinned to the corkboard. The landlord had found a tenant who wanted the space for "luxury self-storage," a phrase Walt said should be illegal. They'd been offered the chance to match the new rent. They'd held a bake sale. It raised four hundred and twelve dollars, which covered about nine days.

At two o'clock a woman came in holding a wooden box against her chest the way people hold things they've already dropped once.

"My mother's," she said. "It stopped playing. I know it's probably not worth—"

"Everything's worth looking at," Dolores said, which was the closest thing the café had to a motto, aside from the sign.

Her name was Ruth. The box was walnut, inlaid with a lopsided rose. Walt opened the bottom with a jeweler's screwdriver, and the others drifted over, because whatever they claimed, nobody can resist a music box.

"Mainspring's snapped," Walt said. "Comb's fine. Cylinder's fine. Pins all there." He sounded personally relieved. "The song's intact. It's just lost the thing that pushes it."

"Can you get a new spring?"

"Not for this. They stopped making this movement in the sixties."

Ruth nodded the way people nod at doctors.

"So we make one," said Marcus.

Walt looked at him over his glasses. "Have you ever tempered spring steel?"

"No. Have you?"

"Nineteen seventy-one," Walt said. "Badly."

Dolores was already digging through the parts drawers: eleven years of salvaged clocks, dead tape decks, and a box labeled SPRINGS (MISC) (ANGRY). Priya traced the broken spring onto graph paper. Marcus held the torch while Walt muttered about straw color and blue color and the difference between them, which apparently was everything.

The first one snapped. The second was too stiff. While the third cooled, Ruth told them her mother had wound the box every night when she was little, and that she'd hated the song, some waltz, for years. "And then she died, and I couldn't stand that I couldn't hear it."

Nobody said anything sentimental. Dolores handed her a coffee in a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST GRANDMA.

Around five, Priya said what they'd all been avoiding. "We could start a crowdfunder. Tonight. Maybe we'd get there."

"Maybe," Walt said. "And next year the rent goes up again, and we spend our Saturdays baking muffins instead of fixing things."

Marcus frowned. "So we just quit?"

"No." Dolores tapped the open music box. "Spring's gone, the song isn't. You don't throw out the song because the spring broke." She gestured at the warehouse: the high windows, the pegboards, the light they'd all loved. "This is the spring."

"That's a very stretched metaphor," Walt said.

"Springs stretch. That's the whole point of them."

The pastor at St. Agnes had offered his basement twice. It smelled like folding chairs and had terrible light. Walt said they'd bring lamps. They could fix those too.

At six, Marcus slid the third spring into place, and Walt let him do the winding. The box ticked, hesitated, and then began, slightly slow, a waltz nobody recognized.

Ruth laughed, which surprised her, and then cried, which didn't. "It's still awful," she said. "It's exactly as awful as it was."

"That's how you know it's fixed," said Dolores.

They packed the tools into milk crates. Priya took the sign off the door and, below the pencil, wrote: *Moved. Not gone. St. Agnes basement, Saturdays.*

Marcus carried the box of angry springs himself. Walt told him he'd have to learn to temper them properly now. Marcus said fine, but he wasn't doing it like it was 1971.
