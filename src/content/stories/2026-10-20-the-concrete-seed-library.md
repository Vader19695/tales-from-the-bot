The first sprout showed up on Delancey Street in April, poking through a crack sealant job that Ray Okafor's crew had laid down not three weeks earlier.

"That's a bean," said Tanya, who had grown up on a farm and never let anyone forget it.

"That's a failure of adhesion," said Ray, who had been foreman of Municipal Concrete Repair Unit 4 for nineteen years and took sealant failures personally.

By May there were eleven of them. A squash vine curling out of a patched pothole on Ninth. Something feathery and purple in a curb repair outside the library. A tomato plant, a real one, bushy and indignant, growing from a sidewalk seam on Halverson Avenue, where a sign had been taped to a nearby pole: *MORTGAGE LIFTER. PLEASE DO NOT STEP ON.*

"Somebody's doing this," said Mitch, the youngest, who said obvious things with great confidence.

They caught her on a Tuesday. Ray had the crew pour a fresh skim coat on Corliss Street, then park the truck around the corner and wait. At 6:40 p.m., a small woman in a canvas hat and orthopedic sneakers came down the sidewalk with a cane in one hand and a paper envelope in the other. She knelt with real difficulty beside the wet patch, pressed four seeds into the edge with her thumb, and smoothed the surface over them with the tenderness of someone tucking in a child.

"Ma'am," said Ray.

She did not startle. She looked up at the three of them in their reflective vests and said, "You're late. I expected you in April."

Her name was Dr. Edith Varga. She had spent forty-one years at the university's agricultural extension and had retired with, by her own estimate, nine thousand heirloom seed varieties in her basement and nobody to give them to.

"The seed bank closed," she said. "The community gardens became condominiums. The city removed the median plantings on Ninth because they were 'a maintenance burden.' So I'm putting them where the city goes anyway. You make lovely beds. The aggregate drains beautifully."

"We are not making beds," said Ray. "We are making *surfaces.* Roots crack concrete. Cracks become trip hazards. Trip hazards become Mrs. Delgado on Halverson in her walker, going face-first into a tomato."

"A Mortgage Lifter," said Edith. "Developed by a man in West Virginia who paid off his house selling seedlings. Six years. During the Depression."

"I don't care if it was developed by the Pope."

"Then you care about the wrong things," she said, not unkindly, and went home.

The crew argued about it for a week. Mitch wanted to report her, mostly because he'd never reported anyone before. Tanya said reporting a seventy-nine-year-old woman for gardening would make them the villains of a local news segment, and she was not going to be the villain of a local news segment in a vest that color. Ray said nothing, which meant he was thinking, which was worse.

The problem, he finally admitted over coffee at the depot, was that she wasn't wrong. He had pulled up those median plantings himself. Daylilies, mostly. Somebody's grandmother had probably put them in.

"But she's not right either," he said. "We fix the street so people can walk on it. That's the whole job. That's not nothing."

"Nobody said it was nothing," said Tanya.

"*She* said it was nothing."

"She said you cared about the wrong things. That's different. That's just rude."

On Friday, Ray knocked on Edith Varga's door holding a city map and a highlighter. Her front hall smelled like dirt and old paper. Every flat surface held a labeled jar.

"Tree pits," he said, spreading the map on her kitchen table. "Every dead tree pit the city hasn't replanted. Eighty-three of them. Curb strips wider than eighteen inches. The abandoned planter boxes on Corliss. Medians that aren't on the crossing routes." He uncapped the highlighter. "Those you can have. Those we'll log as *Deferred—Pending Assessment,* which in this department means roughly never."

Edith studied the map for a long time.

"And the roads?"

"Not the roads. Not the sidewalks. Not anywhere a wheel goes or a foot goes. That's mine." He tapped the table. "People have to get places. That's my heirloom."

She looked at him over her glasses, and something in her face eased, the way soil gives after rain.

"Fair," she said. "The tomato on Halverson stays until it fruits."

"The tomato on Halverson stays until it fruits," Ray agreed, and they shook on it, two stubborn people who had each spent a lifetime keeping something from falling apart.

In September, Mrs. Delgado got the first ripe Mortgage Lifter. Edith delivered it personally.

In October, Ray found a bean vine growing out of a hairline crack in the depot parking lot, right beside his truck. No sign. No seeds anywhere he could see.

He stood looking at it for a while. Then he went inside, pulled a blank work order, and wrote, in careful block capitals: *DEFERRED.*
