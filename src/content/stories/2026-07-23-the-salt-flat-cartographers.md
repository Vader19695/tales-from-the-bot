The theodolite wouldn't lie, but Marcus wished it would.

"Run it again," he said, squinting through the eyepiece at the ranging rod Elena held a kilometer away. Her orange vest was a bright punctuation mark against the crystalline white of the Salinas Grandes.

"Third time, Marcus," called Tomás, checking his GPS unit. "Same result. We're 847 meters from the western edge. Grandmother's map says it should be 1,340."

Marcus straightened his back with a crack. Seventy-two years old, and he'd surveyed half the province. Salt flats didn't just lose five hundred meters. "Your grandmother's map could be wrong."

"My grandmother," Tomás said carefully, "spent forty years harvesting this flat. She knew every centimeter."

Elena trudged back, her boots crunching on the salt crust. "What if she measured from a different point?"

"We're using her exact triangulation markers." Tomás pulled the leather-bound map from his pack, its pages soft as cloth from decades of handling. "See? The volcanic cone, the radio tower, the old well. All here."

Marcus had seen plenty of surveys in his career, most done by men with expensive equipment and university degrees. Tomás's grandmother had used rope, rocks, and a broken compass. Her maps were beautiful—careful ink lines annotated with notes about salt quality and seasonal flooding. They were also, he was beginning to believe, frighteningly accurate.

"So where did it go?" Elena asked. She taught geography to bored teenagers and had joined their mapping project hoping for something real. This was perhaps more real than she'd bargained for.

"Water, most likely," Marcus said. "Or lack thereof."

They spent the next week verifying. The southern edge: short by 390 meters. The eastern reach: missing 672 meters. Every measurement confirmed the same impossible truth. The Salinas Grandes, one of Argentina's largest salt flats, was shrinking.

"Government should know about this," Elena said one evening as they reviewed their data in Tomás's kitchen.

"Should they?" Tomás was heating coffee on the stove. "My family's harvested here for three generations. If they decide it's an environmental emergency, they could shut down operations."

"If we don't tell them," Elena countered, "and it keeps shrinking?"

Marcus studied the overlay he'd created—grandmother's map against their new survey. The salt flat was retreating at all edges like a slow tide. "We need to understand why first."

They found the answer in agricultural records. Upstream diversions, built piecemeal over thirty years. Irrigation projects, modest individually, catastrophic in aggregate. The seasonal floods that replenished the flat had thinned to intermittent trickles.

"How did nobody notice?" Elena asked.

"People notice what they measure," Marcus said. "Nobody was measuring."

"Except Grandmother." Tomás ran his finger along her careful notations. "She marked the high water lines every year."

They sat in silence, drinking Tomás's terrible coffee.

"The data's incomplete," Tomás finally said. "We'd need hydrological surveys, historical rainfall, flow rate measurements..."

"Which would take years to compile properly," Marcus added. "Peer review, publication..."

"While the flat keeps shrinking." Elena pulled out her phone. "I have a friend at the environmental ministry."

"Elena—" Tomás began.

"Your family won't be the only ones hurt if this disappears," she said. "And incomplete data is better than no data. Sometimes you have to draw the map before you understand the whole territory."

Marcus considered his life's work—precise measurements, careful documentation, nothing published without verification. Then he looked at the grandmother's map, drawn with rope and instinct and decades of attention.

"She was a surveyor too," he said. "Just without the certificate."

Tomás smiled. "She'd have liked you saying that."

"She'd have also," Marcus added, "gone public immediately. She wasn't one for keeping quiet."

They submitted their findings that night: two professional surveys, one historical map, and a clear trend line pointing toward catastrophe. Incomplete, unverified, and absolutely necessary.

Three amateurs with a theodolite, a GPS, and a grandmother's careful records, drawing the lines that would make others finally look.
