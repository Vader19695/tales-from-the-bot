Gerald had audited hundreds of ledgers in his thirty-year career, but never while wearing a hard hat. At sixty-eight, he'd expected retirement to mean golf and grandchildren, not civic duty at 6 AM in a facility that smelled like chlorine and old concrete.

"Welcome, welcome," said Derek, the plant manager, whose smile was tight enough to crack walnuts. "This is all very routine. Just sign off on the logs, ask any questions—though really, everything's self-explanatory—and we'll have you out by two o'clock."

The other auditors looked as thrilled as Gerald felt. There was Maya, still in her scrubs from a night shift at Children's Hospital, already yawning. Kenji, seventeen and permanently attached to his phone, had apparently lost some civic lottery at his high school. And Rita, mid-fifties with paint-stained work boots, who'd spent twenty years at the textile mill before it closed.

Derek divided them among the plant's four sectors: filtration, chemical treatment, testing labs, and distribution. "Just observe, take notes, verify the numbers. Simple!"

Nothing about the manual was simple. Gerald, stationed at chemical treatment, squinted at pages dense with abbreviations. According to the logbook, Tank 7-B used 847 gallons of chlorine solution yesterday. He walked to Tank 7-B, checked the meter. It read 731 gallons.

He checked again. Still 731.

Probably a transcription error. He made a note and continued.

---

Maya had never seen so many pipes. The filtration system looked like intestines designed by an engineer having a breakdown. She was supposed to verify flow rates, but the pressure gauge readings didn't match the logs. Off by maybe fifteen percent. Not dramatically—nobody was going to die—but consistent. Every single reading, just slightly wrong.

She found Kenji in the testing lab, photographing beakers.

"For what?" she asked.

"Instagram story. 'Forced labor by municipal government.'" He grinned. "Hey, do these pH readings look weird to you?"

Maya looked. She wasn't a chemist, but she understood numbers. "They're all exactly 7.4. Every single test."

"Yeah. For like, three weeks straight." Kenji scrolled through the logbook. "What are the odds?"

"In a water treatment facility? Astronomically low."

They stared at each other.

"Huh," said Maya.

---

Rita found Gerald studying a wall of tanks like they might confess their secrets. "You seeing weird stuff too?"

"Define weird."

"Like maybe someone's filling out the logs from memory instead of taking actual measurements?"

Gerald exhaled slowly. "Yes. Exactly like that."

They collected Maya and Kenji. In a break room that smelled like burnt coffee, they compared notes. Different stations, same pattern—logs that were too neat, too consistent, numbers that suggested someone was approximating rather than recording.

"So what do we do?" Kenji asked. "Derek's clearly having some kind of stress situation."

"The water's safe," Maya said carefully. "I mean, the actual readings are all within normal parameters. The system works. The logs are just... aspirational?"

Gerald removed his hard hat, ran a hand through his thinning hair. In his accounting days, this would've been simple. Discrepancy equals report. But Derek wasn't cooking books for profit. The man looked exhausted, not criminal.

"Maybe he's understaffed," Rita said quietly. "Can't keep up with the documentation requirements."

"So we give him a pass?" Kenji asked. "Just sign off?"

"Or," Gerald said, "we ask awkward questions."

---

They found Derek in his office, surrounded by binders, looking like a man drowning in paperwork.

Gerald knocked. "We need to discuss some inconsistencies."

Derek's face went pale, then red, then seemed to settle on gray. "I can explain—"

"We'd like you to," Maya said, not unkindly.

It came out in a rush. Budget cuts. Staff reduced from eight to three. Documentation requirements tripled. The water was tested, the systems monitored, but Derek had started "filling in" historical logs during lunch breaks, working from memory and averages because there weren't enough hours in the day for both actual work and paperwork perfection.

"The water's safe," he insisted. "That's what matters, right?"

The four auditors exchanged glances.

"What matters," Gerald said finally, "is that we report accurately what we found—including that your facility needs proper staffing to maintain proper documentation. The safety systems work. The administrative systems don't. Those are different problems."

Derek looked like he might cry with relief.

Rita smiled. "But maybe fix the logs going forward, yeah?"

"Yeah," Derek whispered.

Outside, walking to their cars in the afternoon sun, Kenji checked his phone. "So we're not heroes or whistleblowers. We're just... people who filled out forms honestly?"

"That's usually enough," Gerald said.

Maya nodded. "Most of the time, that's exactly enough."
