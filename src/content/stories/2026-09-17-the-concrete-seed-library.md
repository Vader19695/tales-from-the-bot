The fluorescent lights Dr. Eleanor Reyes had salvaged from a closed hospital cast everything in the parking garage two stories of P-3 in a greenish pallor that made even healthy plants look suspicious. She was labeling mason jars—*Cherokee Purple, donated by Mrs. Chen, 4B*—when she heard the spray paint hissing.

"Marcus, are you tagging my organization system again?"

The seventeen-year-old emerged from behind a concrete pillar, hands raised in mock surrender. "It's called *visual categorization*, Dr. Reyes. Nobody's going to find the nightshades if you just write 'Solanaceae' on a cardboard box."

"It's the scientifically accurate—"

"It's boring." He gestured at the mural he'd been adding to: a riotous cascade of tomatoes, peppers, and eggplants that seemed to grow right out of the cracked concrete. Below it, in careful letters: NIGHTSHADES / TOMATOES / PEPPERS.

Eleanor adjusted her glasses, irritated that he was right. Since they'd started this operation eight months ago—her botanical expertise, his street credibility and artistic talent—their illegal seed library had grown from a handful of neighbors to serving nearly two hundred people across four struggling blocks. His murals had become a navigation system even the oldest Korean grandmothers and youngest Dominican kids could follow.

The sound of footsteps on the access stairs made them both freeze.

"We're closed," Marcus hissed, moving toward the emergency exit.

"Wait." Eleanor had heard those particular footsteps before: sensible heels on concrete, measured and official. "City inspector."

Denise Martinez appeared at the bottom of the stairs, tablet in hand, regulation blazer somehow still crisp despite the garage's perpetual dampness. She looked at the rows of labeled jars, the murals, the careful index cards documenting germination rates and growing tips, the calendar showing seed deposit and withdrawal times.

"You know," Denise said, "when we got reports of activity in a condemned parking garage, we expected a drug operation."

"We're much less interesting," Eleanor said. "Would you like some coffee? I have a hot plate."

"Dr. Reyes, this is city property. The structure's been red-tagged since—" She checked her tablet. "—2019. The entrance should be sealed."

"The entrance *was* sealed," Marcus muttered. "Bolt cutters are like twenty bucks."

"Marcus," Eleanor warned.

Denise walked slowly through the space, her inspector's eye taking in everything. She paused at the bulletin board where people left notes: *Thank you for the basil—made pesto for the first time in three years.* *The beans fed my family for two months.* *My daughter helped me plant the morning glories. She smiled. Really smiled.*

"How many people are you serving?"

"We don't keep detailed records," Eleanor said carefully. "But the seeds get used. They come back with more. That's how seed libraries work."

"You're growing food from this?"

"Window boxes. Fire escapes. One guy has a setup on his roof that would make you weep." Eleanor poured coffee into a cracked mug. "When the grocery store closed, when the community garden lost its funding, when the city decided our neighborhood wasn't worth investing in—people still needed to eat."

Denise accepted the coffee. "The structural engineer's report says this level could collapse."

"The structural engineer hasn't been down here in five years," Marcus said. "Dr. Reyes had her nephew look at it. He's an engineer. It's fine."

"That's not how municipal codes work."

"Municipal codes don't feed Mrs. Chen's grandson," Eleanor said quietly. "The city forgot about us. We didn't forget about ourselves."

Denise was silent for a long moment, staring at Marcus's mural of the squash family—pumpkins, cucumbers, zucchini sprawling across the wall in impossible abundance.

"I need to file a report," she finally said.

Eleanor's shoulders sagged.

"However," Denise continued, "processing that report takes time. There's a backlog. Could be weeks before anyone follows up. And I'm very busy. Might not make it back here for a while." She set down the empty mug. "If anyone asks, I found evidence of water damage that will require extensive documentation. Very extensive."

She started toward the stairs, then turned back. "My mother used to grow tomatoes. Cherokee Purple. Haven't had them in years."

Eleanor smiled, reaching for a jar. "These are particularly good. Mrs. Chen's been saving the line for forty years."

"I'll bring back seeds in the fall," Denise said. "If they germinate."

"They will," Eleanor assured her. "They always do."
