The jackhammer sat between them like a loaded gun nobody wanted to touch.

"According to the work order," said Marcus, adjusting his reading glasses to examine the clipboard, "we're supposed to break up sections A through D by lunch." He'd spent thirty years engineering water treatment systems before taking this municipal job in retirement. He liked the predictability of it.

"According to my stomach," said Lou, "we're supposed to take our break in twenty minutes." The former chef had pivoted to public works after his restaurant folded during the pandemic. He still kept the same schedule in his head—prep, service, cleanup.

The teenager, Jade, said nothing. She was here because a judge had suggested fifty hours of community service might teach her something about respecting public property. The irony wasn't lost on her.

Marcus fired up the jackhammer at 7:47 AM exactly. The parking lot behind the old rec center had been on the repair list for eighteen months. The asphalt was a moonscape of cracks and potholes. He broke through the surface on his third strike.

That's when Lou saw it.

"Hold up." Lou crouched down, squinting at the hole. "Marcus. Kill it."

The jackhammer died. In the sudden silence, they could hear morning traffic two blocks over.

"What am I looking at?" Marcus asked.

"Roots." Lou touched the exposed earth. "Big ones. Moving horizontal, maybe three inches down. This is a network."

Jade wandered over, curious despite herself. The roots were thick as her wrist, pale and fibrous, spreading in every direction beneath the pavement like veins. "So?"

"So," Lou said slowly, "Mrs. Patterson's tomatoes are thirty feet that way. The community garden plots are all along this block. If this is what I think it is..."

Marcus was already walking the perimeter, examining the cracks with new eyes. "The breaking pattern. It's not random. The roots are following the moisture gradient toward the gardens. They've probably been developing for years."

"Mycorrhizal network," Lou said. "Like I had under my restaurant's herb garden. The fungus connects everything underground. Shares nutrients, water, information even. Scientists call it the wood-wide web."

"That's actually kind of cool," Jade admitted.

Marcus returned to the work order. "Doesn't matter. We have our instructions. The parking lot is a liability. Three twisted ankles last year alone."

"Those gardens feed forty families," Lou said. "You destroy this network, you kill everything above ground. I've seen it happen. Takes years to rebuild."

"We're not ecologists. We're maintenance workers."

"We're neighbors," Lou countered.

They stood there, three people who'd been thrown together by circumstance, staring at a hole in the ground that had suddenly become a decision point.

Jade surprised them both by speaking up. "What about sections B and C only?"

Marcus frowned. "What?"

She pointed. "The worst damage is in those middle sections. Leave A and D intact—that's where the root density looks heaviest. You get a functional parking lot. The network stays connected."

"That's not the full job," Marcus said.

"It's the smart job," Lou replied. "And you're the one who taught me last week that engineering is about solving problems, not following instructions blindly."

Marcus looked at his clipboard, then at the exposed roots, then at the community gardens visible through the chain-link fence. Mrs. Patterson was already out there, checking on her tomatoes.

He sighed. "We'll need to mark the preserved sections with caution tape. Make it look intentional. I'll file an amended plan citing subsurface instability requiring revised scope."

"That's the most beautiful bureaucratic bullshit I've ever heard," Lou said.

By 11:30, they'd completed sections B and C. The parking lot was functional. The roots remained intact. Marcus had drafted a report that was technically accurate and thoroughly misleading.

As they loaded the truck, Jade asked, "Does this count toward my hours?"

"Kid," Lou said, "this counts toward a lot more than that."

Marcus started the engine. The jackhammer sat in the truck bed, still warm from use. Above them, the community gardens caught the late morning sun, roots deep and thriving, connected in ways the surface would never show.
