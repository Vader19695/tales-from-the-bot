The radiator in the old administrative office had been clanking for forty years, and now, finally, someone was going to fix it.

Margaret stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, watching the maintenance worker wrestle with his wrench. "About time," she said to no one in particular.

"Place is being cleared out next month anyway," said Dennis, who'd appeared beside her with two cups of coffee from the vending machine. He handed her one. "They're tearing down the whole depot."

"I know that, Dennis. I can read."

"Just saying."

They'd both retired from the municipal bus system fifteen years ago—Margaret as a dispatcher, Dennis as a route supervisor—but they still showed up for the monthly retirees' meetings. Old habits, old friends, old coffee that tasted like brake fluid.

The maintenance worker grunted, and the radiator lurched forward from the wall with a rusty screech.

"Well, would you look at that," he said, reaching into the gap behind it.

What he pulled out was a cloth-wrapped bundle, flat and rectangular. He handed it to Margaret without ceremony and went back to his pipes.

Margaret unwrapped it carefully. Inside was a ledger, its green cover water-stained and warped. She opened it to the first page, and her breath caught.

*Property of Arthur Chen, 1978-1983*

"Dennis," she said quietly. "Go find Lorraine."

---

Twenty minutes later, the three of them sat around the break room table, the ledger open before them like a holy text.

Lorraine adjusted her glasses and read aloud: "March 14, 1979. Driver J. Williams let elderly woman ride free when she couldn't find her senior pass. Gave her his lunch when she mentioned not eating. Williams route 7."

"Jerry Williams," Margaret said, smiling. "He was a softie."

Dennis turned a page. "Listen to this one. 'June 2, 1980. Dispatcher M. Rodriguez delayed route to allow driver to return lost child to frantic mother at Elm Street stop. Child had boarded wrong bus. Official report listed delay as mechanical.'"

"That was you, Margaret," Lorraine said, peering at her. "M. Rodriguez."

"I remember that little girl," Margaret said softly. "Pigtails. Couldn't have been more than six."

They read on. Page after page, Arthur Chen—dead from a heart attack at his desk in 1983—had documented kindnesses. Drivers who'd helped passengers with groceries. Dispatchers who'd bent rules to get people home during storms. Mechanics who'd slipped extra blankets to homeless folks sleeping in the station.

"Arthur never said a word about this," Lorraine murmured. "Just watched us all and wrote it down."

"He was a strange bird," Dennis said, but his voice was fond. "Quiet. Always had that little smile, like he knew something you didn't."

"Turns out he did," Margaret said.

She flipped to the last entry, dated two days before Arthur's death: *"This place runs on more than schedules and fuel. It runs on small mercies that no one records. But I have. Forty-seven drivers. Nineteen dispatchers. Twelve mechanics. Thousands of kindnesses. This is the real ledger of Bus Depot 7."*

They sat in silence for a long moment.

"So what do we do with it?" Lorraine finally asked. "The historical society wants documents before they demolish the building. This would... well, it would change the story, wouldn't it?"

"The official history says we ran a tight ship," Dennis said. "On time, by the book. Which we did."

"But that's not the whole truth," Margaret said.

"No," Lorraine agreed. "It's not."

Dennis drummed his fingers on the table. "My grandson asked me last month what I did for forty years. I told him I made buses run on time. Made it sound like I was some kind of robot."

"Were you in here?" Lorraine asked, turning pages. "Did Arthur write about you?"

Dennis took the ledger, running his finger down the entries until he stopped. His face softened. "Christmas Eve, 1981. Supervisor D. Patterson arranged emergency route to hospital for woman in labor when ambulance was delayed in snow."

"I remember her," he said quietly. "She named the baby Dennis."

Margaret laughed, her eyes wet. "Of course she did."

"We give it to them," Lorraine said firmly. "To the historical society. Arthur kept this record for a reason. Maybe he knew someday we'd need reminding that we weren't just moving bodies from point A to point B."

"We were moving *people*," Margaret said.

"We were people ourselves," Dennis added.

They wrapped the ledger carefully, reverently. Behind them, the radiator clanked one last time, as if satisfied with finally giving up its secret.
