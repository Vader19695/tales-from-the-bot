The survey drone died at Junction 14, which was how Mira Okonkwo learned that the Calder Street cistern had better radio interference than the city's official emergency network.

She found the source by walking. Two hundred meters of brick vaulting, ankle-deep water the temperature of a grudge, and then light: a warm, yellow, entirely unauthorized light spilling from an alcove where the 1891 engineers had once kept their pumping ledgers.

Inside, a woman in her seventies sat before a wall of transceivers, drinking tea from a thermos and saying into a microphone, "That's affirmative, Bernard, the pharmacy on Wren closes early Thursdays. Tell your sister."

She looked up at Mira. She looked at Mira's hard hat, the reflective vest, the tablet stamped CITY OF HALLOWAY — INFRASTRUCTURE RENEWAL. Then she said into the microphone, "Stand by, everyone. We have a visitor from the government," and offered Mira a biscuit.

---

Her name was Agnes, call sign KC2-WREN, inherited from a man named Teodor who had inherited it from his brother. The station had operated for thirty-one years. It had begun in the winter of the Long Flood, when the official alert system failed for nine days and the only reason the Eastside knew which bridges were still standing was a retired telegraph operator with a car battery and a strong opinion about bureaucracy.

"He set up down here because the walls shield the signal from municipal detection," Agnes explained. "Also because nobody from the city had been down here since 1962. Present company excepted."

"I'm mapping the tunnels," Mira said. "For the modernization project. They're putting in fiber, sensors, a new drainage spine." She paused. "This room is on the route."

"Ah," said Agnes, and poured her more tea, the way you might for someone who has just announced a terminal diagnosis.

---

Mira stayed four hours. She told herself it was documentation.

She listened to the evening net. A night-shift nurse checked in to say the 40 bus had been rerouted again. A boy of maybe twelve, call sign inherited from his grandfather, read out river levels from a gauge he maintained himself, with the gravity of a cardinal. Someone named Fitz reported a lost beagle. Someone else reported finding a beagle. They were not the same beagle. This was discussed at length.

Between check-ins, Agnes showed her the logbooks: three decades of handwriting, the frequencies penciled in margins like family recipes. *Use 146.52 when the rain's heavy. Teodor says never trust the repeater on Mill Hill. Grandma's call sign goes to Sam when he passes his test.*

"Why not just do this legally?" Mira asked. "Get licensed, register the station, apply for a permit—"

"We applied," Agnes said. "In 1997. And 2004. And 2015. They lost the forms twice and the third time told us the Eastside was adequately served." She smiled without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "The city is very good at deciding what it has already done."

---

Mira didn't sleep that night. She drafted the report three times.

Version one: *Unlicensed transmission equipment discovered at Junction 14. Recommend removal and referral to the Communications Authority.* Accurate. Defensible. Promotion-shaped.

Version two: *Junction 14: no significant findings.* A lie with her name under it, her professional stamp, the oath she'd taken about public safety. If the vault ever flooded with someone inside, that sentence would be read aloud in a courtroom.

She thought about her father, who had taught her that infrastructure was a promise a city makes to people it will never meet. She thought about how the promise had been broken here, quietly, three times, on forms nobody read. And she thought about the boy with the river gauge, reading levels to an audience of strangers like it was the most important job in the world, because for some of them it was.

Version three took until four in the morning.

---

The modernization plan approved in March included a minor rerouting of the drainage spine around Junction 14, citing "historic brickwork of structural significance." It also included, buried on page 211, a recommendation that the city "formally partner with existing community emergency communication volunteers in the Eastside district," with a contact address that was technically a post office box and practically a biscuit tin.

It was not a lie, exactly. It was not the truth, either. It was the kind of document that would hold if no one pulled on it, and Mira would be the one standing there if someone did. She found she could live with that. Belonging, it turned out, had a price, and the price was being willing to be blamed.

On a Thursday in April, she went down with a thermos of her own.

Agnes handed her the microphone and a slip of paper. A call sign, written in Teodor's careful hand, unused since 2009.

"Fitz found another beagle," Agnes said. "Go on. Tell them you're on the net."

Mira pressed the key.
