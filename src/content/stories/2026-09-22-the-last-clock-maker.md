The first night Mara found the girl in her workshop, she nearly called the peacekeepers. The teenager crouched beside the grandfather clock in the corner, her fingers tracing the frozen hands, breath fogging in the cold air above the shuttered station.

"Don't," the girl whispered, though Mara hadn't moved. "Please. I just want to understand."

Mara's hand hovered over the emergency bell—a relic from before, when emergencies could be timed, measured, rushed toward. "You shouldn't be here."

"I know." The girl stood, thin as a rail spike. "My name is Juni. I've been coming for three weeks."

Three weeks. Mara felt something crumble inside her chest. She'd been so careful for fifty years, yet careless enough not to notice an intruder. Age was making her as unmeasured as everything else in Stillwater.

"Why?" Mara asked.

Juni's eyes shone in the lamplight. "My grandmother died last winter. Or was it spring? No one could tell me. We buried her when the ground thawed, but I don't know how long she lay there, waiting. I don't know how old she was. The stories about her age contradicted each other by years."

Mara knew this pain. It was why she'd kept the clocks alive, hidden, ticking in their silence. Why she still wound them in the dark, maintaining springs and escapements for mechanisms that served no purpose.

"Time doesn't stop existing just because we stop counting it," Juni said.

"That's what they were afraid of," Mara replied. "That we'd become slaves to it again. The factory whistles, the train schedules, everyone rushing, always late, always behind. The founders wanted us free."

"But we're not free." Juni moved closer, and Mara saw the desperation in her young face. "We're just lost. My mother talks about things happening 'a while ago' or 'recently,' but she means the same span. My father can't remember how long he's been building our house. Some say two years, some say seven."

Mara had stopped arguing these points with herself decades ago. She'd simply kept winding, kept maintaining, kept the knowledge alive in her hands if nowhere else.

"If I teach you," Mara said slowly, "you'll become like me. Separate. You'll know something that sets you apart from everyone you love."

"I'm already separate." Juni touched the grandfather clock again. "I count my heartbeats at night. I watch the sun and try to divide the day. Everyone else seems content to drift, but I feel like I'm drowning."

Mara studied the girl's face and saw her younger self—the apprentice who'd refused to destroy her master's tools when the Timeless Act passed, who'd hidden springs and gears in her skirts and climbed to this abandoned workshop above the station where trains no longer ran because who could keep a schedule without time?

"A clock," Mara said, "is just a wheel turning. Gears catching gears, each motion predictable, measurable. It doesn't create time—it only reveals what's already flowing past us."

She walked to her workbench, covered in cloth. With careful hands, she pulled the fabric away, revealing thirty pocket watches in various states of repair.

Juni gasped.

"The question isn't whether to measure time," Mara continued. "The question is whether to measure it together or alone. The founders thought time made us slaves, but they created a town of ghosts instead—people who can't mourn properly because they don't know how long someone lived, who can't celebrate anniversaries, who forget their children's ages."

She lifted a pocket watch, its case silver and warm. "I've kept these alive because I believed someday we'd need them again. That the town would remember what it lost."

"Teach me," Juni breathed.

Mara held out the watch. When Juni took it, Mara didn't let go immediately.

"If we do this," she said, "we'll have to teach others. We'll have to give Stillwater back its hours. Are you ready for that responsibility?"

Juni's fingers closed around the watch, and through the metal, Mara felt the girl's pulse—steady, rhythmic, insistent as any mechanism.

"Yes," Juni said.

Outside, the sun rose or fell—Mara wasn't certain which. But in the workshop, time was suddenly no longer something to hide. It was something, finally, to share.
