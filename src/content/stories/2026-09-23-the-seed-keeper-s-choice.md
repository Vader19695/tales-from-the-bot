Margaret had been volunteering at the Riverside Community Seed Bank for three years, and in that time, the most exciting thing that had happened was when someone donated a collection of Cherokee Purple tomato seeds in a vintage tobacco tin. So when she descended the cistern stairs for the monthly inventory check and found half the storage jars writhing with pale, desperate sprouts, she sat down hard on the bottom step and laughed until she cried.

It was impossible. The cistern maintained a steady fifty-two degrees—cool enough to keep seeds dormant for decades. The jars were sealed. The humidity was controlled. And yet here they were: Mortgage Lifter tomatoes, Moon and Stars watermelons, Scarlet Runner beans, all sending out ghostly white tendrils in the darkness, pressing against glass like prisoners who'd finally had enough.

Margaret pulled out her phone to call Diane, the board president, then stopped. Diane would call an emergency meeting. The board would panic. They'd bring in experts who'd condemn the facility, relocate the collection, probably install expensive new climate controls funded by a grant that would take six months to write. By then, these seeds—some of them eighty, ninety, a hundred years old—would be compost.

She looked at the jars. The seeds looked back, in their way.

"Well, hell," Margaret said.

---

By nightfall, she'd smuggled forty-seven jars into her garage, wrapped in her winter coat like botanical refugees. She spent the evening on the neighborhood forum she usually avoided, crafting a message that she revised seventeen times before posting.

*"FREE heirloom vegetable seedlings to good homes. Rare varieties, excellent for experienced gardeners. Must pick up this weekend. First come, first served."*

The responses came immediately. Marcus from three blocks over wanted the watermelons—his grandfather had grown them in Mississippi. Elena, the teacher who always had dirt under her fingernails, claimed the tomatoes. The Nguyen family, who'd transformed their entire front yard into a vegetable garden that made the HOA apoplectic, took the beans.

Saturday became a parade. Margaret had planted the sprouted seeds in every container she could find—yogurt cups, coffee cans, a cracked terra cotta pot her ex-husband had left behind. She handed them out with care instructions printed on index cards, feeling like she was running an underground railroad for vegetables.

"The Brandywine tomatoes are temperamental," she told Elena. "They'll break your heart if you let them."

"All the best ones do," Elena replied, cradling the seedlings like infants.

By Sunday afternoon, Margaret had placed the last of them—a jar of Dragon Tongue beans—with Mr. Kowalski, who was ninety-three and still gardened in raised beds he could reach from his wheelchair.

"These are older than me," he said, examining the sprouted beans with something like reverence.

"Almost," Margaret agreed. "They've been waiting a long time."

"Haven't we all," Mr. Kowalski said.

---

Monday morning, she returned to the cistern to document what remained. She'd expected to feel guilty, maybe scared of discovery. Instead, she felt lighter than she had in years.

The remaining jars sat in perfect dormancy—corn, squash, flowers that would sleep for another season or another decade. She photographed the empty spaces where the germinated jars had been, noting the varieties, the dates, the donors long dead whose gardens would bloom again in yards across Riverside.

She'd write it up as a mysterious partial failure, cause unknown. Diane would furrow her brow. The board would schedule a facilities review that would find nothing wrong. Life would continue.

But in July, Marcus would bring her a Moon and Stars watermelon the size of a basketball. Elena's students would eat Brandywine tomatoes still warm from the sun. The Nguyens would save seeds, and those seeds would carry forward, the way they were always meant to.

Margaret climbed the cistern stairs into the light, already composing the email she wouldn't send, smiling at the secret she'd keep. Some things, she'd learned, were too precious to save. They were meant to grow.
