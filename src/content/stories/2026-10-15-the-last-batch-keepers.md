Odalys Reyes unlocked the gate at 6:40, as she had every Tuesday for three years. She knew before the fluorescents finished flickering that something was wrong. The air on the old platform smelled green.

Not damp, not moldy. Green, like a cut lawn, like spring forcing its way through concrete.

She found Marguerite already inside Vault B, standing very still in her parka, one gloved hand pressed to her mouth. The shelving stretched away under the curved tile ceiling where trains had once roared, rows of sealed foil packets and glass jars, each labeled in Marguerite's precise script. Half of them had split open.

Pale shoots curled from the seams. Roots threaded through the wire racks like white embroidery. A jar of 1962 sorghum had burst its lid, and the seedlings inside stood two inches tall, cotyledons unfolded as if they had been waiting for this morning their whole lives.

"The vault is at minus eighteen," Marguerite said. Her voice was steady in the way it got when she was frightened. "It has been at minus eighteen for thirty-one years."

Dev arrived twenty minutes later with three coffees, saw the racks, and set the cardboard tray down on the floor without a word. He was the youngest of them, a doctoral student who volunteered because the university's greenhouse wouldn't let him touch anything older than he was. He crouched by the lowest shelf and touched a radicle with his fingertip, gently, like a pulse.

"This isn't possible," he said. "Even if the compressor failed, you'd need water. Days of warmth. These look like they've had a week."

"The compressor didn't fail." Odalys had already pulled the temperature log on the tablet bolted to the wall. A flat blue line, unbroken. "Minus eighteen all night. Humidity at four percent."

They stood together in the cold, breath fogging, and listened to the hum of the machinery and something underneath it—a faint, low vibration in the tile that Odalys, who had spent twenty-two years on the transit maintenance crews before her knees gave out, knew at once.

"That's the Line 9 extension," she said. "They started boring under Halloran last week. Two hundred feet down."

Dev looked up sharply. "Vibration as a germination cue? There's research on sound frequencies, but nothing like..." He gestured at the racks, the impossible meadow of them. "Nothing like *this*."

Marguerite had moved down the aisle, reading labels. "It isn't random," she said. "Look. The Andean quinoa. The Sahel millet. The barley from the Lebanese collection. Everything that sprouted is a drought variety. Everything from a hard place."

"And the rest?"

"Asleep. Untouched."

Odalys felt the hair rise on her arms, and not from the cold.

They retreated to the old ticket booth, which they used as an office, and closed the door. On the desk sat the binder of protocols from the Municipal Parks Department. Page four, in bold: *Any compromise of collection integrity must be reported to the Department within 24 hours.*

"If we report it," Dev said, "they'll send an inspector. The inspector will see contamination. They'll incinerate the affected stock and probably shut us down pending review. Nobody downtown will care *why*."

"And if we don't," Marguerite said, "we are three volunteers hiding a biological anomaly in a public facility underneath a construction site. If this is something in the soil, the water, something that spreads—"

"It's not spreading. You said so. It chose."

"Seeds don't choose, Dev."

But she didn't sound sure.

Odalys looked through the booth's scratched glass toward Vault B, where the pale shoots were already, unmistakably, turning. Not toward the lights. Toward the eastern wall, toward the sealed tunnel mouth, toward the deep and patient rumble of the drill.

"These came to us from people who were losing their farms," she said quietly. "Marguerite, you told me that the first week. That's what this place is. A promise that somebody keeps the thing alive until it's needed." She tapped the binder. "Maybe it's needed."

The silence stretched. The vibration hummed in the soles of their boots.

At last Marguerite took off her glove and uncapped her pen. "Twenty-four hours," she said. "That's what the protocol allows. We use every minute of it. We photograph everything, sample everything, record the vibration frequency. We pot what we can save, and we split the samples three ways and take them home." She looked at each of them. "And then we report. Honestly. With data they can't ignore."

Dev let out a breath. Odalys nodded.

They worked through the day and into the night, three figures moving among the racks in the old station, speaking softly, as if not to wake anything. Near midnight, Odalys paused with a tray of seedlings in her arms and pressed her ear to the tile.

Far below, the drill kept turning. And above it, in the cold, something green leaned closer to listen.
