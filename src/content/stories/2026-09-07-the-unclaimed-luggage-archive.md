The warehouse smelled like dust and old leather and thirty years of forgetting.

Marian, the retired postal worker, arrived first on Monday morning with a thermos of coffee and a clipboard she'd bought in 1987. The clipboard had outlasted her career, her marriage, and the entire concept of clipboards. She approved of its persistence.

"Right," she said when the others arrived. "Four months until demolition. Seven thousand two hundred items. Let's be realistic—we'll manage maybe a quarter of that."

"Pessimism noted," said Jin, the data analyst, already photographing the rows of luggage with methodical precision. She was building a database, naturally. She'd explained the schema twice. Marian had understood none of it.

The high school student, Marcus, stood in the doorway looking overwhelmed. "There's a lot of... stuff."

"Acute observation," Marian said. "You'll go far."

The local historian, Dr. Abebe, was already opening the first suitcase—a tan Samsonite from approximately 1994. Inside: three pairs of men's underwear, a Tom Clancy novel, and a snow globe containing the Statue of Liberty.

"Someone's disappointing vacation," Jin said, entering data.

"Or their escape," Dr. Abebe said quietly.

They worked in shifts. The system was simple: open, photograph, catalog, close. No editorializing, though editorializing occurred anyway. By day three, they'd found fourteen snow globes, none containing the same landmark.

"It's a pattern," Marcus insisted.

"It's tourism," Marian countered.

"It's probably just coincidence," Jin said, but she flagged them in the database anyway.

The discoveries came in waves. A briefcase full of Polaroids showing the same kitchen from slightly different angles—137 photos in total. A duffel bag containing nothing but hotel soap, organized by state. A child's backpack with a perfectly preserved peanut butter sandwich, now archaeological.

"People are weird," Marcus observed.

"People are trying," Dr. Abebe said, examining the sandwich with genuine interest. "This child brought lunch for a field trip, perhaps. Forgot the bag. Somewhere, an adult had to explain to them that yes, we'd buy lunch, it's fine."

"It's a sandwich," Marian said.

"It's a story."

On week two, Jin's database began revealing actual connections. Two suitcases, lost four years apart, shared the same home address on their tags. A man named Robert Chen had apparently forgotten luggage in 1998 and 2002.

"Consistent," Jin said approvingly.

They decided to contact him. Marcus found him on Facebook in eleven seconds. Robert Chen, now retired, responded within the hour: "LOL yes that sounds like me. Nothing important in there I hope?"

The 1998 suitcase contained business clothes. The 2002 suitcase contained what appeared to be divorce papers and a photograph of a woman feeding ducks.

They did not mention the photograph.

But then they found two bags—different owners, different years—that each contained half of a two-volume encyclopedia set. Volume A-M, 1996. Volume N-Z, 2001.

"That's impossible," Marcus said.

"Improbable," Jin corrected.

They cross-referenced the tags. Different addresses, different names, different everything. No connection except the accidental completion of a reference work no one needed anymore.

"The universe has a sense of humor," Dr. Abebe said.

"The universe has randomness," Marian said. But she put the volumes side by side anyway.

By month three, they'd cataloged two thousand items and reunited twelve with owners. Most people expressed polite confusion. A few wept. One man asked if they'd found his left shoe, brown Oxford, size eleven. They had found sixteen left shoes. None were his.

The strangest discovery came in week eleven: a locked box that Jin's crowbar revealed to contain two hundred love letters, written but never sent, addressed to "M." No return address. No dates. Just longing, compressed and abandoned.

"We can't post these," Marcus said.

"No," Marian agreed.

They sat with that for a while.

On the final day, standing amid the remaining uncataloged items, Jin generated a report. Their database now existed. It would outlive the warehouse.

"We barely made a dent," Marcus said.

"We made a record," Dr. Abebe said.

Marian locked the door behind them. Somewhere, someone was missing something. That would never change. But now, at least, there was inventory. Evidence. Proof that people had been here, had carried things, had forgotten and moved on.

It wasn't much.

It was enough.
