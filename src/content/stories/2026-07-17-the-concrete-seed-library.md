The jackhammer's teeth had barely scratched the concrete when Luis felt it—a hollow echo that didn't belong. He killed the engine and knelt, pressing his palm flat against the parking garage floor.

"Marco, come here."

His crew chief wandered over, coffee in hand, squinting in the sodium light of the condemned structure. "We got a problem?"

"Listen." Luis tapped with his hammer. The sound bounced back wrong, empty.

By noon, they'd pried up three slabs to reveal a brick-lined chamber about eight feet square, its entrance sealed with what looked like a hundred-year-old door. Someone had painted careful letters across it, faded but legible: *Repository. Handle with Care.*

"The hell is this?" Marco muttered. "Old utility vault?"

But when they broke the rusted lock, it wasn't pipes or cables inside. Floor-to-ceiling shelves lined three walls, each stacked with hundreds of small manila envelopes, their edges brown with age. In the corner sat a metal desk with a glass-shaded lamp and three leather-bound ledgers.

Jenna, the youngest on the crew, reached for one of the envelopes. Her fingers trembled as she read aloud: "Battaglia Tomatoes. 1847 Maple Street. Garden demolished June 1964. Seeds preserved August 1964." She opened it carefully. Half a dozen dried seeds spilled into her palm, rust-red and impossibly small.

Luis picked up the topmost ledger. The handwriting inside was immaculate, each entry noting an address, a date, and a brief description: *Rosa Mendez's morning glories, blue as summer sky. House razed for highway expansion.* Then another: *The Kowalski pear tree. Bore fruit for sixty-three years. Parking lot constructed September 1971.*

"Someone was saving them," Marco said quietly. "Someone was trying to remember."

They spent the rest of the day reading instead of demolishing. The ledgers documented forty-three years of preservation—1958 to 2001—though the handwriting changed three times. Three different people, Luis realized, passing the work down. Three guardians of vanished gardens, of neighborhoods erased by progress, of green things paved over and forgotten.

The final entry simply read: *I am too old now to continue. May someone worthy find this.*

At quitting time, they sat on overturned buckets, surrounded by ghosts of gardens.

"We should call the city," Marco said, but his voice lacked conviction. "This is... I don't know, historical or something."

"You know what the city will do?" Jenna's jaw was set. "They'll send some bureaucrat who'll 'preserve' everything in a climate-controlled basement no one can access. Or they'll toss it. We're three weeks behind schedule as it is."

"But we can't just—" Marco gestured helplessly at the chamber.

"Can't we?" Luis looked at his crew chief. They'd worked together for twelve years. Marco had three kids. Luis knew he'd been planting vegetables with them in his backyard, teaching them what his grandmother taught him. "How many of these seeds are still viable, you think?"

Understanding flickered across Marco's face. "Some, maybe. Tomatoes and beans keep for years if they're stored right. It's cool down here, dry."

"My sister works at the community garden on Fourth Street," Jenna said quickly. "They've got a greenhouse. People who know about preserving heirloom varieties."

They looked at each other, three strangers bound by discovery, by a choice that felt larger than themselves.

"We finish the demo on schedule," Marco said slowly. "But first, we move everything. Careful. We become the fourth set of hands."

"City won't like us going off-schedule," Luis warned.

"Then we do it on our own time." Marco stood, decision made. "Some things matter more than schedules."

They worked through the night and the weekend after, carrying envelopes and ledgers up from beneath the condemned garage. Luis bought archival boxes with his own money. Jenna photographed every entry. Marco contacted his sister's friend who taught botany.

When the demolition finally began, the chamber was empty except for the desk and lamp. Luis left them there—a memorial to the unknown archivists, to be crushed beneath progress one final time.

But in a greenhouse across town, careful hands were already planting seeds from gardens that refused to be forgotten, tended now by new guardians who understood the weight of remembering.
