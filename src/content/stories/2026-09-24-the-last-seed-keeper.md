Margaret Chen had spent forty-three years teaching people the Dewey Decimal System, so the email from Parks & Recreation barely fazed her. It took exactly one sentence to destroy forty years of work: "The municipal seed bank will be decommissioned on November 15th, contents to be disposed of per regulations."

She read it three times. Disposed of. As if ten thousand varieties of heirloom seeds—some from before the war, some from her own grandmother's garden—were no different than outdated filing cabinets.

Margaret printed the email, drove to Riverside Park, and descended the concrete steps to the old cistern's steel door. Inside, the cool darkness smelled of earth and time. Row after row of climate-controlled drawers held small envelopes and glass vials, each labeled in meticulous handwriting: *Cherokee Purple Tomato, 1979. Hidatsa Red Bean, 1982.*

"You're the librarian."

Margaret startled. A woman in mud-stained coveralls sat at the sorting table, eating a sandwich.

"Tess Kowalski. I run the plot on Seventh Street." The urban farmer gestured with her sandwich. "Got the same email. Figured I'd take one last look before the city mulches forty years of biodiversity."

"We could move them," Margaret said.

"To where? My apartment?" Tess laughed bitterly. "These need proper storage. Temperature, humidity. The whole setup."

"Then we keep this setup."

"They're changing the locks tomorrow."

Margaret sat down across from her. "What if we don't let them?"

---

By eight PM, they had a plan that would have horrified Margaret's former self. By nine, they had recruited Eddie Pham, a seventy-two-year-old retired engineer who'd helped install the original refrigeration system, and Marcus Williams, a sullen seventeen-year-old sentenced to community service for spray-painting a overpass.

"Let me get this straight," Marcus said, descending the stairs with Eddie. "Grand theft seeds?"

"Temporary relocation," Margaret corrected. "Eddie, can you disconnect the cooling units?"

"Can I? Did it in '87 when we upgraded." The old man was already examining junction boxes. "Take three hours, maybe four."

"We don't have four hours," Tess said. "Parks crew arrives at six AM."

"Then we work faster." Margaret began opening drawers. "Marcus, you're young. Start carrying boxes."

"My community service is picking up trash, lady."

"Consider this very important trash." Margaret handed him a crate of seed packets. "These squash varieties are older than your parents. Some were saved by families fleeing wars. Others survived floods, droughts, entire civilizations. Now they'll survive bureaucracy, if you help."

Marcus shifted the box. "Where am I taking them?"

Tess grinned. "My truck. Then my garden shed, Eddie's garage, Margaret's basement—anywhere with power for the cooling units Eddie's about to steal."

"Repurpose," Eddie said cheerfully, unscrewing a panel.

---

At midnight, they'd moved half the collection. Margaret's hands ached. Marcus had stopped complaining an hour ago, settling into a rhythm. Tess and Eddie argued good-naturedly about optimal storage temperatures while hauling equipment.

"Why'd you work here?" Marcus asked Margaret during a water break.

"I didn't. I'm the library's cataloging specialist. Was. I retire next month." She carefully packed a drawer of beans. "But my grandmother volunteered here in the eighties. Taught me that libraries aren't just books—they're memory. What we choose to save says everything about who we are."

"What if nobody wants old seeds?"

"Someone will." Margaret sealed the box. "Maybe not this year. But someday there'll be a bad drought, or someone will want to taste the tomatoes their great-grandfather grew, or—"

"Or everything goes to hell and we need food that actually grows," Tess interrupted, hauling another crate. "Sorry. Cynical urban farmer talking. But she's right. This stuff matters."

At four AM, Eddie reconnected the last cooling unit in his garage. The quiet hum sounded like victory.

At five-thirty, they returned the cistern keys to Margaret's desk, leaving the empty facility spotless.

The following week, Margaret submitted a proposal to the city council. Three months later, the Riverside Community Seed Library opened above ground, distributed across six volunteer locations, cataloged by a retired librarian using a system that would have made Dewey proud.

Marcus logged his hours. Then kept showing up anyway.

Sometimes salvage looks like theft. Sometimes hope requires a little civil disobedience. And sometimes the future is saved by four people who refused to let it be thrown away.
