The basement had been built for boilers, and it still smelled faintly of rust and old heat. Marguerite Osei had swept it out three winters ago, strung two work lamps from the pipes, and set up a card table that now held forty-one shoeboxes of seeds.

Danny Reyes, fifteen, from 4C, came down every Tuesday and Thursday after school. He had started by carrying her groceries and ended up learning the difference between a determinate and an indeterminate tomato.

"Calendula," Marguerite said, tipping a pinch of curled, claw-shaped seeds into his palm. "Draw it from the side. People should know what they're holding."

He drew. That was his gift: a few strokes of a fine pen and a seed became a portrait, ridged and particular. Beneath each drawing Marguerite wrote the notes in her slanted hand. *Sow after last frost. Forgives neglect. Flowers until November if you keep cutting.* They folded the envelopes from old calendar pages, and on Saturdays Danny slipped them under doors.

Nobody asked where they came from. That was the arrangement. Mrs. Haddad on the second floor grew mint in coffee tins. The Kowalskis had pole beans climbing their fire escape. Someone on the sixth floor, they never learned who, had coaxed a fig tree into a bucket, and its leaves pressed against the glass like hands.

---

The notice came on a Monday, taped to the basement door.

*UNAUTHORIZED USE OF UTILITY SPACE. CONTENTS TO BE REMOVED. DOOR TO BE SEALED FRIDAY.* It was signed by Halvern Property Group, which had bought the building in August and repainted the lobby a colour Danny called "dentist."

He was furious by Tuesday. "We fight it," he said, laying out his phone on the card table like evidence. "I've got a petition drafted. Mrs. Haddad will sign. The Kowalskis. I can get the local news, they love this stuff. 'Retired Scientist and Teen Secretly Feed Neighbourhood.' They'd have to back off."

Marguerite sat with a box of bean seeds in her lap, turning one over. "And then what?"

"Then we keep going."

"Then we have a story," she said gently. "Then Halvern has a lawyer, and the city has an inspector, and the inspector finds the wiring I did myself. Then the people who grow mint in coffee tins have their names in a newspaper next to the word *unauthorized*. Some of them don't want to be seen, Danny. Some of them can't afford it."

He opened his mouth and closed it. He thought of Mr. Abara in 3F, who never answered his door before checking the peephole twice.

"So we just let them win."

"I didn't say that." She set the box down. "I'm saying we have three days, and a room is not the thing we're keeping."

---

They worked late Wednesday and later Thursday. Marguerite didn't explain at first; she simply opened the catalogue, the thick binder holding every drawing and note, eleven years of her handwriting and three of his, and began to unclip the rings.

"What are you doing?"

"Dividing it."

One page per household. One page, with its drawing and its notes, paired with a full envelope of that variety's seed and a second, empty envelope. On the empty one she wrote: *Save seed from your best plant. Pass it to a neighbour. Keep this page.*

Danny understood slowly, then all at once. There would be no library to seal. There would be ninety-two small libraries, each holding one volume, scattered through the building and down the block and into the next.

"Nobody will know it was us," he said.

"That's rather the point." She smiled. "You wanted it visible. It will be. Every windowsill."

He looked at the binder's empty rings. "But your name. Eleven years."

Marguerite took his pen and, on the last page, the calendula, his drawing, she wrote at the bottom in small letters: *Drawn by D. Reyes.* Then she handed it back to him.

"I've had my name on plenty of things," she said. "Journals. Grant reports. No one remembers those. Keep that one. Someday someone will ask you where calendula came from, and you'll get to decide what to tell them."

---

On Friday a man from Halvern unlocked the basement with a contractor and a roll of caution tape. He found a card table, two work lamps, and swept concrete. He sealed it anyway.

In April, Danny walked home along Ferris Street and counted. Beans on three fire escapes. Marigolds in a stairwell window. The fig on the sixth floor, enormous now. On a stoop four buildings down, a stranger was cutting calendula, and taped to her door was a page in a slanted hand, its corner soft from being read.

He didn't stop. He just kept the count.
