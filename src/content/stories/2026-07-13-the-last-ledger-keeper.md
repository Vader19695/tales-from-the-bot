Margaret Chen had been coming to the municipal bus depot at 6:47 AM for forty years, which meant she'd walked past the motivational poster in the break room—"Teamwork Makes the Dream Work!"—approximately 10,400 times. She had never once seen anyone work as a team to replace the burnt-out fluorescent tube that made everyone look vaguely tubercular.

On her final day, she arrived at 6:47 AM carrying a cardboard box and a green ledger the size of a tombstone.

The ledger had no official name. Margaret called it The Book in her head, though she'd never spoken about it aloud. It contained forty years of handwritten entries: fuel discrepancies, shift swaps the digital system couldn't accommodate, personal loans between drivers that affected their availability, notes about which mechanics owed whom favors. The entire invisible circulatory system of the depot, recorded in her careful script.

She set it on her desk with a soft thump.

"Morning, Margaret." Dispatch supervisor Tom Reeves passed her cubicle with his usual styrofoam cup. "Big day, huh?"

"Indeed."

"You train your replacement yet?"

"There isn't one."

Tom laughed. "Budget cuts, am I right?" He disappeared around the corner.

Margaret opened The Book to a random page. March 2003. Driver Luis Ramírez had lent driver Patricia Wu $340 for her daughter's medical bills, repayable at $20 per paycheck. The digital accounting system showed Luis requesting unexplained overtime on routes that would put him on the same schedule as Patricia, the only way they could coordinate repayment without involving official channels that would trigger a policy violation.

Margaret had tracked it. Seventeen months of payments, correctly accounted for in ways the system couldn't see. No flags, no audits, no one's employment endangered.

She flipped forward. 2011. The fuel vendor had been shorting deliveries by forty gallons per month. The digital invoices matched the contracts perfectly. But Margaret's handwritten fuel logs, cross-referenced with driver reports, showed the discrepancy. She'd quietly mentioned it to the fleet manager, who'd quietly switched vendors. Thousands saved. No fanfare, no report filed.

The Book was full of these invisible corrections. The municipal accounting software was designed by people who'd never been to a bus depot, for processes that didn't account for human beings having lives. Margaret's ledger was the translation layer between bureaucratic theory and operational reality.

At 10:30, her retirement cake appeared—sheet cake from Costco, "Good Luck Margret" in blue icing. The spelling made her eye twitch.

At 2:15, she received a plaque: "In Recognition of 40 Years of Dedicated Service."

At 4:00, the office began emptying. Tom Reeves stopped by her cubicle. "So, uh, you leave any notes? Transition docs?"

Margaret looked at The Book. "What would you like to know?"

"I don't know. Anything important?"

"The digital system closes accounts automatically after three months of inactivity."

"Right. It's supposed to."

"Driver Chen—no relation—has been on medical leave for two months. When he returns next month, his fuel card won't work. The system will flag him. It will take six weeks to resolve, during which he can't work, and he'll miss his mortgage payment."

Tom blinked. "How do you know about his mortgage?"

"I pay attention."

"Well, I guess we'll handle it when it comes up."

Margaret nodded slowly. She'd written Tom's name in The Book three times over the years—twice for expense report errors she'd corrected before they reached auditing, once for a scheduling conflict that would have left the entire west route uncovered on Thanksgiving. He had no idea.

At 6:00 PM, Margaret was alone in her cubicle. The Book sat before her, patient and enormous.

She could digitize it—scan all 40,000 entries, create a database no one would maintain. She could pass it to someone, though she couldn't identify who that someone might be. She could destroy it, let the depot finally learn what it cost to run on official systems alone.

Instead, Margaret Chen did what she'd always done.

She opened to a blank page and wrote one final entry, dated today. Driver Chen's fuel card reactivation, the file number, the department contact. Instructions clear enough for anyone who bothered to look.

Then she closed The Book, placed it in her desk's bottom drawer, and locked it.

She didn't leave a key.

But she did, she noticed, keep one.
