The demolition notice arrived on a Tuesday, taped to the parking garage's concrete pillar like a death sentence in triplicate. Marcus, who'd spent twenty-three years collecting quarters in the attendant booth, brought it down to the basement with hands that wouldn't quite steady.

"Sixty days," he said, setting the paper on the makeshift table between the seed catalogs and Mason jars. "They're putting up luxury condos."

In the fluorescent-lit basement, surrounded by filing cabinets labeled with Latin names and climate zones, the news landed like a stone in still water. Dr. Eleanor Vasquez looked up from the Cherokee Trail of Tears beans she'd been sorting, her weathered hands frozen mid-count. Across from her, seventeen-year-old Tasha stopped labeling seed packets, marker hovering over "Detroit Dark Red Beet—1892."

"That's not enough time," Eleanor said finally. "We have fourteen hundred varieties here. Some of these seeds are over a century old."

"The city doesn't care about old seeds," Marcus replied. He'd been saying some version of this for years, every time they'd expanded the operation another shelf, another drawer. The parking garage was dying—had been dying since the factories closed—and everyone knew corpses couldn't protect anything.

Tasha stood abruptly, her chair scraping concrete. "So we move it. My school has a basement. Or—"

"Your school's basement floods every spring," Eleanor interrupted, not unkindly. "These seeds need stable temperature, low humidity, darkness. This"—she gestured at the brutalist cave around them—"is accidentally perfect."

"Was accidentally perfect," Marcus corrected.

They fell into the argument they'd been having in various forms since the seed library began: what deserved saving, what could be sacrificed, whether their small rebellion against forgetting meant anything at all. The seed library had started five years ago when Eleanor, newly retired from the university, had nowhere to put her life's work. Marcus had offered the unused basement, and slowly, others had come. Tasha, growing vegetables in her grandmother's backyard, hungry for varieties that actually tasted like something. Old men with seeds from the countries they'd left. Young mothers wanting to know what a real tomato was.

They were still arguing when the stranger arrived.

She appeared at the bottom of the stairs like something conjured: expensive coat, architect's tube under one arm, the unmistakable bearing of someone who owned things. Marcus instinctively moved between her and the seed cabinets.

"I'm Yolanda Chen," she said. "I'm the developer."

The temperature in the room dropped ten degrees.

"Come to gloat?" Tasha asked.

"Come to negotiate." Yolanda set the tube on the table, unfurled blueprints across their seed packets. "I need this project to work—the city's broke, this neighborhood needs housing. But my grandmother kept seeds through the Cultural Revolution. Hid them in her shoes when she emigrated." She pointed to the drawings. "This is the building we're planning. These"—she indicated shaded rectangles—"are climate-controlled storage units we're required to include. Regulations. Fire code. Nobody ever uses them."

Eleanor leaned forward, studying the plans. "The temperature—"

"Constant fifty-five degrees. Humidity controlled. Each unit has independent power." Yolanda met her eyes. "I can't save the garage. But I can design a vault that happens to meet your needs. The condo board would never know they're sitting on a seed library."

Marcus laughed, surprised and bitter. "Why?"

"Because cities forget things," Yolanda said simply. "My grandmother understood that. And because I'm tired of building places with no memory."

The three of them looked at each other. It wasn't what they wanted—the familiar cave, the parking garage's accidental sanctuary. But Eleanor thought of the Cherokee beans, carried along the Trail of Tears. Tasha thought of her grandmother, who remembered when Detroit grew its own food. Marcus thought of twenty-three years of small rebellions in a concrete booth.

"We'd need access," Eleanor said slowly. "Climate logs. Security."

"I'll write it into my lease." Yolanda was already making notes. "Call it a historical preservation requirement."

Outside, the city was forgetting itself street by street. But in a brutalist basement, surrounded by seeds that had survived worse than demolition notices, four people bent over blueprints and began the work of remembering forward.
