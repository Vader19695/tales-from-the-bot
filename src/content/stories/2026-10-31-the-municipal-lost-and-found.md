The Municipal Lost Property Depot occupied Level 3 of the old Hargrove Street car park, where the ceiling still bore painted arrows pointing toward an exit that had been bricked up in 1994. Agnes Pell had worked beneath those arrows for thirty-one years. She found them encouraging.

Her system was simple. Every object received a tag, a number, and five minutes of her full attention. An umbrella was never just an umbrella. It was an umbrella with a dented ferrule and a dry-cleaning ticket folded into the strap, which meant a person who walked with it as a cane but was too proud to buy a real one. That person lived within four blocks of Feeney's Cleaners. That person, nine times out of ten, could be found.

The first wooden box arrived on a Tuesday, handed in by a bus depot clerk who had been told to stop bringing her single gloves. It was cedar, roughly the size of a hardback novel, joined at the corners with neat dovetails. It was empty. Agnes logged it as *Box, wooden, unremarkable*, and immediately regretted the adjective. It was quite remarkable. Nobody dovetails a box by accident.

The second arrived Thursday. The third and fourth came the following Monday, from different ends of the city. By the end of the month she had eleven, lined up on Shelf 40 like a small, polite queue.

Agnes did what she always did. She looked.

Each box had a number pencilled on its underside: 3, 7, 12, 19, and so on, none higher than 40. Each lid bore the initials *M.O.*, burned faintly into the wood. And every box had been found on a Route 14 bus or at one of its stops. People did not lose eleven identical boxes on one bus route. They left them.

The cedar carried a faint purple lumberyard stamp on one inner panel, half sanded away. It took her three phone calls and one mild lie about being a magistrate's secretary to learn that Baird & Sons had sold a quantity of aromatic cedar the previous spring to a T. Okafor of Lindell Road.

Teodor Okafor was seventy-four and wore an apron as though it had been issued to him at birth. When Agnes explained who she was, he looked at the box in her hands and sat down rather suddenly on his front step.

"Forty," he said. "I made forty."

His wife, Mira, had driven the 14 for twenty-six years. When she died last spring, the funeral had filled with people he didn't recognise: a man who took the 6:10 every morning, a woman who had boarded with a cello twice a week, a teenager Mira had once refused to let off in the rain without an umbrella. Her regulars. Teodor, who had not known what else to do with his hands, made each of them a box.

"To keep something in," he said. "I didn't say what. I thought they'd know."

"Evidently they didn't," said Agnes.

"Evidently."

She went back to Level 3 and did the second thing she always did, which was to assume that every object wanted to go home, even if its owner had not yet worked out where home was. Teodor had kept a guest list from the funeral, most of it first names and descriptions in his late wife's shorthand: *Cello. Grey Scarf. Boy w/ Chemistry Books.* Agnes cross-referenced the numbers against the order in which the boxes had been handed out. She rode the 14 for six days with a notebook. She was, she noted, extremely good at this.

The man in the grey scarf, when she found him, went red to the ears. "I didn't know what to put in it," he said. "Everything seemed too small. So I left it on her seat. It seemed like the right place."

They all said something similar. The cellist. The chemistry boy, now a university student. A retired postman who had left his box at the stop outside the hospital where Mira used to wait an extra minute for the night nurses.

Agnes posted eleven letters announcing a claim day.

They came on a Saturday, standing awkwardly between the shelves and the painted arrows. It emerged, slowly, that they had ridden the same bus for years without ever speaking to one another. They had always spoken to Mira instead. Now they spoke to each other, at first about her and then, cautiously, about other things. The postman and the cellist discovered they lived on the same street. Someone produced biscuits.

Teodor arrived last, carrying a twelfth box.

"Number one," he admitted. "I left mine on the bus as well."

Agnes took it from him, turned it over, and wrote out a tag.

*Box, cedar. Claimed.*

Then she handed it back, and went to put the kettle on.
