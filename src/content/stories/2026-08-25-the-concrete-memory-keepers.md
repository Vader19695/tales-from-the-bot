The fluorescent lights buzzed against the 2 a.m. silence as Maria pushed her cleaning cart through Level Three of the Westbrook Municipal Garage. In six days, the demolition crew would reduce this concrete cathedral to rubble, making way for luxury condos nobody who worked here could afford.

"Hey, check this out," called Tommy, her youngest crew member, crouched beside Pillar 47. He traced his finger over something carved into the concrete. "JH + MW, 1987. That's the year I was born."

Maria walked over, shining her flashlight at the pillar's surface. What she'd dismissed as water damage and grime revealed itself differently under direct light: letters, numbers, crude hearts, initials stacked upon initials like archaeological layers.

"Mother of God," whispered Yuki, joining them. "They're everywhere."

And they were. Once you started looking, the pillars transformed from blank gray sentinels into dense texts. *Beth was here, 1974*. *Miguel forever*. *First date—nervous!* A child's handprint, dated 2003. Phone numbers from when they had seven digits. A surprisingly skilled etching of a bird in flight.

For the next hour, they abandoned their mops and moved through the garage in wonder, three witnesses to decades of anonymous testimony. Each pillar held hundreds of markings, some carved deep with keys or knives, others scratched faintly with coins or pebbles. The earliest they found was from 1962, the year the garage opened: *Eddie Turner—I helped pour this concrete.*

"People really wanted to be remembered," Tommy said softly.

Maria thought of her own name carved in her childhood closet back in El Salvador, in a house that might not exist anymore. These marks were somebody's proof: *I was here. I mattered. I loved someone.*

On Level Two, they found what Yuki called "the chronicle"—one pillar where the same person seemed to have returned over years. *First job interview, 1979. Scared shitless.* Then, *Got the job!! 1979.* Further down: *Asked Lisa to marry me, 1981.* And near the base: *Lisa left, 1983. Everything ends.*

"We should photograph them," Yuki said. "Document this before—"

"Before what?" interrupted a voice behind them. They turned to find Carl, the site foreman for Apex Development, standing at the garage entrance in a hard hat despite the late hour. "Before we do our job?"

"These are historical," Yuki protested. "People's stories."

Carl walked to the nearest pillar, barely glancing at it. "People's vandalism, you mean. You know how many parking garages exist in this country? If we preserved every one somebody scratched their initials into, we'd never build anything."

"But nobody knows these are here," Maria said. "Shouldn't someone decide—"

"Someone did decide. The city council. Progress doesn't stop for graffiti." Carl softened slightly. "Look, I get it. It's your last week, you're sentimental. But this place is going down Tuesday whether you photograph it or not. What's the difference?"

After he left, the three stood in heavy silence. 

"We could call the historical society," Tommy offered without conviction.

Maria shook her head. "They'll say the same thing. Wrong building, wrong neighborhood, wrong kind of history." She'd learned long ago which stories society chose to keep.

"So we just let it disappear?" Yuki asked.

Maria walked to Pillar 47, running her palm across its scarred surface. All these declarations of love and existence, made by hands that believed concrete meant permanence. People who thought parking garages lasted forever.

"We witness," she said finally. "We spend our last week reading everything we can. We learn who they were."

"That's not preservation," Yuki said.

"No," Maria agreed. "But it's something. Maybe it's enough—to know someone read their messages. To not let them vanish unwatched."

So they did. Each night shift became an archaeology of the ordinary. They read thousands of messages aloud to each other, giving voice to the silent archive. They laughed at jokes from 1991, mourned heartbreaks from 2005, marveled at the optimism of someone who carved *Class of 2020* in 2016, not knowing what was coming.

On Tuesday morning, they finished their final shift as the demolition crew arrived with their machines. Maria touched Pillar 47 one last time.

"We saw you," she whispered. "That matters."

Then she walked into the dawn, carrying nothing but memory, which is the only permanence any of us really have.
