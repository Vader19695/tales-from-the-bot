For thirty years, Voss had known every seam of the Great Cistern the way a priest knows scripture. She knew the hairline at the eastern buttress that wept a little each spring. She knew the sandstone patch the founders had laid crooked, and the echo that came back from the north vault half a heartbeat late. So when she found the new fracture on the morning of the long fast, she knew at once that it was wrong.

It ran along the lowest course of the western wall, just above the waterline. That alone was strange; the pressure there pushed outward, and stone under compression did not crack like this. She knelt on the wet ledge and held her lamp close.

The fracture did not wander. It branched at clean angles, split, rejoined, and split again, like a river delta drawn by a careful hand.

Voss took out her notebook and began, as she always did, with measurements. Length: one meter, eleven centimeters. Depth: unknown; her probe slid in past its full handle without touching bottom. Moisture: none. In a cistern holding forty million liters, a crack below the waterline was dry.

She sat back on her heels and let herself breathe.

Over the next nine days, the fractures multiplied. They appeared overnight, each one in the lowest course of the western wall, each one dry. She logged them all. She ran the city's geology surveys through her old terminal until her eyes ached: limestone over sandstone, no fault lines, no seismic activity in eighty years. Nothing beneath the city could make stone behave this way.

On the tenth day, she noticed the pattern.

She had been copying the fractures into her notebook at scale, and when she laid the pages side by side, the angles repeated. A fork, a hook, a closed loop. Again and again, in different orders. Not random. Not geological.

Writing.

That night, she brought a hammer down to the western wall and tapped the stone beside the oldest crack: three blows, evenly spaced.

The water stilled. Then, from somewhere deep inside the wall, came three blows in reply.

Voss did not sleep. She sat on the ledge until dawn, her lamp guttering, and tapped, and listened, and wrote. Whatever lay behind the wall answered slowly, patiently, the way one speaks to a child. By the third night, she had begun to match taps to marks. By the fifth, she could read a sentence.

*We are the record. We were sealed when the water was taken.*

She found the old founders' charter in the municipal archive, a document every schoolchild memorized. *Here, in the empty desert, we raised a city from nothing.* Then she found the surveyor's ledger from the same year, uncatalogued, its pages brittle as moth wings. It described a settlement at the spring before the city existed, and a council vote to "relocate" its people. There was no record of where.

The wall told her the rest, tap by tap. The people at the spring had pressed their histories into the stone the founders used to line the cistern: their names, their songs, the shape of their villages. They had sealed it with a mason's trick, a pressure lock that would hold only as long as the reservoir stayed full.

*When the city no longer needs the water,* the wall said, *it will need us.*

Voss checked the council minutes. The new aquifer pipeline had been approved four months ago. The Great Cistern was to be drained by winter.

She stood in her office before the terminal, the report half-written. Procedure was clear: structural anomalies went to the council within forty-eight hours. She had never missed that deadline. She had built a life on not missing it.

But the founders had hidden this once, and she knew how institutions dealt with inconvenient histories. A quiet survey. A sealed file. A wall reinforced with fresh concrete, "for safety."

*Hidden for a reason*, her instinct said, and she had nearly listened. Then she understood the reason wasn't fear. The people at the spring hadn't hidden their record to keep it buried. They had hidden it to outlast the people who would bury it. They had waited for a city that might finally be ready to listen.

Voss deleted her draft.

She wrote a new one, and filed it publicly, copied to the university, the newspapers, and every name in the founders' registry she could trace. Then she went down to the cistern with her hammer.

She tapped three times.

*They are coming,* she told the wall. *All of them. Not just the council.*

The answer came back slowly, and for the first time in thirty years, the stone was warm beneath her hand.
