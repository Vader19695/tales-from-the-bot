The email arrived at 6:47 PM: construction crews scheduled for 8 AM tomorrow. Demolition permit approved. Marcus read it twice on his phone, standing beside rows of carefully labeled mason jars that held three years of meticulous work.

"Well," he said to the empty cistern, "shit."

By eight o'clock, all seven of them had descended the maintenance ladder. They gathered in the central chamber where the old water tank had once held the city's reserve supply, back when the neighborhood above was farmland instead of condos. Now it held something else: 2,847 varieties of seeds, each catalogued, tested, and stored in climate-controlled darkness.

"We knew this might happen," Priya said, though her voice caught slightly. She'd been the first to suggest the cistern three years ago, when the community garden where they'd all met was bulldozed for a parking structure.

"Knowing and experiencing are different animals," Chen observed, running his hand along a shelf of Brandywine tomato seeds. "I vote we tell the city council. Present everything. They'll have to see the value."

Marcus snorted. "The same council that approved demolishing this place? They'll confiscate everything, fine us for trespassing, and probably scatter the seeds anyway."

"We could move it all," suggested Rosa. "Rent a warehouse."

"With what money?" Dmitri asked. "We pooled everything just to get the climate control working down here. Besides, we'd need refrigerated transport, proper storage immediately ready. We have, what, twelve hours?"

Silence settled over them like dust.

The cistern had been perfect—forgotten municipal infrastructure with stable temperature and humidity. They'd jury-rigged the electrical, installed proper ventilation, built the shelving themselves. Every weekend for three years, they'd come here: trading seeds at farmers' markets, accepting donations from elderly gardeners whose children didn't want their life's work, even driving six hours once to collect Cherokee Purple tomatoes from a man who'd grown them from his grandmother's seeds for fifty-seven consecutive summers.

"I have an idea," Yuki said quietly. She was the youngest, a grad student in urban planning who rarely spoke in their meetings. "What if we don't choose?"

"Elaborate," Priya said.

"The council meets Thursday. Construction starts tomorrow. What if we do both—move what we can tonight, and show up Thursday with evidence of what they almost destroyed?"

"A public shaming campaign?" Chen looked skeptical.

"A conversation starter," Yuki corrected. "We can't save everything tonight. But we can save enough to continue the work, and document what's here to show what matters. Make them decide, publicly, whether to halt construction."

Marcus pulled out his phone. "I have a friend with a refrigerated van."

"My cousin has a basement," added Dmitri. "Not ideal, but temporary."

They worked through the night with practiced efficiency, prioritizing the rarest varieties: Dragon Tongue beans, Moon and Stars watermelon, Mortgage Lifter tomatoes with lineage traced to a West Virginia gardener in 1930. Rosa photographed everything, creating a visual archive. Yuki drafted a presentation, her fingers flying across her laptop balanced on an empty shelf.

By 6 AM, they'd moved forty percent of the collection. The rest they arranged artfully in the center of the cistern, labels facing outward, each jar telling its story of vanishing diversity.

When the construction crew arrived at eight, they found seven exhausted people standing guard, and Yuki already on the phone with a city council member who happened to be her former professor.

The photos went viral by noon—rows of labeled jars, each one a variety that had nearly disappeared. The council called an emergency meeting. The hashtag #SeedCistern trended locally.

They didn't win everything. The construction was delayed, not cancelled. But the city agreed to discussions about establishing an official seed library. The preservation society offered partnership. A local university expressed interest in their documentation methods.

In Dmitri's cousin's basement, surrounded by mason jars and the earthy smell of preservation, the seven of them shared coffee and allowed themselves cautious hope.

"So," Marcus said. "Anyone know any abandoned subway stations?"

Priya threw a seed packet at him, and they laughed—the tired, genuine laughter of people who'd learned that sometimes saving the world means showing up, one seed at a time.
