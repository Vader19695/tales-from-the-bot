Keiko Tanabe had worked the night shift at the Harlow Basin Water Treatment Facility for nineteen years, and for eleven of them she had kept two logbooks.

The first was the official one: turbidity readings, chlorine residuals, pump amperage, written in the block capitals her supervisors had long ago stopped reading. The second was a green field notebook she kept in her coat pocket. It recorded other things.

*March 4. Cattail shoots in the northeast quadrant, 6 cm. Sedge still dormant. Red-winged blackbird returned. Same one? Notch in left primary.*

Clarifier Three had been taken offline in 2009 when a drive shaft cracked. The repair budget never came, and the city simply drained it to a foot of mineral sludge and forgot it. Keiko hadn't forgotten. That first spring she'd noticed a haze of green on the dark surface, willow seed and thistle down, carried in on the wind from the rail yards. By the third year there were rushes. By the fifth, a willow sapling had rooted near the outflow weir, and frogs had arrived from somewhere, chorusing in the April dark while the city slept.

She did not interfere much. She cleared the overflow channel when it clogged, so the water stayed sweet and moving. She pulled out invasive knotweed by hand, wearing her gloves and headlamp at three in the morning. And she wrote everything down.

In the seventh year she found the sedge.

It took her months with a borrowed key from the library to be sure. *Carex lupuliformis*, false hop sedge, listed as extirpated from the county since 1962. Somehow a seed had survived in the river silt that settled here, and woken.

---

The memo came in October. Harlow Basin would be decommissioned by December 15th and replaced by a new automated plant on the adjacent lot. Existing structures would be demolished and backfilled. Night-shift positions would be eliminated.

Her supervisor, Dale, delivered the news at shift change with a coffee in each hand. "Package is decent," he said. "You've got your years in."

"What happens to Clarifier Three?"

He frowned, as if she'd asked about the weather on another planet. "Same as the rest. Concrete gets crushed, sludge goes to landfill. Why?"

She could have told him then. She had imagined that moment often: the notebook on his desk, the photographs, the sedge. But she had watched Dale for eleven years. She had seen how he handled a raccoon nesting in the pump house. *Liability*, he would say. *Contamination. Delay.* A protected species would mean environmental review, and environmental review meant cost, and someone above Dale would make a phone call, and a bulldozer would arrive early on a Saturday when no one was watching.

"No reason," she said.

---

She had fifty-three nights.

She worked methodically, as she did everything. She called the state herbarium from the break room phone and was told a botanist could perhaps visit in the spring. She thanked them and hung up. Spring would be gravel.

So she did what she could with her own hands. She collected sedge seed heads into paper envelopes, labeling each with date and quadrant. She dug willow cuttings and wrapped them in wet burlap in the trunk of her car. She scooped sludge into buckets, because the sludge was the true treasure, dense with spores and dormant seeds and the patience of decades.

On night thirty, she walked the new plant's perimeter. The landscaping plan, pinned in the construction trailer, showed a stormwater swale along the eastern fence: a long, shallow ditch designed to catch runoff from the roof. *Seed with standard turf mix*, the plan read. It would be mowed twice a year. No one would ever walk there.

She studied it for a long time.

---

On December 14th, her final shift, the frogs had long gone silent for winter. Keiko worked until four, then carried her buckets out to the swale in the dark. She spread the sludge in a thin black ribbon along its bottom. She pressed the sedge seeds into the cold earth one by one and pushed willow cuttings into the soft banks where water would gather. It took two hours. Her knees ached.

At six, Dale arrived to collect her keys.

"Anything to report?" he asked, out of habit.

She considered the green notebook in her pocket. Eleven years. Enough to make her name in some journal, maybe. Enough to have mattered, in the way the world measured mattering.

"No," she said. "Everything's running fine."

She drove to the county library that afternoon and donated the notebook to the local history collection, uncatalogued, tucked between old flood reports. Someone, someday, would find it.

In April, she drove past the new plant. Along the eastern fence, in a ditch no one had bothered to mow, something green was rising.
