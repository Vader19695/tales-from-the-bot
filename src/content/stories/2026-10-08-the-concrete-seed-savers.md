The garden occupied the roof of the old Halvorsen Street parking garage, which meant every tomato we grew had climbed four flights of oil-stained ramp to get there. The soil came from wherever soil could be found: demolished lots, a church that was repaving, one memorable truckload Marcus swore was "basically legal." We grew squash in kiddie pools and potatoes in stacked tires. The water came from a fire standpipe that Marcus had tapped with a brass fitting and a confidence the fire department would not have shared.

There were about fifteen of us, depending on the season and who was mad at whom. And there was Delphine.

Delphine was seventy-three, wore the same canvas hat in all weathers, and said perhaps eleven words a week, most of them Latin. We knew she'd been a botanist at the university before retiring. We did not know, until Priya went looking for zip ties in the old cashier's booth by the stairwell, that she had been running a seed bank out of it.

There were hundreds of film canisters and baby food jars, each labeled in tiny, furious handwriting.

*Lamb's quarters. Vacant lot, 9th & Bell. Lot now "The Bellwether" (luxury studios).*

*Wild violet. Mrs. Haskins' front yard. Yard now parking pad.*

*Fig cutting, rooted. Behind Sal's Bakery. Sal's now a cycling studio.*

*Chicory. Cracks in sidewalk, Garnet Ave. Sidewalk now "streetscape."*

"She's been doing this for six years," Priya said, holding a jar of milkweed fluff like it was a relic. "She's been collecting the neighborhood."

We confronted Delphine the way people with no confrontation skills do, which is to say we stood around her bean trellis until she noticed.

"Someone had to," she said, and went back to tying up beans. Five words. A big day.

The surveyor arrived the following Tuesday.

He was young, in a reflective vest, with a tripod and the expression of a man who had been told this would be an empty roof. Behind him, the letter we'd all been expecting was finally made of flesh: Corvin Development had bought the garage. The garden, in their parlance, was "temporary site activity."

We held a meeting in the shade of the corn.

Option one was silence: let him measure, let them come, go quietly like the fig tree behind Sal's. Nobody liked this, though Gerald, who owned the only reliable truck, pointed out it was cheaper than lawyers.

Option two was war. Marcus proposed chaining ourselves to the standpipe, which would have drawn attention mainly to the standpipe. Priya proposed a petition, which had worked zero of the four times the neighborhood had tried it.

Delphine, who had been sitting on an overturned bucket, cleared her throat. Everyone went quiet, the way you would if a statue coughed.

"A garden in one place," she said, "is a target."

That was the whole speech. It turned out to be enough.

Over the next three weeks, the seed bank went out the door. Not all at once — Delphine was a scientist and insisted on proper germination conditions — but steadily. Chicory went back into the cracks on Garnet Avenue, where the city's new streetscape had conveniently left joints between pavers. Violets went into the tree pits along Bell Street. The lamb's quarters went everywhere; lamb's quarters needs no encouragement. Gerald drove rooted fig cuttings to anyone with a yard, a fire escape, or a willingness to lie to their landlord. Priya printed tiny labels for each planting, matching Delphine's old ones: *Returned.*

The surveyor came back twice more. On his third visit he found Delphine repotting milkweed by the cashier's booth and asked, with real curiosity, what it was.

She told him. Then she told him about monarch butterflies, and then about the vacant lot on Hollis Street where she'd collected it, and then — this took forty minutes, the longest anyone had ever heard her talk — about the woman who'd lived beside that lot and fed every stray cat in the ward.

He left with a jar. He said his grandmother had lived on Hollis.

The garage came down in October. We watched from the sidewalk, and it was terrible, and nobody pretended otherwise.

But in spring the neighborhood came up green in places it had no business being green. Violets in the tree pits. Chicory along Garnet, blue as gas flames. A fig on Gerald's cousin's fire escape, fruiting out of a five-gallon bucket. And outside the gleaming lobby of the new Halvorsen Residences, in the decorative planter between the boxwoods, a stand of milkweed nobody at Corvin Development could account for.

Delphine walked past it every morning. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to.
