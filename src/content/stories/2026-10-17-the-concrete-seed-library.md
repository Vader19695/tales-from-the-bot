The demolition notice was taped to the concrete pillar marked P2-C, right next to the hand-lettered sign that said SQUASH (CUCURBITA) — PLEASE RETURN BY OCTOBER.

Margarethe Pell read it twice, then took off her reading glasses and read it again, as though farsightedness might soften the language. It did not. *Thirty days. All occupants. Structural removal to commence.*

"Occupants," she said. "As if we're squatters."

"We kind of are squatters," said Dev, who was sixteen and believed in accuracy above all things, including tact.

"We have a lease."

"We have a letter from a guy who owned the building in 2019 saying, quote, 'Sure, why not.'"

Margarethe had been a botanist for forty-one years, the last eleven of them retired, and she had learned that most living things survive by being too small to bother with. For two years, the seed library had been exactly that kind of thing: a converted parking level beneath a shuttered department store, lit by grow lamps and a single humming dehumidifier, where she and three teenagers sorted, dried, labeled, and lent heirloom seeds to anyone in the neighborhood with a windowsill and a little faith.

Four thousand two hundred packets. Cherokee Purple tomatoes. Glass Gem corn. A bean called Grandma Tuttle's that a woman named Mrs. Odum had brought in a coffee can, saying it had come up from Georgia in her mother's coat lining in 1951.

You did not bulldoze Grandma Tuttle's bean.

"We need a plan," Margarethe said. "Lawyers. Petitions. Someone at City Hall who owes someone a favor."

"Or," said Lucía, from behind the sorting table.

Lucía was the quiet one, the one who had first wandered down the ramp two years ago to escape a rainstorm and stayed to alphabetize the brassicas. She was holding her phone the way people hold something that might detonate.

"Or what?"

"Or I should probably tell you something."

Toby, the third of the trio, looked up from the drying racks. "Oh no. Did you finally tell people about the mouse?"

"There's no mouse."

"There's *definitely* a mouse."

Lucía turned the phone around. On the screen was a video: Margarethe's own hands, spotted and steady, tipping okra seeds into a paper envelope, while a soft voice-over said, *This is Margarethe. She says okra is "the most misunderstood vegetable in America and frankly it brought that on itself."*

Beneath it, a number: 2.3 million views.

Margarethe sat down on an overturned bucket.

"I started posting last spring," Lucía said quickly. "Just little things. Toby labeling. Dev yelling at the dehumidifier."

"It was making a *sound*," Dev said.

"People liked it. Like, a lot. There's a whole comment section where people share what they grew from our seeds. A guy in Ohio named his tomato plant after you."

"I'm a tomato plant," Margarethe said faintly.

"You're several tomato plants. There's a hashtag."

Toby grabbed the phone and scrolled. His eyebrows climbed steadily higher. "Forty-eight thousand followers. Lucía. *Lucía.* We're famous and nobody told me? I've been wearing the same hoodie in every video."

"That's sort of your brand now."

Margarethe held out her hand for the phone. She scrolled slowly, the way she used to read field notes. A schoolteacher in Tucson who'd started her own seed library in a classroom closet. A man in Manila asking how to dry pepper seeds without mold. A grandmother in Leeds who'd written simply: *I watch these when I can't sleep. Thank you for doing something patient.*

Something patient. She had spent her career writing grant applications that tried to make patience sound exciting. It had never once occurred to her that patience, filmed in good light, might be enough.

"Post the notice," she said.

Lucía blinked. "Really?"

"Really. And tell them the truth. Tell them we have thirty days and four thousand packets and nowhere to go."

By the end of the week, there were sixty volunteers in the garage on a Saturday, and a queue up the ramp. The petition had eleven thousand signatures. A city councilwoman who'd never returned Margarethe's calls now returned them twice daily. The developer released a statement describing itself as "deeply committed to community green spaces," which Dev printed out and taped to the dehumidifier as a joke.

But it was Toby — Toby of the hoodie — who had the idea that actually mattered.

"What if we just lend it all out?" he said. "Everything. Every packet. A library isn't a building, right? It's the stuff moving around."

So they did. For three weeks, they mailed and handed out seeds to anyone who asked, with one condition: grow them, save some, bring them back. Or start your own.

On the twenty-ninth day, the garage was nearly empty. Only one coffee can remained on the sorting table.

Mrs. Odum came for it herself. She looked around the bare concrete and shook her head.

"Seems a shame," she said.

"It isn't," Margarethe said, and found she meant it. A Lutheran church six blocks over had offered its basement — drier, with an actual lease. But that wasn't the point.

Somewhere in Tucson and Leeds and Manila, and in the backyard of a man in Ohio, Grandma Tuttle's bean was putting down roots.

You could not bulldoze that.
