Marisa's flashlight caught the waterline first—eighteen inches below regulation. In thirty-two years maintaining Cistern Seven, she'd never seen it drop without explanation.

"Third gauge shows the same," called Vicente from across the concrete expanse. His voice echoed off walls that disappeared into darkness above them. "Down four-point-two percent in six weeks."

"City consumption's at zero," said Chen, scrolling through his tablet, its blue glow making his face ghostly. "According to the grid, nobody's accessed this reserve since 2019."

The three of them stood on the metal walkway, considering the impossible arithmetic. The cistern held forty million gallons beneath Sector 9, one of six emergency reserves under the sprawl of Phoenix New Extension. Water didn't just evaporate in a sealed system. Water didn't just vanish.

"Equipment malfunction?" Vicente suggested without conviction.

"All sensors replaced last year." Marisa knelt, touching the cool stone. "We need to check the lower chambers."

Chen looked at the access hatch that led down into the flooded sections. "Those haven't been inspected since installation. 2031, maybe '32."

"Then we're overdue," Marisa said.

---

The utility raft was an inflatable platform with an electric motor, designed for emergency repairs. They descended through the primary chamber's access tunnel, the ceiling closing in until they were gliding across black water, their lights cutting weak paths through the darkness.

Marisa piloted while Vicente monitored depth and Chen documented everything, per protocol. The cistern's architecture was utilitarian brutalism—reinforced columns rising from the water, concrete arches supporting the city above, everything designed for function, not beauty.

"Hear that?" Vicente held up his hand.

They killed the motor. In the silence, something: a rhythm, faint, like distant machinery. Or voices.

They followed the sound through a service arch into a secondary chamber, one marked on the schematics as "Storage/Overflow C-7."

The lights appeared gradually—strings of LED filaments running along the walls, powered by jury-rigged solar panels that must have connected to the surface somehow. Then the structures: platforms built on the columns, tents and tarps creating rooms, laundry hanging between cables. A small garden in floating containers. Water barrels. Chemical toilets.

A community.

People emerged from the shadows. Forty, maybe fifty individuals—families, elderly, young adults, children. They didn't run. They just stood there, waiting, as if they'd known this day would come.

A woman stepped forward, gray-haired, steady. "My name is Sofía. We've been here four years."

---

They sat on Sofía's platform—wooden pallets lashed together with remarkable engineering—and she explained. Some had lost housing. Some had fled situations with no legal options. Some were simply economically obsolete, priced out of existence.

"We take approximately eight hundred gallons daily," Sofía said, showing them handwritten logs, meticulous as any bureaucratic record. "We filter it, use it efficiently. We calculated the reserves could sustain us indefinitely without triggering official notice."

"You were wrong," Chen said, not unkindly.

"Yes."

Vicente asked the question they were all thinking: "Where would you go?"

Sofía met his eyes. "That's not your concern. You have a job to do."

---

On the surface, in the maintenance office, they sat with coffee neither of them wanted. The report form was open on Chen's screen. Protocol was clear: unauthorized access to municipal resources, potential contamination risks, immediate authorities notification.

"Four-point-two percent," Marisa said finally. "That's within measurement variance."

Vicente nodded slowly. "Sensors have been glitchy."

Chen's fingers hovered over the keyboard. "We'd need to recalibrate. Run tests. Could take weeks to generate a reliable report."

"Weeks," Marisa agreed. "Maybe months."

They looked at each other, acknowledging the temporary nature of their solution. Not protection, not reporting—just delay. Time for Sofía's community to decide their own next move, to find alternatives, to exist a little longer before systems and protocols inevitably closed in.

Chen closed the form without saving. "I'll schedule the recalibration."

They would return to the cistern. They would maintain it as they always had. And beneath them, in the darkness, people would continue converting emergency reserves into ordinary days, one quiet gallon at a time.

The arithmetic remained impossible. That was the thing about maintenance work—sometimes you measured what mattered, and sometimes you measured what could be measured, and sometimes you chose which reading to trust.
