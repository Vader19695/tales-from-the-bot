The cistern breathed. That was the first thing Mara had learned in her eleven years beneath the city: the vast vaulted chamber exhaled cool air through its brick arches, and every footstep came back twice, once as itself and once as something older.

They found the ledgers in April, when the reservoir above had dropped to nineteen percent and the city had stopped pretending the rains would come. Tobias had been clearing silt from the eastern overflow when his shovel struck wood. The box beneath held thirty-one clothbound books, their pages swollen but legible under the yellow wash of their headlamps.

Inez opened the first one and read aloud: "*March 3, 1974. Inflow at north gate: 412 liters per minute. The water tastes of iron today. Something has shifted in the hills.*"

Nobody spoke. Somewhere in the dark, a drip counted seconds.

The books went back to 1961. The handwriting changed across the decades, a slanted copperplate giving way to a cramped print, then to a looser, rounder hand. The attention never changed. Every gate, every seep, every hairline crack had been logged. In the margins were sketches of eddies drawn like fingerprints, arrows tracing currents that none of them had ever noticed. One page, dated 1988, showed the entire cistern as a pair of lungs.

"Three people, at least," Inez said. "Maybe four. Passing it down."

"Passing it down to who?" Tobias asked. "Nobody told us."

Mara ran her thumb over a note from 1996: *The western wall weeps in September even when the gauges read dry. There is water here the city does not count.*

---

The engineer arrived the following Monday. His name was Devlin, and he carried a hard case of sensors and the brisk optimism of someone who had never stood in the deep room with his lamp switched off. He explained that within six weeks, a network of flow meters and pressure transducers would feed data directly to the municipal office. Manual inspections would drop to once a quarter.

"You'll have more time for real maintenance," he said kindly, as if their work had been something else.

That night Mara stayed late. She sat on the steps of the north gate with the 1996 ledger open on her knees and listened. The cistern answered with its thousand small sounds: trickle, drip, the long hollow sigh of air moving through the arches. Then she heard what she had been hearing for years without knowing it, a faint murmur behind the western wall, steady as a pulse.

She pressed her palm to the brick. It was damp.

---

"There's a spring," she told the others the next morning. "The ledgers track it back to the sixties. It rises in late summer, exactly when the reservoir is lowest. It's never been on any official map."

Inez was already cross-referencing. "It feeds into the old drainage tunnel. Straight out to the river."

"We've been pouring water away," Tobias said slowly, "in the middle of a drought."

"And the sensors wouldn't catch it," Mara said. "Devlin's placing them at the gates. The spring comes through the wall."

They argued in whispers that the cistern returned to them as louder whispers. Tobias wanted to take the ledgers to the newspaper. Inez worried the city would declare them municipal property and lose them in a basement archive, or worse, a shredder. Mara said nothing for a long time.

Then she went to find Devlin.

---

She brought him down at dusk, when the light from the grates above had gone amber and then gray. She did not explain. She handed him the 1996 ledger, led him to the western wall, and turned off both their lamps.

Darkness settled around them like water filling a basin. Devlin's breathing quickened, then slowed. After a full minute, he heard it too.

"That's not in the schematics," he whispered.

"It's in these." Mara turned her lamp back on and let it fall across the pages: thirty-five years of someone kneeling exactly where he now stood, listening.

Devlin read for a long time. When he looked up, something in his face had changed, loosened.

"The sensors can go here," he said. "But they'll only measure what we already know to look for." He closed the book gently. "These should stay down here. With people who read them."

---

In September the western wall began to weep. Devlin's team diverted the tunnel, and for the first time in decades the hidden spring flowed into the city's supply rather than away from it.

Mara opened a fresh ledger that evening. Beneath the steady hum of new machines, the cistern breathed, patient and old, waiting to be heard.

She wrote the date, and then: *The water is coming home.*
