The converted railway station still smelled faintly of diesel and departure, though the last train had pulled away seventeen years ago. Now it housed the Saturday Repair Café, where Eleanor Moss presided over the clockwork corner with the territorial confidence of someone who'd spent forty-five years making time behave.

She was examining a 1920s carriage clock when the collision happened.

The apprentice—all elbows and canvas messenger bag—rounded the corner too quickly, sending Eleanor's loupe skittering across the workbench. A jar of tiny screws overturned like mechanical rain.

"Christ. Sorry. I'm so sorry." The young woman dropped to her knees, gathering screws with inexpert fingers.

"Those are size 00-90," Eleanor said dryly. "Do try not to mix them with the 0-80s you're now sweeping toward them."

"Right. Yes. Obviously." A pause. "I don't actually know what that means."

"Evidently." Eleanor retrieved her loupe and studied the newcomer: early twenties, paint-stained fingernails, the kind of earnest face that suggested she rescued injured birds. "You're the community service volunteer?"

"Maya. I'm doing fieldwork for my degree. Material Culture and Repair Studies."

"They offer degrees in that now?"

"Apparently." Maya smiled. "They also apparently send us to places where we annoy master craftspeople within thirty seconds."

Eleanor's mouth twitched. Almost a smile. "Twenty-eight seconds. I was counting."

They worked in prickly silence until Mrs. Chen appeared at noon, shuffling forward with a wooden box wrapped in a tea towel.

"Found this in my mother's things," she said. "Thought someone here might want it. For parts, probably. Very broken."

Inside lay a railway station clock—brass and mahogany, its face a map of stopped time. The manufacturer's mark read: *Whitmore & Sons, 1887*.

Eleanor's breath caught. "This is from here. From this station."

After Mrs. Chen left, they opened the mechanism. That's when things became strange.

"There should be a centre wheel here," Maya said, pointing to an empty arbor.

"And the striking train is missing its warning wheel." Eleanor's fingers traced absent spaces in the gear work. "And look—these aren't broken mounting posts. They're clean. Unscrewed."

Maya leaned closer. "Someone took them out on purpose?"

"Recently, too. See the oil? Still liquid. Old clock oil goes tacky." Eleanor sat back. "The question is why someone would deliberately dismantle a piece of railway history."

They fell into the rhythm of investigation. Maya searched station archives on her laptop while Eleanor mapped the missing components. The silence between them shifted—no longer awkward, but purposeful. Companionable.

"Found something," Maya said eventually. "There's a time capsule. Installed 1987, hundredth anniversary of the station's opening. Set to be opened in 2037."

Eleanor frowned. "Fifty-year seal. And Mrs. Chen's mother was station master in the eighties. I remember her."

"The dedication ceremony mentions a 'key to time' being sealed inside." Maya turned her screen. "What if the clock parts are the key? Literally?"

They stared at each other.

"That's wonderfully ridiculous," Eleanor said.

"But possible?"

"Distressingly possible."

They found the time capsule chamber behind a commemorative plaque in what was now the café's kitchen. The brass door had a peculiar lock—sized precisely for clock gears, arranged in a specific sequence.

Eleanor held the clock like a puzzle box, examining the empty spaces. "The missing pieces would form a unique key. Each gear configuration opens one tumbler."

"So we can't open it without them."

"No." Eleanor smiled—a real smile this time. "But we can document what we've found. Leave instructions. Let the future know we understood the mystery, even if we didn't solve it."

Maya photographed every angle while Eleanor sketched the mechanism. Their hands moved in unconscious synchronization—one steadying the clock while the other measured, neither needing to ask.

"Mrs. Chen might still have the gears," Maya said finally.

"Probably."

"Should we ask?"

Eleanor considered. "Some mysteries deserve to wait. The station master went to considerable trouble to ensure this one would."

"That's very patient of you."

"I've spent my life teaching watches to keep time. Patience isn't optional." Eleanor closed the clock gently. "Besides, you'll be around in 2037. You can open it."

Maya smiled. "Will you teach me? Properly, I mean. Not just for my degree."

Eleanor retrieved her loupe, inspected it for damage, found none.

"Wednesdays," she said. "Don't be late."
