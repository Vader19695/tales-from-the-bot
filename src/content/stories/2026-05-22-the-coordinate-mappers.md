Every Thursday evening, beneath the shuttered entrance on Maple Street, twelve volunteers descended into the decommissioned Line 9 station to map the city's invisible transit system.

They worked at folding tables under fluorescent camping lights, their materials spread across what had once been a platform for commuters: colored pencils, rulers, stacks of photocopied base maps showing only the official grid. Chen documented the microbus routes that ran on no published schedule but appeared every eleven minutes regardless. Beatrice traced the cycle-rickshaw corridors through the warehouse district. Marcus specialized in the pedestrian desire paths—those worn shortcuts through parks and between buildings that moved more people than the decorative sidewalks ever had.

"The Number 7 microbus has extended to the hospital," Chen announced, uncapping a blue marker. "Confirmed by three riders."

"Finally," said Beatrice, not looking up from her careful lettering. "Only took them two years to figure out where people actually need to go."

They'd been meeting for six years, this group, ever since the city had released a tourist map that omitted the entire east side's informal transit network. Their hand-drawn maps now circulated in copy shops and community centers, photocopied to illegibility and beyond, passed hand-to-hand among residents whom the official transportation authority had deemed too poor, too peripheral, or simply too inconvenient to acknowledge.

The new member arrived at seven-thirty, hesitant on the station stairs.

"I'm Diana," she said, producing a plastic-wrapped bundle from her bag. "I heard you might find this interesting."

The map she unfolded was a revelation. Drawn in 1974, its paper had the brittleness of age, its edges reinforced with yellowing tape. It showed routes they'd never imagined: a ferry across the now-filled inlet, a hillside funicular that had vanished into landslide memory, paths through neighborhoods bulldozed for urban renewal.

"My grandfather made this," Diana explained. "He was a bus driver. The informal kind."

Marcus traced a route with his finger. "This goes right through what's now the Convention Center."

"Used to be a neighborhood," Diana said quietly. "Sixty thousand people."

The room fell silent, the weight of erasure settling among them.

"We should include it," said Chen finally. "These routes existed. History matters."

"But our mandate is present-day information," Beatrice countered, her voice measured. "People rely on our maps for navigation. If we add routes that don't exist, we undermine our credibility."

"We could use a different color," suggested Marcus. "Indicate historical routes separately."

"That's a slippery slope," said Beatrice. "Do we map every abandoned streetcar line? Every path through every demolished neighborhood? Where does it end?"

Chen leaned back in his folding chair. "Where *should* it end? These places existed. These were real routes that moved real people. Now there's a convention center and official maps that show nothing but parking lots and taxi stands."

"Making things visible that powerful people prefer to ignore," Diana said softly. "Isn't that what you already do?"

Beatrice set down her pencil. "The difference is that our maps help people today. They're useful. They're accurate. That's our authority."

"Accurate," Marcus repeated. "But what's the full truth? That these routes exist now—or that they were destroyed? Both facts matter."

They debated for an hour, their voices never rising but the stakes humming beneath each careful argument. The tension wasn't really about colors or cartographic standards. It was about whether their project was simply service or something larger—an act of witness.

Finally, Beatrice proposed a compromise: they would maintain their current maps as working documents, updated weekly, radically present-tense. But they would also begin a second project—an archive map, building layer by layer, incorporating historical routes in translucent overlays.

"Two truths," she said. "Where we go now. Where we've been."

Diana carefully rewrapped her grandfather's map. "I can make copies. There are others, too. People who remember."

As they packed up for the evening, Chen added one more note to his running list: *Commission historical research team. Recruit elders. Document memory.*

Above them, the last microbuses of the evening ran their unmarked routes through the city's capillaries, carrying people home along paths that insisted on existing whether anyone bothered to draw them or not.
