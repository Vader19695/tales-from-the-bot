Marcus held the flashlight steady while Elena photographed the graffiti for the third time. "Got it," she said, adjusting her glasses. "1947. 'Johnny loves Marie.' How romantic that their love will outlast the entire city's memory of them."

"That's us," Marcus said. "Professional rememberers of things nobody cares about."

They'd been mapping the Old City Cistern for four years now—every rusted valve, every maintenance log scrawled on water-stained paper and shoved into junction boxes, every bend in the labyrinth of pipes that kept Riverside Heights from dying of thirst. The city paid them nothing. The university gave them a scanner and wished them luck. Most people didn't know the cistern existed.

Elena's phone buzzed. Then Marcus's. Then both of their radios crackled simultaneously.

"All volunteers exit immediately," the voice said. "Structural failure in Sector Seven."

They looked at each other and ran.

---

The Emergency Operations Center smelled like burnt coffee and panic. On the screens, thermal imaging showed water temperature changes—a crack in the cistern wall, spreading. Thousands of gallons were escaping somewhere into the substrate beneath the Riverside neighborhood.

"We have maybe six hours before it destabilizes the whole block," said City Engineer Patricia Voss, pointing at a digital map. "We need to redirect flow through the secondary channels here and here."

Marcus squinted at the screen. "Those channels don't connect like that."

Voss looked at him as if he'd materialized from thin air. "Who are you?"

"The Cistern History Project," Elena said, stepping forward with their tablet. "We've mapped every pipe down there. Your diagram is wrong. Those secondaries were sealed in 1962 after the Rodriguez administration decided—"

"I don't need a lecture," Voss interrupted. "I need solutions. This is a CAD model built from city records."

"The city records are incomplete," Marcus said. "We've found thirty-seven discrepancies between the official plans and what's actually down there. Pipes that were rerouted but never updated on paper. Whole sections that don't exist anymore."

Voss's expression hardened. "I appreciate your... hobby. But this is an engineering emergency, not a historical society meeting."

Elena pulled up a photograph on her tablet. "This is from Sector Five. See this junction? Your map shows it connects to the main drain. But there's a steel plate here—installed 1978, according to the maintenance note we found wedged behind it. If you try to divert water that way, it'll back up into the cistern instead of draining."

"I have degrees from two universities," Voss said slowly.

"We have four years in the actual tunnels," Marcus replied.

---

The deputy mayor, looking exhausted, held up a hand. "Can you prove any of this? Right now?"

Elena scrolled through their archive. "Every photo is GPS-tagged and time-stamped. Every piece of documentation cross-referenced. We have..." she checked, "8,742 catalog entries."

The room went quiet.

"Show me the sectors around the crack," Voss said finally, her voice tight.

For the next twenty minutes, Elena and Marcus walked them through it. The 1956 expansion that the records called complete but that had been abandoned halfway. The temporary bypass that became permanent. The hand-drawn corrections that Maintenance Supervisor Chen had left in a notebook in 1983, never transferred to official plans.

"Here," Marcus said, pointing. "This is your path. These three channels. They're old, but they're clear. We were just there last month."

Voss studied their photographs, her jaw working. "If you're wrong, homes flood."

"If we're wrong, we're wrong," Elena said. "But we've been right 8,742 times already."

---

The water diverted at 11:47 PM. By dawn, engineers confirmed the flow was holding. Riverside Heights woke up to blocked streets and dire warnings, but dry basements.

Marcus and Elena returned to the cistern the following week. The city had given them actual funding—a modest grant, but real money. Voss had even sent a terse email: "Recommend full digitization of your archive for integration with city systems."

Elena photographed a new piece of graffiti someone had added near Sector Seven: "Thanks, history nerds."

"Well," Marcus said, smiling in the dark. "Someone cares."

"8,743," Elena said, logging it into their catalog. "And counting."

The flashlight beam swept forward, illuminating another passage, another story waiting to be remembered.
