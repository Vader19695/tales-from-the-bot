Ruth Abernathy had reconciled the Harlow Point manifest every Wednesday morning for thirty-seven years. The ritual was simple: tickets sold against the deckhand's headcount, discrepancies flagged in red. In all that time she'd used the red pen perhaps forty times, usually for a child who'd slipped aboard behind a parent's coat.

Last Tuesday's 4:15 crossing showed forty-one tickets sold and thirty-seven heads counted.

She checked the names out of habit, not suspicion. The fourth one down stopped her: Walter Pruitt. She knew Walter. He'd ridden the 7:40 every weekday for twenty years until his hip went, and she'd read his obituary in the *Courier* two weeks ago, sitting at this same desk with her coffee going cold.

She pulled the past month's papers from the recycling bin in the break room and spread them across the chart table. Within twenty minutes she'd matched three more: Eleanor Vasquez, 81. Thomas Greer, 64. Deborah Lind, 52.

The protocol was clear. A manifest irregularity went to the regional office, which would open a fraud inquiry, which would mean a man named Kessler driving up from the city to ask questions in a tone that made deckhands quit. Ruth looked at the four names for a while. Then she put the manifest in her desk drawer and checked next Tuesday's schedule.

---

She boarded the 4:15 in her own coat, not her uniform, and took a seat by the stern rail where she could see the whole passenger deck. The *Cormorant* pulled away from the slip at 4:16, which she noted with mild irritation.

It wasn't hard to find them. Most commuters on the afternoon run sat alone, faces lit by their phones. But near the bow, four people sat in a loose cluster on the long bench, and beside each of them was an empty space they seemed careful not to fill. A man in his seventies had set a folded newspaper on the seat next to him, the way you'd save a place.

Ruth walked forward and sat across from them.

"I'm the superintendent," she said. "I'm not here to cause trouble. But my count's been off four for a while now, and I'd like to understand why."

The man with the newspaper looked at the others. A woman about Ruth's age, with a canvas bag in her lap, answered.

"We meet at the library," she said. "Tuesday grief group. It's mostly sitting in folding chairs and drinking bad coffee." She nodded at the man with the newspaper. "Frank started this. His wife used to ride this crossing to visit her sister. After she died he kept buying two tickets."

"Habit at first," Frank said. "Then it wasn't."

"He told us about it in group," the woman went on. "And Marisol said her father had always wanted to take the ferry and never did. So she bought him a ticket." She shrugged. "Then it was four of us. Sometimes five."

Ruth looked at the empty seats. "Walter Pruitt," she said.

A young man at the end of the bench raised his hand slightly, like a student. "My grandfather. He rode this boat to work for twenty years. He always complained the coffee machine ate his quarters."

"It did," Ruth said. "We replaced it in 2019."

The young man smiled. "He said that was the best thing that ever happened to this town."

They talked for the rest of the crossing. Eleanor Vasquez had been a nurse who never took a vacation. Thomas Greer had been afraid of water his whole life and his daughter thought he might like to finally be brave about it, now that it couldn't hurt him. Deborah Lind's husband just didn't want to sit at home on Tuesdays anymore. The woman with the canvas bag, whose name was June, said the tickets were the cheapest part of grieving she'd found.

"We know it's strange," Frank said. "Paying for people who aren't here."

Ruth considered this. "You're paying for the seat," she said. "The seat's here."

---

The *Cormorant* docked at 4:51, two minutes late. Ruth watched the group walk off together toward the library, Frank tucking his newspaper under his arm.

On Wednesday morning she reconciled the manifest. Forty tickets, thirty-six heads. She took out the red pen, then set it down and picked up a pencil instead. In the margin beside the count, where she'd normally write *DISCREPANCY: REVIEW*, she wrote *4 reserved, accounted for*, and initialed it.

It wasn't in the protocol. But after thirty-seven years she figured she'd earned the right to decide what counted as missing on her own boat, and what counted as found.

Then she pulled up the maintenance log and put in a request to have the bow bench repainted before spring.
