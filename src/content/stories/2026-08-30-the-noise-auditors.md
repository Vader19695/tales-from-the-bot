At 6:47 AM, the auditors arrived at Treatment Station K-9 in their white van, parking in the same spot they'd occupied for three years. Maya carried the Brüel & Kjær Type 2250 sound analyzer. Chen managed the calibration microphone. Rodriguez held the clipboard.

The morning checklist began at the primary clarifiers. Maya positioned the analyzer 1.2 meters from Tank A, exactly where the yellow mark indicated. The digital readout settled: 67.3 decibels, a low mechanical drone at 120 Hz. Expected. Rodriguez logged it with his usual cramped handwriting.

They moved through the facility like monks observing canonical hours. The trickling filters produced their characteristic white noise at 58.1 dB. The aeration basins hummed at 71.4 dB, slightly elevated due to increased bacterial activity—within normal parameters for late autumn. Maya noted everything. Chen verified. Rodriguez recorded.

They spoke little. Speech contaminated the soundscape.

By 9:15 AM, they'd reached the cistern access corridor, a concrete tunnel that descended forty feet beneath the plant. Here, sounds acquired strange properties—echoes layering upon echoes, creating phantom frequencies. Maya had learned to distinguish real phenomena from acoustic artifacts. It was why she'd been hired.

But this morning, something was different.

She held up a closed fist. The team froze. Chen tilted his head. Rodriguez's pen stopped mid-notation.

There—beneath the expected whoosh of water through ceramic pipes, beneath the familiar thrum of distant pumps—was something else. A pattern. Almost rhythmic. Almost deliberate.

Maya activated the analyzer's recording function and crept forward. The sound grew clearer: three low tones, then two high ones, repeating every seventeen seconds. Not mechanical. Too organic. Not biological. Too regular.

"Frequency analysis," Rodriguez whispered, breaking protocol.

Maya checked the display. The sound registered at 82, 87, and 91 Hz for the low tones; 440 and 493 Hz for the high ones—A4 and B4, she realized with a chill. Musical notes. Perfect musical notes, emerging from a water treatment cistern.

Chen was already consulting his manual. "No equipment operates at those frequencies. Nothing in the system."

"Could be resonance," Rodriguez offered. "Water flowing through specific pipe geometries, creating harmonic—"

"For three years, we've measured this corridor," Maya interrupted. "Every day. Same pipes. Same water. Same geometry. This is new."

The sound pulsed again. Three low, two high. In the analyzer's display, the waveform looked almost like handwriting.

Protocol dictated they report anomalies immediately. Fill out Form 17-B. Contact the shift supervisor. Maintain objective distance from phenomena. But protocol assumed anomalies were comprehensible—a failing valve, an obstructed vent, a loose panel vibrating.

Protocol didn't account for music.

Maya made a decision. She disconnected the analyzer from the central database. Chen's eyebrows rose, but he said nothing. Rodriguez closed his clipboard.

"We go deeper," she said.

The access ladder descended into darkness barely penetrated by their headlamps. The musical tones grew louder, clearer, joined now by others—a whole progression, complex and beautiful. Maya felt it in her chest, in her bones. After three years of cataloging the mundane symphony of water and machinery, her ears had become instruments themselves, tuned to extraordinary sensitivity.

At the cistern's base, the sound enveloped them. Maya understood, suddenly, that they'd spent years listening without truly hearing. The plant had always been singing—in the specific pitch of pump six, in the resonant frequency of the eastern filters, in the particular gurgle of settling sediment. They'd reduced it all to numbers, to parameters, to normalcy.

But the world demanded more from its listeners.

Chen reached out, touching the cistern wall. His palm came away wet. Where water droplets fell from a hairline crack in the concrete above, they struck a configuration of pipes that shouldn't exist according to any schematic—a natural xylophone, created by years of corrosion and mineral deposit.

"We report this?" Rodriguez asked.

Maya considered. Report it, and crews would repair the crack, remove the mineral buildup, silence the music. Return the facility to its cataloged, quantified, dead normalcy.

"We listen," she said finally. "First, we listen."

They sat in the darkness as the cistern sang its patient song, and for the first time in three years, heard.
