The cistern had been forgotten for forty years, which Eleanor supposed was about right. Modern plumbing had that effect on collective memory.

She descended the metal ladder behind Marcus, whose farmer's hands moved with surprising delicacy over each rung. Below them, Priya's headlamp cast wild shadows across the stone walls, making the chamber seem to breathe.

"Mind the fourth step," Marcus called up. "It's slippery."

"Everything's slippery," Eleanor muttered, but she appreciated the warning. At seventy-two, her engineering days might be behind her, but her knees remembered every ladder she'd ever climbed.

The cistern was smaller than expected—perhaps twenty feet across—and entirely dry. The irony wasn't lost on any of them. Above ground, the town of Millbrook was entering its third year of drought. Below ground, in a chamber designed to hold water, they'd found something else entirely.

Wooden crates lined the eastern wall, surprisingly intact. Priya had opened the first one yesterday, gasped, and immediately called the others.

"I still can't believe it," Priya said now, carefully lifting a leather-bound journal from the crate. "Who archives their notes in a cistern?"

"Someone who understood that damp destroys paper eventually," Eleanor said, running her hand along the wall, "but not as quickly as fire. These stones would have kept the temperature constant. Relatively dry, once the water was pumped out."

"Paranoid farmers, more likely," Marcus said, though his voice was gentle. "My grandfather used to say the old-timers trusted stone more than banks."

The journal in Priya's hands was dated 1933. Inside, someone named Catherine Millbrook—probably the town founder's daughter—had documented well locations, seasonal rainfall patterns, and crop rotation strategies. The handwriting was cramped but meticulous.

Over the next week, they established a routine. Marcus would arrive at dawn with coffee in a battered thermos. Priya brought archive-quality gloves and acid-free folders she'd ordered with her own money. Eleanor created a cataloguing system that would have made her former engineering firm weep with joy.

They found twenty-three journals spanning 1889 to 1959. Planting calendars. Hand-drawn maps showing underground springs. Detailed accounts of the 1934 drought and how the community had survived it.

"There's a spring marked here," Marcus said on the eighth day, studying a map dated 1912. "Two miles north of the Hendricks property. I didn't know that existed."

"Might not anymore," Eleanor cautioned.

"Might," Marcus agreed. "But worth checking."

That evening, the town council called an emergency meeting. The reservoir was at seventeen percent. Voluntary rationing would become mandatory. Old tensions surfaced quickly—the golf course versus the community garden, the mayor's still-green lawn versus the brown parks.

Marcus didn't attend. Neither did Eleanor or Priya. They sat in the cistern instead, arranging journals in chronological order.

"We should tell them," Priya said finally. "There's information here that could help."

"Pass around hundred-year-old paper to a panicking crowd?" Eleanor raised an eyebrow. "I've seen what people do to historical documents when they think they're looking for treasure maps."

"So we keep it secret?" Marcus's tone was carefully neutral. "Let folks upstairs argue while we sit on solutions?"

"We could make copies," Priya offered. "I could scan everything at the library after hours. It would take weeks, though."

"We don't have weeks," Marcus said.

Eleanor was quiet for a long moment, thinking about Catherine Millbrook and whoever else had carefully preserved these records. They'd hidden knowledge to protect it, but protection without purpose was just hoarding.

"We share it," she said finally. "But we control how. Priya scans the critical pages—water sources, survival strategies. Marcus, you identify which locations are worth investigating. I'll present the findings to the council as engineering recommendations."

"And the originals?" Priya asked.

"Stay here. Protected. Where they've always been."

Marcus smiled. "You're proposing we become a secret society of librarian-farmers?"

"Retired engineer-librarian-farmer," Eleanor corrected. "Get it right."

The next morning, Marcus drove his truck toward the coordinates from the 1912 map. Priya began her scanning marathon. Eleanor drafted her report.

In the cistern below the dying town, twenty-three journals waited in the cool darkness, their pages finally breathing again after decades of silence, their words rising like water from a deep well, ready to sustain whoever had wisdom enough to drink.
