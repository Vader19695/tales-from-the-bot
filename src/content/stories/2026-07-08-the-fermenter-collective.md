The lights went out at 2:47 PM, just as Mira was explaining the difference between *Aspergillus oryzae* and *Aspergillus flavus* to a group that wasn't particularly interested in mold taxonomy.

"Generator?" asked Chen, the retired chemist, already on his feet.

"Broken," said Mira. "Repairman's two hours out, maybe three."

In the converted railway depot that housed the Northside Fermentation Cooperative, this was a problem. The temperature-controlled rooms held 3,000 jars of active ferments—kombucha mothers bubbling for the Saturday market, kimchi destined for the food bank, koji colonizing rice for six local restaurants that had pooled resources after the pandemic shuttered their suppliers.

"How long before things spoil?" This from Dae, who came to practice English and stayed for the free samples.

Mira checked her phone. "In this heat? The cold room will hold maybe four hours. The warm room—we've got less."

Somewhere in the building, a teenager groaned. That would be Sophie, who'd started volunteering to pad her college applications and discovered she actually cared about mycology. "The koji rice is at day two. If the temperature spikes—"

"It's trash," finished Marcus, the chef. He'd been quiet since his restaurant closed, mostly sanitizing equipment and avoiding conversation. "Enzymes denature. You'd know if you'd ever run a kitchen."

"I'm seventeen."

"Then learn something."

Chen interrupted before it could escalate. "We move the jars. Cold room first—those go to the loading dock, north side. It's concrete, stays cool. Warm room is trickier."

"The old ticket office," said Marcus, surprising everyone. "Those big windows face east. They're hot in the morning, but afternoon? Cross-breeze. I've been watching them."

Mira nodded. "Let's move."

What followed wasn't heroic. It was repetitive, careful, and occasionally disgusting.

They formed a line—Chen and Marcus in the cold room, passing jars to Sophie and Dae, who ferried them to where Mira organized placement on the loading dock. The kombucha SCOBYs sloshed in their sweet tea, gelatinous and alien. The kimchi jars were heavy, fragrant with garlic and gochugaru.

"Your technique is wrong," Chen told Marcus after the fifth jar exchange. "You're lifting with your back."

"I've lifted stock pots heavier than you."

"And you'll have the spine of a shrimp by fifty."

Marcus adjusted his stance.

In the second hour, they relocated the koji trays—hundreds of them, rice covered in white-green fuzz that looked like felt and smelled like sweet grain alcohol. Sophie sneezed violently. "I think I'm allergic to my own activism."

"This isn't activism," Marcus said, but his voice had lost its edge. "It's just work."

"Work that feeds people," Dae said carefully, arranging syllables. "That is... política, yes?"

"Politics," Sophie corrected gently.

"Yes. Politics."

The sun angled through the old ticket office windows exactly as Marcus had predicted. Chen produced a thermometer from somewhere and monitored the koji obsessively. Mira rotated jars, checking lids, redistributing heat. Marcus explained enzyme conversion to Sophie, who actually listened. Dae asked questions that turned out to be surprisingly precise—he'd fermented gochujang with his grandmother in Busan.

When the power returned at 6:15 PM, they'd been sitting on the loading dock for twenty minutes, drinking kombucha straight from the brewing vessels like communal wine.

"Is it supposed to taste like foot?" Sophie asked.

"That's how you know it's working," Chen said.

They moved everything back. The koji was fine—better than fine, according to Mira's inspection. The kimchi was unperturbed. The kombucha mothers pulsed gently in their jars, oblivious to the crisis they'd survived.

As they locked up, Marcus hesitated. "I've got kitchen space Thursday mornings. If anyone wants to learn to work with koji."

"I have school," Sophie said. "But I could skip calculus."

"Don't skip calculus," Chen and Marcus said simultaneously.

Dae nodded. "I come. For English."

"Sure," Marcus said. "For English."

They left separately, as neighbors do, carrying the smell of fermentation on their clothes—that sour-sweet funk of transformation, of things becoming what they're meant to be, given time and the right conditions.
