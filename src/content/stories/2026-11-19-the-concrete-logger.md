The first anomaly appeared in Borehole 14, at a depth of 38.2 meters, in a run of gray biotite gneiss that should have told Mara Vell nothing more interesting than its compressive strength.

She was logging core at the staging yard behind the Harborline Transit site, wooden boxes laid out in rows under a tarp that snapped in the wind off the bay. Standard procedure: measure recovery, calculate rock quality designation, note fractures, weathering, mineralogy. She had done it ten thousand times. She wet the core with a spray bottle to bring out the foliation and saw, running along the banding like a seam of mica, a line of incised marks.

At first she thought they were drill chatter, the bit skipping. But chatter is irregular. These were regular. Under the hand lens they resolved into characters a fraction of a millimeter tall, cut cleanly into crystal faces with no visible tool marks, no heat fracturing, no stress halos.

They were letters. English.

*Did you lock the back door. I think so. You think so or you did.*

Mara set the lens down. She wrote in the margin of her field log, *38.2 m — surface marking, origin unknown*, and then sat very still for a long time.

---

Over the next nine days she pulled every box from Boreholes 11 through 22. She told the site supervisor she was rechecking RQD values because of inconsistencies with the geophysical survey, which was true enough to say aloud.

The inscriptions were everywhere below about thirty meters. Not random. Spatially coherent. Core from beneath the old cannery district held arguments about wages and a marriage proposal made in a parking lot. Core from beneath the hospital held lines so dense she had to photograph them with the microscope camera and stitch the images together: *Her pressure's dropping. Call it. No, give her another minute.* Then, a few centimeters lower: *Time of death 3:41.*

She cross-referenced what she could. A city council vote from 2019, recorded in the rock beneath City Hall, matched the public minutes word for word, but the rock also contained what the councilmembers said in the hallway afterward. A bakery on Fenwick Street, demolished two years ago, appeared in the core as a slow accumulation of orders and small talk: *Same as always. Same as always. How's your mother.*

The writing was not old. That was the part that kept her awake. Using the depth of overlying sediment and the known building dates, she estimated the inscriptions were continuous and current. The rock beneath the city was being written on now, in real time, by something that left no trace of itself except the text.

She found her own kitchen in Borehole 19. Her apartment sat four blocks east of the alignment, but the record extended laterally through the rock. *Did you lock the back door.* Her husband's voice, rendered in gneiss. Her own answer.

---

The report sat drafted on her laptop. She had followed procedure even in that: objective language, photographic appendices, recommended actions. *Immediate suspension of tunneling between Stations 4 and 9. Notification of the state geological survey. Independent verification.*

She knew what would follow. The project would halt. Then would come the scientists, and the cameras, and the realization by two million people that every word they had spoken in this city, every whispered thing, was preserved beneath their feet and could be extracted with a diamond bit. There would be lawsuits over who owned it. Police would want warrants for core. Someone would want to drill for proof of an affair, a confession, a promise.

And the tunnel boring machine, scheduled to launch in March, would grind through it all regardless, a six-meter cutterhead turning the record of a city into slurry.

She thought about the bakery. *Same as always.* Whatever was doing this was not surveilling. Surveillance selects. This kept everything, the mundane with the terrible, with no apparent preference at all. It was closer to tenderness than to watching. It was a ledger kept by someone who believed none of it should be lost.

---

On the eleventh day, Mara deleted the draft. She opened a new file and wrote: *Boreholes 11–22: rock mass highly fractured below 30 m, RQD <25%. Unsuitable for TBM tunneling. Recommend realignment north to Option C.* She attached fracture photographs from a different site, a quarry job from years ago. She signed it.

Then she drove back to the yard, alone, after dark, and ran the small portable rig over Borehole 14 one last time. Ten meters. Twenty. Thirty-eight point two.

She wet the fresh core and bent close with the lens.

*Mara Vell, 4:12 p.m., told her first lie in twenty-two years of professional practice.*

And below it, smaller, in a hand she had not seen before:

*Kept.*
