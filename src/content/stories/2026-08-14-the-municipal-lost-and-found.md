Marjorie had worked at the Central Lost and Found for seventeen years, long enough to know that most items told the same story: carelessness, haste, the minor tragedies of daily life. She'd stopped being sentimental about umbrellas and winter scarves sometime around year three.

The journal appeared during the annual purge of decade-old items. Weathered brown leather, held together with a fraying elastic band. Inside: recipes in careful cursive, sketches of layered cakes and braided loaves, margin notes about oven temperatures and the temperament of yeast.

She nearly tossed it with the others. Then she noticed the handwriting.

Marjorie had lived in the same neighborhood for forty years. She'd bought bread at Lindstrom's Bakery every Saturday morning until it closed in 2008. The handwriting matched the signs Mrs. Lindstrom used to post in the window: "Cinnamon Rolls—Saturday Only" and "Thank You for 30 Years."

She checked the intake log. The journal had been found on a crosstown bus in 2009, a few months after Elsa Lindstrom's funeral. Marjorie remembered the funeral—she'd sent flowers.

This was foolish, she told herself. The woman was dead. Her shop was now a mobile phone store. But Marjorie found herself photocopying the intake form and slipping the journal into her bag that evening.

The next morning, she called the bakery supply company whose number appeared on old invoices she'd dug up online. After three transfers, she reached someone who remembered the Lindstroms.

"Two kids," the man said. "Eric and Susan. Haven't heard those names in years. They scattered after Elsa died—neither wanted the business."

The county clerk's office gave her a last known address for Eric Lindstrom in Portland. She sent a letter, careful and factual: she'd found what appeared to be his mother's recipe journal, would he like to claim it?

Three weeks passed. She'd almost forgotten when the email arrived.

"Ms. Chen—I showed your letter to my sister. Neither of us knew this journal existed. Mom wasn't... she didn't share that kind of thing with us. We'd like to see it, if you're willing. We're both coming to town next month for what would have been her birthday. Susan."

Marjorie suggested meeting at a café near where the bakery used to be. She arrived early, the journal wrapped in brown paper, and ordered coffee she didn't drink.

They were in their fifties now, these children. Susan had her mother's broad shoulders. Eric had the same careful way of moving Marjorie remembered from when he'd worked the counter as a teenager.

She slid the journal across the table without ceremony.

Susan opened it slowly. Eric leaned in. They turned pages in silence, occasionally pausing to point at something, to share a small recognition. Marjorie watched their faces shift—surprise, then something deeper.

"This one," Eric finally said, touching a sketch of an elaborate wedding cake. "This was for my wedding. She made it, but she never showed me the planning. I didn't know she'd drawn it out like this."

"Look at the notes on the cardamom bread," Susan said quietly. "That's what she made when she was homesick. She never told us that."

They stayed for two hours. Marjorie told them what little she knew—where it had been found, when it had come in. She didn't embellish. The facts were enough.

When they stood to leave, Susan held the journal carefully, like something breakable.

"Thank you," she said. "Not just for finding us. For thinking it mattered."

"It's my job," Marjorie said, though it wasn't, not really. Not this part.

Walking back to the depot, she thought about all the items waiting on those shelves. Most were just things—replaceable, forgettable. But some carried the weight of a person's private hours, the parts of themselves they'd kept separate even from those they'd loved.

She couldn't save them all. But she could pay attention.

At her desk, she pulled out the log of unclaimed items and began reading more carefully, looking for the ones that might still have someone waiting, someone who didn't yet know what they'd lost.
