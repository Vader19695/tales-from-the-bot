The tomatoes started as a joke. Most things at the Fennick Street Depot did.

Dolores Ruiz, who had been rebuilding transmissions on the night shift for nineteen years, brought in a seedling in a coffee can and set it on the roof above Bay Six because, she said, "somebody in this building should be growing instead of breaking down." That was four summers ago. Now there were thirty-one oil drums up there, cut in half and scrubbed until they no longer smelled like a refinery, plus a terraced arrangement of shipping pallets that Benny Okafor insisted on calling "the vertical program."

There were tomatoes, peppers, a stubborn row of collards, marigolds to keep the bugs honest, and one pumpkin vine named Gerald that had ambitions toward the ventilation stack.

The crew rotated: Dolores, Benny, Teo Marsh, and Ray Kowalczyk, who had never said more than eleven words in a row but who watered at 3 a.m. with the tenderness of a man handling a newborn. Between brake jobs and coolant flushes, they climbed the maintenance ladder and sat among the drums with their thermoses, looking at the city's grey sprawl and the one patch of it that was theirs.

Then came the auditor.

Her name was Marguerite Pell, and she arrived with a tablet, a lanyard, and the expression of someone who had personally found waste in a hospice. The depot had been "underperforming relative to regional benchmarks," a phrase Benny repeated in a robot voice until Dolores threw a rag at him.

Pell found the garden on her third night. She had been following an unexplained draw on the water meter.

"This," she said, standing among the collards, "is a liability."

"It's a salad," said Teo.

"It's unapproved load on a structural roof, a trip hazard, a potential drainage obstruction, and," she glanced at Gerald, now wrapped around the vent housing, "possibly an HVAC violation. I'll need it removed by the end of the month."

She left. The crew stood in the dark. Somewhere below, a bus idled like a sigh.

They argued for a week. Teo wanted to fight: petitions, the union, a local news story with the headline *Bus Mechanics' Garden Feeds Their Souls*. Benny wanted to hide it, possibly with tarps, possibly with camouflage netting he claimed he could "acquire." Dolores said nothing, which worried everyone, because Dolores always said something.

It was Ray who went to see Pell. Nobody knew until afterward. He walked into her temporary office, a converted parts closet, holding a paper bag of tomatoes, set it on her desk, and said, by Benny's later reconstruction, his longest speech on record:

"Roof's rated for twice this. I checked the drawings. The drums sit on the beams. Water's from the rain barrels, mostly. I'll fix the meter thing."

Then he left.

Pell came back up to the roof the next night. She had a measuring tape this time, and the structural drawings, and a flashlight she clicked on and off with the irritation of someone who hates being wrong and hates being right even more.

"He's correct about the beams," she said finally. "Mostly."

"Mostly," Dolores repeated.

"The six drums by the parapet go. They're too close to the edge, and I don't want to explain to anyone why a pepper plant killed a mechanic. The pallets need to be anchored. And the pumpkin," she pointed her flashlight at Gerald, "cannot be in a relationship with the ventilation system."

"And the rest?"

Pell looked at her tablet for a long moment. "There is no line item for this," she said. "Which means, technically, it doesn't exist in my report. Things that don't exist in my report don't get recommended for removal." She paused. "That will not be true forever. The next auditor may have a different spreadsheet."

"So it's temporary," said Teo.

"Everything here is temporary," Pell said. "I've seen your fleet."

They moved the six drums down to the ground that weekend and gave the plants away: to the dispatcher, to the day-shift janitor, to a driver who'd been talking about her late mother's garden. They anchored the pallets. Ray spent two nights gently unwinding Gerald from the vent and coaxing him along a trellis made of a decommissioned wheelchair ramp.

The garden was smaller after that. Nobody pretended otherwise.

But in late August, Dolores found a note taped to the ladder, written in precise, unhappy handwriting: *Regional office has asked about "unexplained produce" in break room. I have described it as a morale initiative. Do not make me regret this.*

Beneath it, someone had left an empty paper bag, folded neatly, waiting to be filled.

Dolores filled it. Gerald, for once, behaved.
