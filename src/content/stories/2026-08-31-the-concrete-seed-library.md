The fluorescent lights in the basement hummed like wasps trapped in a jar. Margaret pressed another label onto a mason jar filled with pale squash seeds, her handwriting still librarian-precise after fifteen years of retirement. "Cherokee Tan," she wrote, "donated by Mrs. Chen, three doors down from the old Woolworth's."

"Someone's coming." Devi looked up from her phone, eyes wide. "My mom just texted. Some guy in a suit asking questions upstairs."

The seed library occupied what had been storage space for Brennan's Department Store, back when the store sold things people actually wanted to buy. Now the racks upstairs held mostly clearance items and quiet desperation, while down here, Margaret and her three teenage volunteers had built something else entirely: row upon row of carefully catalogued seeds, each jar a small act of defiance against the Monsanto patents and sterile hybrids that had swallowed up the local nurseries.

"Inspector?" Jamal closed his laptop, where he'd been updating their database. At sixteen, he had the methodical soul of an archivist.

"Probably." Margaret's hands didn't shake, though she felt the tremor inside her chest. "The landlord's been trying to sell to Meridian Development for months."

Keisha was already moving toward the back exit, her usual flight response kicking in. She'd joined them six months ago, sullen and court-ordered for community service. Now she handled the heirloom tomatoes like they were made of glass.

"Wait." Margaret stood, joints protesting. "We should talk about this."

"Talk about what?" Keisha's voice pitched high. "They'll shut us down. Fine us, probably."

"Maybe." Margaret looked around the basement—at the hand-built shelving Jamal's father had helped construct, at the germination station where Devi tested viability rates, at the lending log that had grown from a dozen names to over two hundred. "Or maybe we explain."

"Explain what?" Devi asked. "That we're running an unlicensed operation? That these seeds aren't FDA approved or whatever?"

"That Mrs. Chen's husband brought those squash seeds from Taiwan in 1983, carried them in his pocket through customs," Margaret said quietly. "That Keisha's saving pepper seeds from her great-grandmother's garden. That we're not criminals—we're librarians."

Footsteps on the stairs. They all froze.

The inspector was younger than Margaret expected, maybe thirty, with a tablet and an expression of practiced boredom. Behind him, Mr. Brennan wrung his hands apologetically.

"This is the disputed space," Brennan said. "I told them it was just storage—"

The inspector's eyes swept the room, cataloguing violations. "Unlicensed commercial operation, improper storage of agricultural materials, possible health code—"

"It's a library," Jamal interrupted, his voice surprisingly steady. "We don't sell anything. People borrow seeds, grow food, bring back seeds from what they grew. Just like borrowing books."

"Without permits, without inspection, without—" The inspector paused, looking at a jar of beans. "Cherokee Trail of Tears. My grandmother grew these."

The humming fluorescents filled the silence.

"Haven't seen them in years," he continued, softer. "She died when I was twelve. I thought... I thought they were just gone."

Margaret watched his face change, bureaucratic certainty cracking like spring soil. "You can have some," she said. "That's the point. Seeds want to be shared."

"I can't just—" He looked at his tablet, then back at the jars. Hundreds of varieties, each one a story, a connection, a memory refusing to die. "This isn't properly zoned."

"No," Margaret agreed. "It isn't."

"But if you were operating as an educational nonprofit, with the proper paperwork..." He met her eyes. "That might be different. That might take a few months to process."

Keisha breathed out slowly. Devi reached for Jamal's hand.

"I'll need documentation," the inspector said, already moving toward the stairs. "Fire safety, occupancy limits, the whole thing. Real documentation."

"We'll have it," Margaret promised.

At the door, he paused. "Can I really...?"

She handed him the jar of beans. "Bring some back next year. That's how it works."

After he left, they stood together in the humming quiet, four people who'd planted something worth defending.

"Nonprofit paperwork," Jamal said. "I'll start researching."

"I'll handle fire safety," Devi added.

Keisha looked at Margaret. "You knew he'd help us."

"No," Margaret admitted. "But I knew we had to try. That's what community means. Trusting it might catch you."

She picked up her pen and reached for another jar, continuing the careful work of preservation, one seed at a time.
