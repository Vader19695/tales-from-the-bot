The forecast called it a "significant rain event," which Harold said was the kind of phrase you used when you didn't want to be quoted later.

By nine that night, water was already sheeting off the roof of the shipping container and pooling at the low end of the Alder Street Community Garden, where the container had been dropped eleven years ago by a crane operator who, Dele maintained, had never seen a slope in his life.

All five of them came. Nobody had called anyone. They just showed up in raincoats, holding flashlights, looking faintly embarrassed to see each other.

Inside, the container smelled the way it always did: garlic, vinegar, the sour-bread breath of the rye kvass, and under it the low funk of Priya's fish sauce, which had its own shelf and, by unanimous vote, its own lid weighted with a brick. Two hundred and some jars lined the walls. Crocks squatted on the floor. A strip of masking tape on every lid carried a date, an initial, and, in Harold's handwriting, a pH reading to one decimal place.

Water was already licking the door sill.

"Okay," Harold said. "Triage. Everyone takes their own priority items to their cars. Highest value first."

"Value how?" said Mina, who was sixteen and had six jars of fermented habanero mash she intended to sell at the farmers' market as Mina's Revenge.

"Irreplaceability. Time invested."

Priya was already lifting her crock of kimchi, then stopped. "I can carry maybe four things. That leaves two hundred."

"The floor's going to go," Dele said. He was still in his postal jacket, rain dripping off the reflective strip. "It always floods at that corner because the storm drain on Alder clogs. Leaves from the Kowalskis' maple. I walk past it every day."

"So we lose the floor," Harold said.

"So we lose the garden. The beds drain this way. The water backs up, the beds go under, the garlic rots in the ground. Then there's nothing to ferment next year anyway."

Nobody said anything. Rain drummed on the steel roof like someone counting out change.

Ruth, who had been rinsing a funnel at the utility sink as if it were a normal Tuesday, turned off the tap. "Dele, how long to clear the drain?"

"Twenty minutes, with a rake. Faster with two."

"Mina, go with him. Priya, Harold, and I will move everything up. Top shelves first, then the potting benches. We stack crocks on the pallets."

Harold opened his mouth to object and then, visibly, did a sum in his head. He closed it again.

They made a line. Priya passed jars from the floor to Harold, who passed them to Ruth on the stepladder, who wedged them in among the dried chiles and the canning rings. Their hands got slick with brine from a sauerkraut lid that had been burping all week. Harold called out labels as they went, out of habit—"Beets, R., March fourteenth, three-point-eight"—until Priya started answering, "Copy," like a ship's officer, and he laughed despite himself.

"Why do you even do this?" Priya asked him, handing up a jar of preserved lemons. "Really."

"Forty years of numbers that belonged to other people," Harold said. "These are mine. They go up or down, and I know why." He paused. "You?"

"I spend all week measuring reefs that die," she said. "Here, I add salt and something stays alive on purpose."

Ruth said nothing. Harold noticed she had no jars of her own on any shelf. She never had. She washed things, labeled things, mediated the great Kombucha Dispute of last August. He'd assumed she just liked the smell.

Outside, Mina's flashlight bobbed by the street. Dele's voice carried: "Not that way—pull toward you."

The water came through the door at ten-fifteen, an inch of it, then three. Then it stopped rising. Then, slowly, with a sound like a bathtub draining, it went down.

Mina and Dele came back soaked to the hips, holding a garbage bag of wet maple leaves like a trophy.

"I put my hot sauce on the bottom," Mina said, looking at the floor. "It's sealed. It's acidic. It's the toughest thing in here."

Harold checked. Every jar of Mina's Revenge was intact, sitting in a skim of mud.

They lost one crock: Priya's fish sauce, the brick knocked off by Ruth's ladder, the lid tipped, rainwater in it. Priya stared at it for a while.

"Fourteen months," she said. Then: "Honestly, it smelled worse than it should have."

At midnight they sat on overturned buckets, passing a jar of Ruth-labeled-but-Dele-made dilly beans, crunching.

"You never make anything," Harold said to Ruth.

She speared a bean. "I spend all day with people whose lives have been knocked over," she said. "I like a room where things go bad slowly, and five people show up to stop it."

Harold wrote it down in the log, under the date: *Flood. One crock lost. All hands present.*

He didn't record a pH. It didn't seem like the important number.
