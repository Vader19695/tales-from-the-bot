Dr. Elena Vasquez noticed the discrepancy on a Tuesday morning, when frost still clung to the windows of the Svalbard annex facility. The label on drawer C-47 read *Triticum aestivum*—common wheat, accession 1923—but the seeds inside were wrong. Too small, too dark, the hull pattern distinctive of *Hordeum vulgare*. Barley.

Her hand trembled as she checked the neighboring drawers. Drawer C-48 contained what should have been in C-47. C-49 held what belonged in C-51. The pattern continued like dominoes falling in slow motion through her consciousness.

Someone had switched them. Deliberately.

Elena sat back on the rolling stool, the climate-controlled air suddenly too cold against her skin. She'd worked alone in this remote research annex for three years, cataloging and verifying heritage varieties for the main vault in Norway. The work was solitary, precise, vital. These seeds represented humanity's agricultural inheritance—varieties that had fed civilizations, survived famines, adapted across centuries. If their identities were lost, so was everything they represented.

She spent the next six hours checking every drawer in Section C. Forty-three varieties had been compromised. The swaps were careful, subtle. Someone who knew enough to make the sabotage look like clerical errors rather than intentional chaos.

By evening, Elena stood before the phone in her office, her supervisor's number half-dialed. The Svalbard Trust operated on faith—faith that nations would deposit their seed heritage, that scientists would maintain integrity, that the archive would endure beyond wars and climate catastrophe. One whisper of intentional contamination and that faith would shatter. Depositors would withdraw their collections. Funding would evaporate. The entire project could collapse.

And she would be the obvious suspect. The only person with daily access, working in isolation, no witnesses to vouch for her whereabouts or actions.

Elena deleted the number and sat in the darkness.

She began her reconstruction at midnight, when she should have been sleeping. The seeds themselves held clues—morphology, size, color, texture. She had her field notes, photographs, her own meticulous records kept parallel to the official database. If the saboteur had relied on the official labels alone, her private documentation might provide the thread back to truth.

Three days passed. Then five. Elena stopped showering regularly, ate protein bars at her microscope, slept in ninety-minute increments between verification sessions. The patterns emerged slowly. Each swapped variety came from a different country, but all were drought-resistant or salt-tolerant—seeds bred over generations to survive harsh conditions. Seeds that would be priceless as climate change accelerated.

On the sixth day, she found the note.

It was tucked behind drawer C-23, written on the back of an old requisition form in handwriting she recognized. Dr. Marcus Webb, her predecessor, who'd retired eight months before her arrival.

*They'll privatize this. Patent it. Lock it away from the people who bred these seeds across centuries. Better chaos than corporate ownership. Better lost than stolen.*

Elena read it three times, then burned it in the steel sink.

She understood his fear. Already, genetic engineering firms circled seed banks like sharks, filing patent claims on naturally evolved traits. Marcus had seen preservation work twisted into extraction, had watched traditional farmers sued for planting their ancestors' seeds.

But he was wrong. Destruction wasn't protection. Chaos didn't preserve anything.

Elena worked through the night, through the next day and the one after, until all forty-three varieties were restored. She verified each identity three times, updated every database entry, and added additional security protocols to flag any future changes. Then she wrote a detailed incident report describing the discovered "clerical errors" and her systematic correction of them, carefully attributing nothing to malice.

She never mentioned Marcus Webb's name.

When she finally submitted the report, Elena stood at the window watching the Arctic sun hover at the horizon. These seeds would outlive her, outlive everyone. That was the whole point—to pass something forward into an uncertain future, to trust that preservation itself was an act of faith.

Even when faith was complicated. Even when it required uncomfortable silence.

The phone on her desk rang. Her supervisor, probably. Elena let it ring twice more before answering, already knowing what she would say, already carrying the weight of what she'd chosen to protect.
