The warehouse sprawled like a metal cathedral on the city's eastern edge, filled with the accumulated loneliness of a thousand interrupted journeys. Margaret had worked there for thirty-seven years, long enough to develop theories about what the abandoned contents revealed about their owners. The tennis rackets were optimists. The wedding dresses were romantics. The taxidermy owls were beyond her understanding.

"Another plant shipment," called Dev, wheeling a cart stacked with luggage toward the sorting area. The seventeen-year-old had been working there since June, methodically funneling every paycheck toward his college fund.

Margaret peered over her bifocals. "That's the fourth batch this week."

"Fifth," corrected Ernest, emerging from between towers of unclaimed belongings. The retired botanist had taken the job six months ago, claiming his pension needed supplementing, though Margaret suspected he simply missed having somewhere to be each day. He opened the nearest suitcase, revealing nestled packets of seeds, each labeled in different languages. "These are from Bhutan. And these"—he touched them reverently—"medicinal herbs from Madagascar."

Over the following days, they catalogued an astonishing collection. Suitcases from Amsterdam held tulip bulbs. A trunk from Kyoto contained bonsai saplings wrapped in damp cloth. There were heirloom tomato seeds from Italy, protea cuttings from South Africa, rare orchids from Thailand, all abandoned in transit, unclaimed by owners who'd perhaps missed connections or changed plans.

"Processing says they're scheduling everything for incineration next week," Dev announced one morning, reading from his phone. "Company policy—perishables can't be resold."

Margaret watched Ernest's face fall. In six months, she'd never seen him look so deflated.

"That's not right," she said quietly. "These traveled thousands of miles. Someone cared enough to bring them."

"What can we do?" Dev asked. "It's not like we can plant them in our apartments."

Margaret thought of her neighborhood, blocks of forgotten lots where buildings had been demolished years ago, leaving squares of cracked earth and scrubby weeds. She'd walked past them for decades.

"Maybe we can," she said.

Ernest looked up sharply. "You're serious?"

"Completely unreasonable," she agreed. "But also serious."

They developed their plan during lunch breaks, speaking in hushed voices near the loading dock. Ernest would determine what could realistically grow in their climate. Dev would scout locations and handle the physical labor. Margaret, who knew the warehouse's rhythms better than anyone, would orchestrate the "inventory discrepancies."

The first night, they smuggled out three boxes of supplies. Dev had borrowed his brother's pickup truck. They worked under streetlights in an abandoned lot on Seventh Street, Ernest directing placement while Dev dug and Margaret read instructions from her phone's flashlight.

"These sunflowers should tower over the fence by August," Ernest explained, patting soil around stems. "And the wildflower mix—it'll attract pollinators."

"Assuming we don't get arrested," Dev muttered, but he was grinning.

Over three weeks, they transformed seven lots. The Bhutanese seeds became a meditation garden. The Italian heirloom tomatoes lined a former parking lot. The South African proteas made a dramatic statement beside an old community center. They worked after closing, three unlikely conspirators united by rescue mission.

"Did you see Mrs. Chen's face?" Dev asked one night, describing how their elderly neighbor had discovered the garden on Maple Street. "She was crying. Said it reminded her of her mother's garden in Taipei."

Margaret felt something shift in her chest. Thirty-seven years of cataloguing loss, and now—finally—restoration.

On their final night, they planted the orchids in a small lot behind the public library, creating a greenhouse effect with salvaged windows. Ernest handled each plant with the tenderness of reunion.

"You know what's funny?" Dev said, stepping back to admire their work. "These all got left behind, and now they're exactly where they need to be."

Margaret looked at Ernest, whose retirement had felt like exile, and at Dev, who'd found something worth more than wages. She thought about herself, discovering purpose in the final chapters.

"Maybe none of us were really unclaimed," she said. "Just waiting for the right connection."

The orchids glowed pale in the darkness, rooted at last, beginning their patient bloom.
