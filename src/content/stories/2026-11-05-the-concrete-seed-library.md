The Fulton Street Municipal Garage had been half-empty for six years, ever since the department store above it closed. Ines Halloran parked there anyway, on Level P3, because it was free after six and because at seventy-one she liked the long walk up the ramps. It reminded her of fieldwork.

She found the boy on a Tuesday, crouched beside pillar C-14 with a can of cobalt spray paint and a guilty face.

"I didn't do that," he said, before she'd said anything at all.

He was pointing at a small stenciled line near the pillar's base: *41.4993 / -81.6944*. Beneath it was a hairline seam in the concrete, a perfect rectangle no bigger than a paperback book.

"I believe you," Ines said, and knelt, which took some time. She pressed the rectangle. It gave with a soft click and swung outward on a hidden hinge.

Inside, nested in a cavity lined with waxed cork, sat rows of glass vials. Each was labeled in tiny, careful handwriting. *Asclepias syriaca. Echinacea pallida. Andropogon gerardii.* Milkweed, coneflower, big bluestem. Prairie plants, the kind that had covered this ground before anyone poured a foundation.

The boy leaned in. "Is that... seeds?"

"Native seeds," Ines said. Her voice had gone strange. "Collected properly. Dried properly. Somebody knows exactly what they're doing."

His name was Dev. He was sixteen and painted the garage walls because, he said, nobody else ever looked at them. By the end of the week, he was looking at them with her.

They found eleven vaults on Fulton Street alone. Each pillar wore the same stenciled numbers, but the coordinates were always different. Dev plugged them into his phone. Every one pointed to a vacant lot somewhere in the city: the burned-out laundromat on Kinsman, the fenced gravel square behind the old tire plant, the strip of dirt beneath the Route 2 overpass.

"It's a map," Dev said. "It's a map of every dead place."

Over the next month they rode buses to other garages. The Hawley Avenue structure had nine vaults. The hospital deck had fourteen. The abandoned airport lot, reached through a gap in the chain-link that Dev knew about and Ines chose not to ask about, had thirty-two. By November they had counted more than two hundred, spread across twenty-six parking structures, every one hidden in the gray places where the city kept its cars and forgot about everything else.

Some vaults had notes. Most were a single line: *Cold-stratify sixty days.* *Sow before the first thaw.* Once, folded into the airport vault, they found *Nothing here is wasted. Not even this.*

"We should tell somebody," Ines said one evening, sitting on the ramp at Fulton with a thermos of tea between them. "The city. Facilities. It's technically vandalism. Structural, possibly."

Dev turned a vial of milkweed in the sodium light. "And then what? They seal the pillars. They throw these out. Somebody spent years on this."

"Somebody might be in trouble. Might be ill. Might need help."

"Or," Dev said, "they're waiting for someone to notice."

Ines thought about her career, forty years of grant applications and herbarium drawers, of watching tallgrass remnants shrink to roadside slivers while committees debated. She thought about how little of it had ever reached the ground.

"The first thaw," she said slowly, "is about ten weeks off."

They didn't decide out loud. They just started. Ines cold-stratified seeds in her refrigerator crisper drawers until there was no room left for vegetables. Dev made a spreadsheet, then laminated maps. In March, when the ground went soft and black, they began walking to the coordinates with trowels and paper bags.

The lot on Kinsman took an afternoon. The overpass took three. Dev kept a can of paint in his backpack, and at each site he left a small cobalt mark on the nearest wall: a single seed, split open, a green line rising from it.

In late May, Ines went down to P3 to check on C-14 and found the vault restocked. New vials, new handwriting on the labels. Below the coordinates, someone had stenciled a cobalt seed exactly like Dev's, and beside it, two words:

*Thank you.*

She stood there a long time in the cool concrete dark, listening to the drip of water somewhere and the distant hum of the street above.

That summer, milkweed came up through the gravel behind the tire plant. Monarchs found it within a week. Nobody at City Hall noticed, which, Ines decided, was exactly the point.

Dev started on a new pillar in September. He was leaving vaults of his own.
