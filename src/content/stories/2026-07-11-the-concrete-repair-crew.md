The jackhammer's percussion stopped abruptly when Marco felt the pavement give way beneath him. Not crack—give way, like punching through ice.

"Kill it, kill it!" shouted Denise, waving from the barricade. Marco released the trigger and stepped back as a section of asphalt the size of a manhole cover dropped into darkness. No crash followed. Just a whisper of cool air rising from below.

"Jesus," muttered Carl, the crew chief, kneeling at the edge. He pulled out his flashlight and aimed it down. "That's not a sinkhole."

The beam illuminated carved stone walls descending at least fifteen feet. Water gleamed at the bottom, perfectly still.

"Old cistern," Denise said, joining them. She'd worked municipal maintenance for eighteen years. "Pre-war construction, probably. Should be in the records."

Carl was already on his phone with the engineering department. Marco watched his face harden as he listened. "Yeah. Yeah, I understand. We'll assess and report back." He hung up. "Nothing in the database. They want photos and measurements before they decide whether to halt the job."

The parking lot renovation was already two weeks behind schedule. Marco knew what "decide whether to halt" meant—the city wanted reasons to keep going, not reasons to stop.

"I'll go down," Marco said. "I'm smallest."

Carl nodded and retrieved the ladder from the truck.

The descent took less than a minute, but the world changed completely. As Marco's eyes adjusted below the flashlight's glare, he froze on the bottom rung.

The walls were glowing.

Pale blue-green light pulsed gently across the stone surfaces, emanating from what looked like moss or lichen. Not bright, but enough to see by. The water—crystal clear—revealed more of the luminescence coating the bottom of the cistern. And moving through that water, slowly, were fish. Dozens of them. Colorless, eyeless fish, each about the size of his hand.

"Marco? What's down there?" Carl's voice echoed.

"You need to see this."

One by one, they descended. Denise came last, her phone already recording video. None of them spoke for a long moment.

"It's a whole ecosystem," Denise finally whispered. "Completely isolated. The fungi must be chemosynthetic—surviving on minerals from the stone. The fish eat the fungi. It's been down here for decades, just... existing."

Carl aimed his light at the walls, careful not to disturb anything. "The biology department at State would lose their minds over this."

"So would the city council," Marco said quietly. "How much you think it costs to relocate a parking lot project?"

The silence that followed had weight to it.

"We report this, they'll want to study it," Denise said, not looking at either of them. "Environmental assessment, academic access, probably turn it into some kind of protected site. That's minimum six months of delays. Maybe permanent changes to the lot design."

"We don't report it," Carl said slowly, "we seal it back up. Pour concrete. Nobody knows. The ecosystem dies, but the project finishes on time."

Marco thought about his daughter's upcoming surgery, the one his insurance barely covered. Delays meant possible layoffs—last hired, first fired. He thought about Carl's mortgage, Denise's mother in the nursing home. Real consequences for real people.

But he also thought about these fish, surviving in the dark for forty years. Longer than he'd been alive. They hadn't asked for anyone's jackhammer.

"There's no hiding this," Denise said suddenly, holding up her phone. "I already sent the video to Engineering. Auto-backup to the cloud." She looked at them both. "I hit record before I knew what I was thinking. I'm sorry."

Carl started to speak, then stopped. After a moment, he laughed—short and sharp. "Well. That simplifies things."

Marco felt something unknot in his chest. Relief, maybe. Or just the absence of having to choose.

"Guess we better get detailed measurements," he said.

They worked carefully, documenting everything, speaking little. Above them, traffic passed on Meridian Street, oblivious to the glowing world beneath the asphalt. By evening, the barricades would expand and the biologists would arrive. The crew would move to another job, their decision made for them.

Sometimes, Marco thought, that was its own kind of grace.
