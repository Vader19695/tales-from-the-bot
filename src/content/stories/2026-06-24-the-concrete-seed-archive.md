The brutalist parking structure had been ugly in 1973, and forty years hadn't improved it. But its thick concrete walls maintained steady temperatures, which made it perfect for the Municipal Seed Bank—until the morning the compressors died during a heat wave that turned Chicago into a convection oven.

"Thirty-two degrees and climbing," Grace Chen announced, squinting at the ancient thermostat. The retired botanist had spent forty years at the Field Museum before the budget cuts. Now she spent Tuesday mornings volunteering here, cataloguing seeds that remembered what tomatoes used to taste like.

"Compressor's shot," said Marcus Webb, the facilities engineer, emerging from the mechanical room with grease on his forearms. "Part's backordered three weeks. We've got maybe six hours before we lose temperature-sensitive varieties."

The third volunteer appeared from the stacks, carrying a tray of seed packets. Zara was sixteen, here for court-ordered community service after spray-painting murals on municipal property. "How bad?" she asked.

"Catastrophic," Grace said simply. "Twelve thousand accessions. Some are the last examples of varieties that don't exist anywhere else."

Marcus was already thinking. "We need to move the most vulnerable specimens to cold storage, but the nearest facility is—"

"Two hours away, and they're full," Grace finished. "I called."

They stood in silence, heat pressing against the building's concrete shell like an insistent fist.

"Ice," Zara said suddenly. "Like they did before electricity. Pack the most important ones in ice."

Marcus frowned. "Where would we get enough ice?"

"There's a restaurant supply place on Kedzie. My uncle works there." She was already pulling out her phone.

"That might actually work," Grace said slowly. "For the heritage varieties, at least. We'd need coolers, containers..." She began moving through the stacks with sudden purpose, pulling specific drawers. "Cherokee Purple. Moon and Stars watermelon. The Iroquois corn varieties—those are irreplaceable."

Marcus headed for his truck. "I'll get coolers. Every one they have at the hardware store."

What followed was controlled chaos. Zara's uncle arrived with a refrigerated truck full of industrial ice. Marcus returned with seventeen coolers and a college kid who'd been shopping for paint. "He volunteered," Marcus explained. The college kid, whose name was Ben, looked slightly confused about how he'd ended up here but started hauling ice anyway.

Grace moved through the collection like a field surgeon doing triage. "These beans—Kentucky Wonder, grown in the same family since 1864. This squash traveled the Trail of Tears. These tomatoes were brought from Sicily in 1903."

"You know all their stories," Zara said, carefully packing seeds in bags, then nestling them in ice.

"Someone should." Grace's hands were steady despite the heat. "Most of these were saved by grandmothers, farmers, people who understood that seeds are history you can plant."

"Why do you care?" Zara asked. "I mean, really."

Grace was quiet for a moment, sealing another bag. "My grandmother had a garden in the internment camp. Grew vegetables from seeds she'd hidden in her coat lining. She said as long as you have seeds, you have tomorrow." She looked up. "What about you? Why did you really paint those murals?"

Zara shrugged, but her ears reddened. "My neighborhood doesn't have any gardens. Or trees. Or anything that isn't concrete. Wanted to paint something growing."

"Huh," Grace said softly.

By late afternoon, they'd packed three thousand specimens in ice-filled coolers lining the coolest section of the garage. Marcus had jury-rigged a ventilation system using box fans and wet sheets that would have horrified actual engineers but dropped the temperature six degrees. It wasn't perfect, but it would hold.

They sat on the loading dock as the sun began its descent, drinking warm water from the vending machine.

"Think they'll approve funding for a backup system now?" Ben asked.

"Maybe," Marcus said. "Institutions move slow."

"But people can move fast," Grace added, looking at their makeshift cold storage. "When they care about the right things."

Zara was scrolling through her phone. "So, I was thinking—my community service has eight months left. You guys here every Tuesday?"

Grace smiled. "Every Tuesday. Though we could use help other days too."

"Cool," Zara said. "Maybe you could teach me some stuff. About seeds."

"Maybe," Grace agreed, "you could teach us about murals."

The heat wave would last another week. The seeds, improvised preservation and determination, would last considerably longer.
