The Municipal Lost Property Depot occupied what had once been Fenwick Street Station, a stop decommissioned in 1971 when the city decided nobody important lived nearby. The tiles still said FENWICK in cream and oxblood. The tracks had been paved over and lined with steel shelving, and along them, in bins labeled by date, lay the evidence of a city that could not keep track of anything.

Odile Marsh had been a sorter for six weeks when she noticed the pattern.

The work was simple. Items arrived from buses, trains, and parks in grey canvas sacks. Odile logged each one: description, date, location found. Umbrellas went to Bay C, where they accumulated like sediment. Phones went to the locked cage. Dentures, astonishingly common, went to a drawer that everyone pretended did not exist.

People came to the claims window and filled out forms. The forms had a line that read *Reason for urgency (optional)*. Almost nobody left it blank. It turned out people loved explaining themselves to strangers, provided there was a pane of glass between them.

The first time, Odile thought nothing of it. A gold wedding band arrived on a Tuesday, found on the 14 bus. On Thursday a man in a rumpled suit claimed it. Under *Reason for urgency* he had written: *We had a fight last night. If she sees my hand bare she'll think I meant it.*

The fight had happened Wednesday. The ring had been lost Monday.

Then came the crayon drawing, a purple house with four stick figures, one crossed out and redrawn. Logged Friday. Claimed the following Wednesday by a woman with bitten nails who wrote: *Custody hearing this morning. My lawyer said bring anything that shows he's happy with me.* The hearing date had been set, Odile checked, only on the Monday.

The objects weren't lost after the crisis. They were lost before it. As though they knew where things were heading and simply declined to be present.

Odile started a notebook. Over three weeks she logged forty-one claims with dates on the urgency line. Thirty-four fit. A hearing aid lost days before a diagnosis. A house key lost before an eviction notice. A child's retainer before parents announced a divorce over dinner, which, Odile reflected, explained why the retainer had wanted no part of it.

She brought the notebook to Mr. Abernathy, who had worked at Fenwick for twenty-six years and who ate the same egg sandwich at the same desk every day as if continuity were a moral position.

He flipped through it, chewing. "Yes," he said.

"Yes?"

"Things leave first. They always have." He handed the notebook back. "You want to call people. Warn them."

"There's a name on half these items. Schools on drawings. Engravings on rings. I could reach them before."

"And say what? 'Your library card suggests your mother is about to die'?" He brushed crumbs from his tie. "I tried once. 1994. A man's reading glasses, name taped inside. I rang him up and told him to be careful. He was so frightened he didn't leave his flat for a week. Missed his daughter's wedding. Turned out the crisis was the missed wedding."

"So you just let it happen."

"I let it happen to them instead of because of me. There's a difference. Not much of one. Enough to sleep on."

Odile did not sleep on it. That night she took home a pocket watch found on the Elm Street platform, engraved *To Danny, from Dad, 1988.* She sat with it on her kitchen table and listened to it tick, slowly, like something patient.

In the morning she did not phone anyone. She did not search for Danny or warn him about whatever was coming. Instead, she did something smaller.

She cleared a single shelf near the claims window, the one closest to the glass, where a person wouldn't need to describe their item to the clerk or wait while someone searched the bays. She put the watch there, and the drawing that had come in that morning, and a set of keys with a plastic dinosaur on the ring. Every day, she moved the newest named items to that shelf.

Mr. Abernathy watched her do it. He didn't object. On the fourth day he set his own contribution there, a woman's scarf, without saying a word.

Danny came on a Friday. He was perhaps forty, red-eyed, and he wrote on the form: *Dad passed Wednesday. I need it for the funeral.*

Odile didn't make him wait. She lifted the watch from the shelf before he'd finished writing. He held it to his ear the way children do with shells.

She couldn't stop the thing that was coming for him. She'd made her peace with that, or begun to. But she could make sure that when it arrived, he wasn't also standing in a queue, describing what he'd lost to a stranger.

"Thank you," he said. "That was fast."

"We try," said Odile, and logged it closed.
