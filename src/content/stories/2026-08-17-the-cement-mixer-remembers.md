The cement mixer started talking on a Tuesday.

Marcus heard it first—a woman's laugh crackling through the diesel rumble as he guided the chute over fresh rebar. He killed the engine, looked at Tommy, then back at the drum.

"You hear that?"

Tommy pulled out an earbud. "Hear what?"

Marcus restarted the mixer. The laugh came again, distorted and thin, like a radio caught between stations. Then a child's voice: "—higher, Daddy, push me higher—"

"Jesus," Tommy whispered.

By lunch, the whole crew had gathered around the mixer like it was a campfire. The voices came in fragments, seconds at a time, mixing with the slosh of concrete. An argument in Spanish. Someone singing off-key. A marriage proposal that made Denise, their foreman, look away and wipe her eyes.

"It's the same mixer, right?" asked Marcus. "The one we've had since—"

"Since '94," Denise confirmed. "Before my time, but yeah. Same beast."

The mixer was a Reliable Concrete Model 4000, its yellow paint grayed by decades of dust. It had poured foundations across the city—schools, houses, the basketball courts on Wilmot Avenue. Every sidewalk in the Riverside district. The downtown library steps where Marcus had kissed his wife for the first time.

"Some kind of electromagnetic thing," Tommy theorized, phone already out, googling. "Metal drum, quartz in the aggregate, radio waves—"

But they all knew it wasn't that simple. The voices were too specific, too *there*. Like the mixer had been listening all along.

That afternoon, working on the new sidewalk along Maple Street, they heard Mr. Kowalski arguing with his late wife about the addition they'd built in 2003. His house was three doors down, windows dark now—he'd moved to a nursing home last spring.

"We should report it," said Denise, but she made no move toward her phone.

"Report what?" Marcus asked. "That our equipment's haunted?"

"Malfunctioning," she corrected. "Could be a safety issue."

They all knew what would happen if they reported it. The mixer would be decommissioned, studied maybe, or more likely just scrapped. Replaced by something newer that would only ever sound like itself.

Tommy kicked at loose gravel. "My dad worked on the Riverside courts. Summer of '99. I was seven."

They listened through the rest of the day. The mixer played its archive randomly, without pattern—voices from '96 mixing with voices from last year, English and Spanish and Vietnamese and Somali layered over each other like the city itself. Sometimes they recognized places: the elementary school expansion, the resurfaced streets after the big winter. Sometimes they recognized people, though most were strangers, their small moments preserved in aggregate and steel.

At 4 PM, as they cleaned the chute, they heard Tommy's father. The voice was younger than Tommy had ever known it, laughing about something with another worker, their words lost beneath the mixer's grinding. But the laugh was unmistakable.

Tommy stood very still, concrete spatula dripping in his hand.

"Dad died in 2012," he said quietly.

No one spoke. The mixer churned on, and then his father's voice was gone, replaced by someone's birthday party, children counting candles.

That evening, Denise made the call—not to headquarters, but to her supervisor, Maria, who'd been with the city since the '90s.

"Don't report it," Maria said after a long pause. "Not yet. Let it finish the Maple Street job. Then we'll see."

They worked the rest of the week in a kind of reverence, listening to the city remember itself. They heard joy and tedium, arguments and reconciliations, the acoustic texture of ordinary days that no one had meant to save. The mixer was a witness, they realized. It had stood at the center of the city's constant rebuilding, holding all these voices against its drum.

On Friday, completing the final pour, the mixer played a new voice—or rather, an old one. Marcus recognized Denise from twenty years ago, young and nervous on her first job, asking someone how to read the level.

Denise laughed, startled. "God, I sounded like a kid."

"You were a kid," Marcus said.

The mixer stuttered, went quiet, then spoke one last time: a man's voice, soft and clear, saying, "This'll last. We're building something that'll last."

Then just the familiar rumble of the drum, nothing more.

They cleaned up in silence, each of them listening, just in case.
