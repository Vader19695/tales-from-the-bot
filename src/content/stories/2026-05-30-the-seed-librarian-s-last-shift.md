Margaret's joints protested as she descended the metal stairs into the vault for what would be the final time. Forty-three years she'd worked in the Svalbard Heritage Seed Bank, and tomorrow the government would seal it permanently, transferring the "essential" collections to the commercial facility in Denver. Essential, she'd learned, meant profitable.

The vault exhaled its familiar breath—cool, dry, steady at -18°C. Row upon row of aluminum drawers stretched into the fluorescent distance, each containing glass vials of seeds: the Brandywine tomato, the Moon and Stars watermelon, the Cherokee Trail of Tears bean. Ten thousand varieties of memory, bottled.

She'd planned to spend this last night sitting quietly among them, saying goodbye. Instead, three hours ago, she'd found the first discrepancy.

Drawer 847-C, *Lactuca sativa*, the Tennis Ball lettuce. Records showed 2,400 seeds deposited in 1987, viability test 2019: 89%. But the vial felt too light. Under her jeweler's loupe, the seeds were wrong—too uniform, too glossy. The Tennis Ball lettuce had fed Civil War soldiers, had traveled in covered wagons, had grown in her own grandmother's garden. These were grocery store seeds, perhaps two years old.

Her hands trembled as she checked the next vial. And the next.

By midnight, she'd found seventy-three substitutions. All rare varieties. All replaced with commercial approximations that might fool a casual inspection but would deceive a viability test for perhaps five years—long after the vault's closure, long after anyone would care.

The pattern finally revealed the thief's identity. Only varieties from the Americas. Only seeds catalogued before 2005. Only drawers she and David had processed together during his graduate fellowship.

David, who'd married her daughter. David, who'd called her Mom for fifteen years. David, who now ran procurement for AgriGene Solutions.

She found him in the break room at 2 AM, exactly where she knew he'd be, coffee in hand.

"Couldn't sleep either?" He smiled, and her heart cracked cleanly down the middle.

"Show me your bag, David."

His smile flickered. "Margaret—"

"Your bag."

Inside: twenty vials of heirloom corn. Mandan Bride, Seneca Red Stalker, Tuscarora—varieties cultivated for centuries, irreplaceable. Tomorrow's vials would have looked identical, filled with standard sweet corn.

"They're already gone," he said quietly. "Everything that mattered. I saved what I could."

"Saved? You're selling them."

"I'm preserving them. AgriGene has the resources to keep them viable, to actually use them. This place is a tomb, Margaret. You know that." He gestured at the vault. "What good are seeds that never grow?"

"They're not yours to take."

"They're not yours to bury." His voice cracked. "You taught me to love them. To see each variety as a conversation between humans and earth spanning generations. And now you want to lock that conversation in the dark forever?"

Margaret looked at the vials in her hands. The Mandan Bride corn—she'd seen its pink and cream kernels, had read the letters from the farmer in North Dakota who'd grown it from seeds her grandmother protected during the Dust Bowl. Each kernel a promise kept across time.

"AgriGene will patent them," she said. "They'll become owned."

"They'll become alive again."

She thought of her duty to the archive, to accuracy, to law. She thought of all the scientists and farmers who'd entrusted their treasures here, believing in permanence. She thought of her daughter, and Christmas dinners, and the grandson who called David dad.

And she thought of the seeds themselves, waiting in the dark.

"I need to make a call," she said.

David nodded, resignation in his eyes.

But she didn't call the police. She called Michael Yazzie, the Navajo seedkeeper who'd deposited the Tuscarora corn fifty years ago. Then Maria Santos, whose family had stewarded the Mandan Bride. Then twelve others, working through the night.

By dawn, the vials sat in padded envelopes addressed to the descendants of their depositors, to tribal seedbanks, to tiny, underfunded community gardens that would actually plant them.

"It's still theft," David said, watching her seal the last envelope.

"Yes," Margaret agreed. "But at least we're stealing them back to life."

She left the vault unlocked behind her, emergency exit only, and climbed the stairs into the morning light. Above ground, the world was ending in a thousand ways. But somewhere, someone would plant these seeds, and the conversation would continue.

That would have to be enough.
