The cistern smelled like wet concrete and bureaucracy—Fatima's favorite combination at six in the morning.

"Particulate matter's up point-three percent in sector seven," Chen announced, tapping his datapad against the metal railing. The gesture sent a hollow ring through the cathedral-sized chamber beneath Third Avenue. "Someone's doing laundry with pre-ban detergent again."

"Mrs. Khoury," Fatima and Yussef said simultaneously.

Chen made a notation. Not for the official report—those only tracked compliance metrics and contamination levels. This went into what they called the Appendix, though it had long since grown larger than any actual city document. Twelve years of water quality data, cross-referenced with Chen's obsessive marginalia: when the textile factory finally shuttered (elevated dye compounds, June 2019), when the east side homeless encampment formed (spike in pharmaceutical traces, winter 2021), when the new apartment blocks opened despite being "under construction" according to planning permits (sudden increase in residential waste markers, last spring).

The cistern was three stories deep, with walkways crisscrossing above vast pools that glowed turquoise under sodium lights. Their footsteps echoed as they moved between testing stations, following the same route they'd walked a thousand times. Sample, measure, record. The city's memory dissolved in fifty million gallons.

"We have a visitor next week," Yussef said, too casually. He'd been department supervisor for six months and still couldn't deliver bad news properly.

Fatima paused mid-sample. "Define visitor."

"Capital-V Visitor. Efficiency auditor from Regional."

Chen's datapad nearly slipped from his hands. "They're shutting us down."

"Not shutting down. Streamlining." Yussef pronounced the word like a diagnosis. "Automated sensors. Remote monitoring. They want to transfer us to waste management."

"And the records?" Fatima asked.

The question hung in the humid air. Officially, they collected data for public health monitoring—simple stuff. Test, pass or fail, file with the city. But the Appendix lived in three filing cabinets in the break room, a dense chronicle of what actually happened versus what supposedly happened. The industrial runoff that appeared months before permits were issued. The population density that didn't match official census numbers by almost fifteen thousand people. The chemical signature of the disappeared pharmaceutical plant that everyone pretended had been a community center.

"They'll want everything digitized for the new system," Yussef said. "Compatible formats only."

Which meant the Appendix—stubborn, analog, unapologetically thorough—would be incompatible with itself.

Chen had started the archive as a joke, really. A scientist's compulsion to note what the instruments showed versus what reports claimed. Fatima added context from her forty years in the neighborhood. Yussef, unexpectedly, had contributed building permits that didn't exist and infrastructure maps that contradicted official blueprints.

Together, they'd assembled something dangerous: the truth.

"We could just hand it over," Chen said, not meaning it.

"Could do," Fatima agreed. "Along with the evidence that someone approved residential zoning in a contamination zone. And that the capacity numbers have been fiction for three years. And that half the new development happened without proper utility hookups."

"Seventeen bribes worth of information, by my count," Yussef added. "Give or take."

They walked in silence to the next testing station. The water whispered against concrete, patient and impartial. It absorbed everything—confessions, evidence, the accumulated residue of ten thousand small corruptions.

"My cousin has a printing shop," Fatima said finally. "Good scanner. Better discretion."

Chen nodded slowly. "I know an archive collective. Student-run. They'd keep it accessible."

"And we give Regional what they want," Yussef concluded. "Clean data. Compatible formats. A streamlined operation."

They continued their rounds, collecting samples that would become numbers that would become forgotten reports. But some things, they'd learned, shouldn't be forgotten. Some things needed witnesses, even if those witnesses spent their days in underground concrete caverns, measuring the parts-per-million evidence of a city that existed in the margins.

"Point-seven percent increase in sector twelve," Chen called out, already making two sets of notes.

The water remembered everything. Now someone else would too.
