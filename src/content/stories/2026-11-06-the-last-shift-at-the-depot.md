The memo arrived on a Tuesday, stapled to a fuel report, which was how Marisol Ferrante knew nobody upstairs had thought twice about it. *Unit 114 to be decommissioned end of month. Coordinate with salvage.*

Number 114 was a 1991 Flxible Metro with a cracked dashboard and a heater that worked only on the passenger side. It had outlived three transit directors and two paint schemes. Marisol had managed the night shift at the Grafton Street depot for eleven months, and in that time she had noticed exactly one strange thing about it: no one ever wanted to drive anything else on the 11 p.m. run of Route 31.

She mentioned the memo to Walt Pruitt at the coffee machine. Walt had been driving for thirty-four years and moved like a man conserving every joint he had left.

"Huh," he said. Then he stirred his coffee for a long time.

"Something I should know?"

Walt glanced at the dispatch board, then at the door, as if checking for supervisors who hadn't worked there since the nineties. "You ever ride the last 31?"

---

She rode it that Thursday, sitting three rows back with her clipboard, feeling foolish.

At 11:00 exactly, Walt pulled up to the stop outside the old Delmonte cannery, where nobody lived and nothing had been canned since 1987. The doors folded open. A figure climbed the steps.

Marisol would later try to describe the passenger and fail. A long coat, gray or brown. A hat, maybe. A face she looked directly at and could not afterward assemble, the way you can't recall the exact voice of someone in a dream. The passenger did not pay. Walt did not ask. The passenger took the seat behind the rear door, the one with the patched upholstery, and faced the window.

The bus ran the full loop: past the shuttered mall, the hospital, the river bridge, the long dark stretch of Kessler Avenue. No one else boarded. No one spoke. Walt hummed something under his breath, and Marisol had the strong impression that he was humming *for* someone.

At midnight, back at the cannery, the passenger rose and stepped down into the dark. The doors closed. Walt pulled away.

"Thirty years," he said, not turning around. "Ray Dunbar drove it before me. He told me the same way I'm telling you. Every night, 114, eleven o'clock. You don't send another bus. Ray tried once, in '96, when the transmission was out. Sent a newer one."

"And?"

"Nobody got on." Walt shrugged. "Ray said it felt like standing somebody up."

---

Marisol spent the weekend with the fleet records. Unit 114 had never logged a fare on its last run. It had never logged a complaint, an incident, or a ridership count above zero. On paper, for three decades, it had driven an empty loop every night, and nobody had ever flagged the waste, because nobody ever looks at the last run of anything.

She called the cannery's property manager, who had no idea what she was talking about. She checked old newspapers for accidents on Route 31 and found nothing worth the word. She sat in her car outside the cannery at 10:55 one night and watched the stop stay empty until 114 arrived, and then it wasn't.

There was no explanation. She came to understand that there wasn't going to be one, and that this was not the point.

---

The salvage truck was scheduled for the 30th. On the 28th, Marisol sat at her desk with two forms in front of her.

The first was the decommission order, needing only her signature.

The second was a reclassification request she'd found in a binder from 2003, allowing a manager to retain one retired unit as a "depot training and reserve vehicle." It required a justification. It would be reviewed. Someone, eventually, would ask why a depot needed a thirty-year-old bus with a broken heater, and she would have to answer with a straight face.

She thought about Ray Dunbar, whom she had never met, and Walt, whose knees had maybe two years left. She thought about a stop outside a dead cannery and somebody standing there at 11:00, waiting for a bus that didn't come.

She wrote: *Retained for driver training, late-shift route familiarization.* It was almost true. She signed it.

---

Walt retired the following spring. The night after his party, Marisol took the keys to 114 herself.

At 11:00 the doors folded open at the cannery. The passenger climbed the steps, unhurried, and paused beside her for the first time in all the nights she had watched.

"Evening," Marisol said.

There was no answer. There never would be. But the passenger stood there a moment longer than usual before moving to the patched seat by the rear door, and Marisol pulled out onto Kessler Avenue, humming.
