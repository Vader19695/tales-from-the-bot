The cistern smelled of limestone and time. Forty meters beneath the parched streets of Dalworth, Maya ran her finger down the logbook's yellowed pages, the headlamp casting shadows that danced across columns of numbers. Something was wrong.

"Check this," she said, her voice flat in the close air. "Tuesday's reading. 847 parts per million."

Lena looked up from the testing kit, her face gaunt in the harsh light. "So?"

"So I took Tuesday's reading. I wrote 1,240."

The third member of their team, old Henrik, stopped coiling rope. The silence pressed against them like the weight of the city above.

They'd been monitoring the cistern for eight months, since the aquifer failed and Dalworth's ten thousand residents began living on stored reserves. The salinity determined everything—how much water could be released, how long they could last. Too much salt and the crops would die. The logbook was their Bible, and someone had been rewriting scripture.

"Maybe you misremembered," Lena said carefully.

"I don't misremember numbers." Maya flipped back further. "Here. Last Thursday. Henrik's handwriting, but the reading's been changed. You can see where it was erased."

Henrik moved closer, his weathered hands steady as he tilted the page toward the light. The number 9 sat where a 7 had been, the ghost of the original still visible in the impression on the paper.

"I wrote 789," he said quietly. "This says 989."

They looked at each other, three people in a stone chamber the size of a bedroom, breathing recycled air and each other's secrets.

"Someone's making the water seem saltier than it is," Maya said. "The council's been restricting rations based on these numbers. If the real salinity is lower—"

"People are going thirsty for no reason," Lena finished. She was shaking her head. "But who would—we're the only ones with access."

The implication hung between them like condensation.

Maya started checking every entry from the past month. The pattern emerged slowly: small changes, careful ones. Always making the water seem more compromised than it was. Henrik verified his entries. All altered. Then they came to Maya's.

"These are right," she said.

"All of them?" Henrik's voice carried something new. Suspicion.

"Yes. Every single one I wrote is unchanged."

Lena had gone very still. "That's convenient."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying maybe you're smarter than whoever we're looking for. Maybe you knew we'd check the alterations, so you left your own entries alone."

Maya felt the accusation like a physical weight. "Why would I bring it up if I was guilty?"

"Because Henrik was about to notice. You just noticed first."

Henrik raised a hand. "Let's think this through. Who benefits from restricted water?"

The question opened something dark. Maya thought of her brother on the rationing council, how he'd visited last week with that desperate look. *Can you tell me the real numbers? Just between us?* She'd refused, but had he found another way?

Lena worked at the desalination plant. Rationing meant job security, overtime pay. And Henrik—Henrik had two grandchildren in the southern district, where council members' families got preference during restrictions.

They all had reasons. They all had people they'd protect.

"We need to tell someone," Maya said.

"Who?" Lena's laugh was bitter. "And say what? That we're accusing each other? They'll shut down the monitoring program, bring in outsiders. You know what happened in Millbrook when trust broke down."

Millbrook had torn itself apart. Riots over water accusations. Seventeen dead.

Henrik picked up the logbook. His hands weren't steady anymore. "There's another option. We recalibrate all the old readings. Give the real numbers to the council. The water's safe—people can have what they need."

"And the one who's been lying?" Maya asked.

"We handle it ourselves. Quietly. Or we all take the blame together."

The water lapped against ancient stone. Somewhere above them, children were drinking half-rations while adults cracked open pipes to steal what little remained. The truth would save them. But the truth would also destroy whatever fragile trust held this desperate city together.

Maya looked at her teammates—colleagues, friends, maybe conspirators. In the lamplight, they all looked guilty. They all looked innocent.

"Alright," she said finally. "We fix the numbers. But we keep monitoring. Together. All of us. Always."

They nodded, three people bound by suspicion and necessity, knowing they'd never fully trust each other again, knowing they had no choice but to try.

The cistern kept its secrets. For now.
