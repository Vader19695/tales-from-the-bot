The notice arrived taped to the elevator on level P1, beneath a flyer for a lost cat. Ines Halloran read it twice, then peeled it off carefully, the way she'd lift a pressed leaf from a herbarium sheet, and carried it down four flights to where the others were already working.

"Seven days," she said, setting it on the sorting table. "Demolition begins the twenty-third. Mixed-use tower. Retail on the ground floor."

Marisol, who drove the 2 a.m. bus route and came straight from the depot most nights, read it without taking off her gloves. Dev, a respiratory therapist, stopped labeling envelopes. Theo, nineteen and the newest, looked at the long aisles of steel shelving humming under their dehumidifiers and said, "They can't. Can they?"

"They can," Ines said. "The question is what we do."

Five years earlier, the garage had been empty, sealed after a flood rotted the ticket machines. Ines had leased P3 and P4 for almost nothing from a landlord who assumed she was mad. Now forty-one thousand varieties slept down here in labeled jars: Cherokee Trail of Tears beans, a sunflower from a Ukrainian grandmother in Queens, prairie dropseed collected from the last unplowed acre in the county. Each had a card. Each card had a name: who saved it, who grew it out, who brought it back.

They argued the first night, quietly, because that was how they did everything. Marisol wanted to fight: lawyers, the news, the city council. "People should know this is here."

"People knowing is how we got the notice," Dev said. "Somebody's survey drone found the ventilation."

"So we hide better?"

"So we stop needing a basement."

Ines listened. She had spent her career in institutions, botanical gardens and university vaults, and had watched two of them lose funding and quietly incinerate collections nobody could afford to ship. She had built this place precisely so it wouldn't depend on anyone's budget. It had not occurred to her, until the paper on the elevator, that it depended on her.

By the third night, they had a list. A community farm upriver with a root cellar. Two high school greenhouses. A Hmong elders' garden that already kept its own seed. A church in Pell Valley with a dry basement and a pastor who used to be a soil scientist. Eleven homes in all. None could take everything. Together, they could.

"It means splitting families," Marisol said, meaning the varieties, the related lines Ines had kept side by side for cross-reference. "Nobody will see the whole picture anymore."

"Nobody needs to," Dev said. "That was always the risk. One room. One key."

He didn't look at Ines when he said it, which was kind.

On the fifth night, Ines found Theo alone in aisle nine, holding a jar of squash seed up to the light. Lakota squash, its card written in her own handwriting from 2019.

"I grew this one," he said. "Last summer. In the lot behind my mom's building. It got powdery mildew and I thought I'd killed it, but it set fruit anyway." He turned the jar. "Do you think whoever gets it will know that? That it's tough?"

"Only if you tell them."

He looked at her. "You should tell them. You know everything."

"That," Ines said, "is the problem."

She sat down on an overturned crate. Her knees ached; they had for two years. "When I'm gone, and I will be, everything I know about these goes with me unless it's somewhere else first. In someone else's hands. In their mistakes, even." She nodded at the jar. "You know something about that squash I don't."

The last two days were all logistics: cold packs, borrowed vans, a spreadsheet Dev built that outlived every argument. Ines wrote notes until her hand cramped, then dictated to Theo, then stopped dictating and simply let him ask.

On the final morning, the vans idled on the ramp. Marisol had driven the church route twice already. The shelving stood bare, dust outlines where jars had been.

Ines held the original ledger, five years of names in her small slanted print. She had meant to keep it. Instead she handed it to Theo.

"Copy it," she said. "Give a copy to each house. Then keep adding."

"What about you?"

"I'll visit. Someone has to check whether you're labeling properly."

He laughed, a little wet. She climbed into Dev's van, the last crate of beans at her feet, and did not look back at the empty level. She thought instead of eleven cellars and greenhouses across the valley, each holding a piece, each stranger now a keeper, and of a squash in a vacant lot that had gotten sick and fruited anyway.
