Dr. Nell Ferreira had been warned about many things before accepting the position at the Harlow Municipal Composting Facility: the smell, the seagulls, the fact that her office was a former supply closet with a window facing the loading dock. Nobody had warned her about the glow.

She found it on her second Tuesday, during an evening sample run. Digester Three, the oldest of the facility's fermentation tanks, had a viewing port caked with condensation. When she wiped it clean, the slurry inside was shimmering a soft, uncertain green, like a forgotten night-light.

Nell stared at it for a long time. Then she drew three samples, labeled them with hands that were not quite steady, and spent the next four days confirming that what she was looking at could not exist.

The dominant organisms in Digester Three were ordinary anaerobic bacteria, the dull workhorses of decomposition. None carried the genes for bioluminescence. None should have been capable of producing light at all. Yet under the microscope, a faint byproduct pooled between the cells, emitting a steady, gentle radiance. It was nontoxic. It was odorless, which at a composting facility made it practically a miracle on its own. It was also, by every textbook she owned, impossible.

She drafted an email to her supervisor, Gary Pruitt, three times. Gary was the operations manager, a man whose primary concerns were throughput, odor complaints, and a long-running feud with the Parks Department over mulch allocation. In her first week, he had told her, "We don't need discoveries here, Dr. Ferreira. We need dirt." She suspected an email titled *Possible Violation of Known Biology* would not be received well.

She deleted the draft and went to the break room for coffee, where Dolores from the forklift crew looked up from her crossword and said, "So you found Glenda."

Nell set down her mug. "I'm sorry?"

"Digester Three," said Teo, who worked nights and was eating what appeared to be breakfast at four in the afternoon. "We call her Glenda. Like the good witch."

"She started glowing in March," Dolores said. "Faint at first. We figured you'd notice eventually. Fancy degree and all."

It turned out the crew had been keeping a log. Marcus, a quiet man from the sorting line, produced a battered spiral notebook from his locker. Inside were eight months of observations in four different handwritings: dates, temperatures, brightness rated on a scale from "barely" to "you could read by it." Someone had noted, with apparent scientific seriousness, that Glenda glowed brightest the week the high school cafeteria sent over its leftover pumpkin pie.

"You should have reported this," Nell said, more out of reflex than conviction.

"To Gary?" Teo laughed. "Gary would've drained her and filed a work order."

Nell looked at the notebook, then at the three of them waiting with an expression she recognized from graduate school, from the best days, when the question mattered more than the answer.

"Okay," she said. "But we do it properly."

What followed was the strangest research project of her career. Dolores, it emerged, had an uncanny instinct for which incoming loads would brighten the tank. Marcus built a light meter from a salvaged solar panel and a multimeter, which turned out to be more accurate than the one Nell ordered. Teo took readings every hour of the night shift and annotated them with remarks like "Glenda moody tonight" and "sang to her, no measurable effect, but she seemed to like it." Nell sequenced samples during lunch breaks and found a cluster of genes that matched nothing in any database, as if the bacteria had simply decided to invent something.

They met in the break room at shift change and called it the Pie Committee, to discourage questions.

By November, Nell had enough data for a paper and a growing knot of dread. Protocol was clear. Unusual biological activity had to be reported. Every day she didn't tell Gary felt less like science and more like hiding.

So she asked him to stay late on a Friday.

He followed her to Digester Three with the air of a man missing dinner. Dolores had already switched off the overhead lights. Teo and Marcus stood nearby, carefully pretending to inspect a pipe.

Nell wiped the viewing port. Glenda glowed.

Gary looked at it for a full minute. "Does it affect throughput?"

"No."

"Odor?"

"It actually reduces it."

He nodded slowly. "Then I suppose you'd better write it up."

"There are four other authors," Nell said.

Gary glanced at the crew, then back at the tank. For just a moment, his face softened into something like wonder.

"Put me in the acknowledgments," he said. "And somebody turn the lights back on before Parks sees this and wants it."
