The limestone quarry held its breath at night. Three hundred feet below street level, surrounded by walls that had once supplied half the city's foundations, Maya sat at her workstation with packet 47-G-1203 between her gloved fingers.

The label read *Solanum lycopersicum* 'Brandywine'—heirloom tomato, deposited 2019. But the seeds inside were wrong. Too small. Too dark. The distinctive mottling along their edges triggered something in her memory from graduate school, from the taxonomy courses she'd abandoned when the funding dried up.

She pulled up the digital microscope and positioned a single seed under the lens.

Her hands stopped moving.

*Solanum umbelliferum insulare*. Island nightshade. Channel Islands endemic, declared extinct in 1984 after fire and invasive species decimated the last population on San Miguel Island. She'd written a seminar paper on it, back when she thought she'd spend her life doing something other than part-time catalogue work.

Maya sat back. The quarry's ventilation system hummed through its hourly cycle. In six hours, the day shift would arrive—the directors, the department heads, the USDA compliance officers who visited quarterly. Above ground, two hundred community gardens across three counties waited for their spring seed allocations, scheduled to ship in four days.

She knew the protocol. Suspected contamination or mislabeling required immediate supervisor notification. Dr. Reeves would file a formal incident report. The regulatory investigation would begin within forty-eight hours. The entire facility would freeze operations—no deposits, no withdrawals, no distributions. Six months minimum, possibly longer.

The community gardens would scramble for commercial alternatives. Most would survive. Some wouldn't. The Eastside Victory Garden, which supplied the refugee community's food co-op. The Roosevelt Elementary teaching plot. The North End recovery program gardens that gave people coming out of addiction something living to tend.

She could correct the entry. Relabel the packet as unidentified, flag it for her own follow-up review. Route it to her station instead of the general collection. Continue the investigation quietly, trace the deposit records, find out how extinct seeds had entered the system.

The microscope screen glowed. She captured three images, saved them to her personal drive, then the facility's isolated server. Documentation either way.

Maya pulled the deposit log. Packet 47-G-1203: received from the estate of Harold Chen, processed by volunteer intake coordinator Melissa Park, February 2019. Chen had been a community gardener, one of the old-timers. Died at ninety-two. His family had donated his entire seed collection—forty years of careful saving and trading.

She cross-referenced the name against her personal notes. Chen had worked on the Channel Islands as a park ranger in the seventies and early eighties. Before the fires. Before the extinction declarations.

Maya felt something shift in her chest. Not excitement exactly—something quieter, more serious.

If she reported it, the investigation would eventually reach the same conclusion she already suspected: Harold Chen had saved these seeds for forty years, carefully, illegally, waiting for someone to understand what they were. He'd hidden them in a tomato packet because he knew the system wouldn't accept them otherwise, knew that "extinct" meant "impossible," meant "paperwork error," meant burial in bureaucratic process.

He'd trusted someone would look closely enough.

She opened the formal incident report template on her screen. The cursor blinked in the first empty field.

Maya thought about protocols designed to protect collections from contamination and chaos. She thought about community gardens waiting for seeds. She thought about a ninety-two-year-old park ranger carefully writing "heirloom tomato" on a packet containing something vastly more precious.

She closed the template.

She opened the collection management system and created a new entry: packet 47-G-1203-R, flagged for specialist review, assigned to her station. Then she drafted an email to Dr. Sarah Voss at the botanical garden—her old professor, now running the native species recovery program.

*Found something that needs quiet verification before formal channels. Can we meet?*

She packaged the seeds in a clean envelope, locked them in her station's secure drawer, and returned to the evening's remaining catalogue work. Above her, three hundred feet of limestone held steady. The quarry kept its secrets well.

Outside, morning was still hours away. She had time to finish the shift, time to think, time to be wrong or right about what trust demanded.

She had time to decide what growing things needed most: perfect procedure or careful hands.
