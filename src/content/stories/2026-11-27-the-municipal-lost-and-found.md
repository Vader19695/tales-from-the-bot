The rule at the Central Lost Property Depot was simple, and Edith Pell loved it the way only a retired archivist could: every unclaimed item must be catalogued, photographed, and stored for exactly one year. Not eleven months. Not "about a year." Three hundred and sixty-five days, then disposal, with a form in triplicate.

After forty years in the municipal records office, Edith had expected the depot to be restful. Umbrellas, mostly. A surprising number of dentures. One taxidermied ferret, which nobody ever claimed and which the night clerk had named Gerald.

It was the shoe that started it.

A child's shoe, red, left foot, size nine, with a buckle strap mended in mismatched blue thread. Item 2024-0447, found on the Number 12 bus. Edith photographed it, filed it, and felt a prickle of recognition so specific she went straight to the old ledgers.

There it was. Item 2022-1183, red child's shoe, left foot, blue-mended strap. Found at Larkfield Library. Claimed eleven months later. And again in 2020, found outside a chemist's. Claimed.

"Shoes get lost," said Dev, her supervisor, who was twenty-six and regarded her investigations the way one might regard a grandmother taking up skydiving.

"The same shoe, Dev. Three times. Lost by a child who never seems to grow."

Over the following weeks, Edith worked the way she always had: quietly, thoroughly, and with a highlighter. She found the love letter next, addressed *Dear R.*, signed *Always, B.*, with a coffee ring in the corner shaped faintly like Portugal. Lost four times in six years. Then a brass house key on a green plastic fish tag, lost five times. Each was claimed within weeks of its disposal date. Each claimant was different. Each finder, too.

And every finder's address was in Larkfield, the narrow terraced neighborhood behind the old biscuit factory.

That was when Edith remembered Section 14(b) of the depot regulations, a clause so dusty she suspected she was the only living person who had read it. When an item was reclaimed by its owner, the city paid the finder a reward of forty pounds "in recognition of civic honesty."

Edith set down her highlighter. "Oh," she said aloud. "Oh, you clever devils."

---

On Saturday she took the Number 12 to Larkfield with the shoe's photograph in her handbag, feeling rather like a detective and rather more like someone's aunt who had got lost. The trail of addresses led her, eventually, to a laundromat called Suds & Sundries, where a woman in her seventies was folding towels with military precision.

Edith laid the photograph on the counter. The woman looked at it for a long moment, then at Edith.

"You're from the depot," she said.

"I'm the one who photographs things. I noticed the stitching."

The woman sighed, put down her towel, and switched the kettle on, which Edith understood to be a confession.

Her name was Bernadette. The letter was hers, written to her husband Ray in 1971, lost on a bus by accident in 2018. When it was returned, the finder, a young man two doors down who was behind on his rent, got forty pounds. Bernadette had thought about that for a week.

"So we lost it again," she said. "On purpose. Whoever's struggling that month finds it. Someone else claims it. Nobody asks who needs what. The shoe belonged to my granddaughter. She's at university now, studying economics, which I find very funny. The key opens nothing. We bought it at the hardware shop."

"It's fraud," Edith said, not unkindly.

"It's forty pounds," Bernadette said. "The council spends more than that on the forms."

Edith, who had filled in those forms, could not honestly disagree.

---

She thought about it all the way home. She thought about the rule she loved, the one that said every lost thing deserved a full year of someone's attention before the world gave up on it. She thought about the man two doors down, the one who'd been behind on his rent.

On Monday, Dev mentioned that the city was sending an auditor in the spring.

"He'll be thorough," Dev said gloomily. "Spreadsheets. Pattern-matching software."

"Will he," said Edith.

That evening she went to Suds & Sundries with a parcel. Inside were her reading glasses, a pearl hatpin of her mother's, and a paperback with her name inscribed on the flyleaf: dozens of new small things, none of which had ever been lost before.

"Fresh stock," she told Bernadette. "Retire the shoe. The auditor will notice the shoe."

Bernadette looked at the parcel, then at Edith, and slowly smiled.

"You'll need your glasses," she said.

"I have spares," Edith said. "I was an archivist. We always keep copies."
