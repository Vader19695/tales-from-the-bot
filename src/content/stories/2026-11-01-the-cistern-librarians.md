The cistern beneath the old municipal waterworks holds forty-one centimeters of standing water, which makes it the wettest place in a city that has not seen rain in nineteen months. The volunteers find this funny for about a week. After that it is simply where they work.

There are eleven archivists on the rotation, three to a shift, eight hours per shift. They sit on a raised platform of shipping pallets beneath the brick vaults and copy text from a solar-charged tablet onto acid-free paper, by hand, in ink. Candles are beeswax, because paraffin smokes and smoke yellows paper. Finished pages are laid on the stone ledges that line the walls, where the damp air dries them slowly enough that they do not curl. Someone calculated that at the current rate the group will finish transcribing the essential human record in four hundred and twelve years. Someone else pointed out that this was optimistic.

Teo Varga is sixteen and not an archivist. He is the courier. Three times a week he wades in with a waterproof sack of blank paper, ink, and candles, and wades out with bound volumes for the dry vault upstairs. He is paid in sandwiches. He considers this fair.

He is also, as it turns out, the only person who reads what they copy.

It starts with a chemistry textbook. Teo is waiting for a binding to set, and he idly compares the copied page to the tablet. A temperature is wrong: 140 degrees in the original, 410 in the copy. A transposition, he thinks. He checks the initials in the margin, as every page is initialed. *M.O.*

Margarethe Oyelaran is eighty-three, the oldest of the eleven, and the fastest. Her handwriting looks typeset. She has been copying since the first week, when the cistern still had frogs.

Teo checks more of her pages. He spends four shifts doing it, saying he is organizing the dry vault, which is true in the sense that he is in it. He finds nineteen discrepancies. A reagent swapped for an inert cousin. A dosage shifted by a decimal place. A wiring diagram in which two terminals have quietly traded places. In a history of the twentieth century, a speech has been shortened by one paragraph, the paragraph that made the speaker's argument sound most reasonable.

None of the errors appear in poetry, cookbooks, or the seventeen-volume history of Portuguese maritime law, which Teo finds mildly reassuring and also suspicious.

He brings it to her on a Thursday, at the end of her shift, when the other two have gone up the ladder. The candles hiss. Water drips somewhere in the dark at intervals of roughly six seconds; Teo has timed it.

"You're changing things," he says.

Margarethe caps her pen. "Yes."

He had prepared for denial and has nothing ready for this. "Why?"

"Because in four hundred years, if anyone is reading these, it will be because everything else has failed. And people in failed places should not find instructions for making things worse sitting on a shelf beside the bread recipes." She looks at the drying pages. "I have lived through two of those places. Knowledge is not neutral when it is the only knowledge left."

"But it's a library. It's supposed to be true."

"It's supposed to be useful. Those overlap less than librarians like to admit."

"The speech wasn't a recipe for anything."

She is quiet for three drips. "No," she says. "That one was vanity. I thought I knew better than the reader. I suspect I do, but that is not the point, is it?"

"Then what is the point?"

Margarethe reaches beneath her pallet and lifts out a ledger bound in oilcloth. She hands it to him. Inside, in the same typeset hand, is every change she has made. Volume, page, line, original text, altered text, date. Three hundred and six entries.

"I kept a record," she says, "because I am not a vandal. I am a coward with good penmanship. I wanted someone to find it. I hoped it would be someone young enough to still be here when it matters."

Teo turns the pages. The candlelight makes the water glow orange around his boots.

"I won't fix them," he says at last. "Not all of them. Not the reagents." He closes the ledger. "But I'm not hiding it either. Each altered book gets a sealed envelope in the back: the true text, waxed shut, with a note saying *this page was changed, here is why, here is what it said.* Whoever opens it has to choose to."

Margarethe considers this. "Friction instead of erasure."

"A door instead of a wall."

"It will slow the work."

"Everything slows the work."

She almost smiles. "And the speech?"

"The speech goes back in. Tonight."

She uncaps her pen, pulls a fresh sheet toward her, and waits for him to read it aloud. He does, slowly, and she copies it exactly. When it is finished, they lay it on the stone shelf to dry beside the others, where the damp will take its time, as it always does.
