The fluorescent lights hummed in rows across Level B-3, illuminating what had once been premium parking for the medical district. Now, Dr. Eleanor Moss stood before Shelf 47-C, carefully placing packets of Mortgage Lifter tomato seeds between the Kentucky Beefsteak and the Cherokee Purple.

"You alphabetize tomatoes by their full names?" Marcus asked, skateboard tucked under his arm. At sixteen, he'd stumbled into the archive six months ago, looking for a place to practice tricks away from security guards.

"Would you prefer I organize them by color? Acidity? Days to maturity?" Eleanor adjusted her glasses. "Taxonomy is the difference between preservation and hoarding."

"Fair," Marcus said, grinning. He'd learned not to argue with Eleanor about systems.

The parking structure had been abandoned for three years, caught in permit limbo between the city and a bankrupt development company. Eleanor had noticed the "For Lease" sign first during her evening walks, recognized the climate-controlled potential. Two years later, the space held over twelve thousand seed varieties on refurbished grocery store shelving, each packet dated, documented, saved from discontinued catalogs and dying gardens.

Jade arrived next, bringing coffee and news. "Survey crew was here this morning. Hard hats, clipboards, the whole aesthetic."

Eleanor's hand paused over a packet of Moon and Stars watermelon. "How many?"

"Three. They were taking measurements near the entrance ramp."

"The developer's moving forward," Eleanor said quietly. She'd known this moment would come. Underneath everything beautiful, there was always an expiration date.

The group gathered that evening—all seven of them. Besides Marcus and Jade, there was Terrence (who'd designed their cataloging database), Ana and Sofia (twin sisters who'd contributed their grandmother's Mexican heirloom collection), and the brothers David and James (who handled security and supply runs).

"We could move everything," David suggested. "Find another location."

"Where?" Eleanor asked. "Do you know how rare this is? Climate controlled, secure, accessible. And we'd need trucks, time, permission we don't have."

"We could go public," Ana said. "There are organizations that do this. Seed banks, conservation groups."

"Who would immediately take over, professionalize it, probably relocate everything to some university basement in Minnesota," Jade countered. "No offense to Minnesota."

Marcus had been quiet, rolling his skateboard back and forth. "What if we go public, but locally? Make allies before they can take it away."

Everyone turned to look at him.

"Hear me out," he continued. "My mom works at City Hall. Parks department. They're always talking about urban agriculture grants, sustainability initiatives—all that stuff that looks good in press releases. What if this becomes a civic asset instead of an illegal squat?"

Eleanor smiled despite herself. "That's either brilliantly strategic or spectacularly naive."

"Can't it be both?" Marcus asked.

The next week moved quickly. Marcus's mother made calls. Jade designed a website. Terrence compiled their catalog into something presentable. Eleanor wrote a mission statement that took her seventeen drafts.

The morning of the city council's zoning subcommittee meeting, Eleanor stood at a podium in a wood-paneled room that smelled of old carpet and bureaucracy. Behind her sat thirty people—the teenagers, Marcus's mother, two journalists, a representative from the state agricultural extension office, and a scattering of gardeners who'd heard about the archive online.

She explained their work simply: preservation, education, food security, community. She showed photographs of the shelves, the meticulous organization, the rare varieties that existed nowhere else in the region.

The developer's lawyer spoke about property rights and commercial viability. He was polished, prepared, and utterly uninterested in seeds.

When the subcommittee chair called for questions, Councilwoman Rodriguez leaned forward. "Dr. Moss, how many varieties did you say?"

"Twelve thousand, four hundred and seventeen," Eleanor answered. "As of this morning."

Rodriguez smiled. "That seems worth preserving."

The vote wasn't unanimous, but it was enough. The city would negotiate with the developer for the lower level, grant them formal access, explore partnership funding.

Walking back to the archive afterward, Marcus skated lazy circles on the sidewalk. "So we're legitimate now. Does that feel weird?"

Eleanor considered this. "Terrifying, actually. But good terrifying."

"Like stage fright?"

"Like watching something small become visible," she said. "Hoping the light doesn't burn it up."

"It won't," Marcus said with teenage certainty. "We've got good roots."

Eleanor laughed—a real laugh, full and surprised. Below them, in the climate-controlled darkness, twelve thousand futures waited in paper packets, saved from obsolescence by stubbornness and taxonomy and unlikely friendship.
