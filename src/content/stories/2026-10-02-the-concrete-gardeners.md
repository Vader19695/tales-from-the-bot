The engineer arrived on a Tuesday, which Marcus considered a breach of etiquette. Mondays were for bad news. Tuesdays were supposed to be for recovering from it.

He watched from the attendant's booth as the man parked a white city sedan on Level 1, unfolded himself, and stood looking up at the ceiling the way people look at a sky that might rain. He carried a clipboard, a laser measure, and a thermos with a cartoon moose on it. Marcus wrote *moose thermos* on a sticky note, because the group text would want details.

By 4 a.m., they were all on Level 3, northeast corner, behind the concrete barrier where the fluorescent tubes had died in 2019 and nobody had replaced them. Dolores came straight from the hospital laundry, still smelling of industrial bleach. Teo had grease from the bus depot under his nails. Priya from the overnight pharmacy brought coffee in a cardboard carrier, which meant she thought this was serious.

Around them, in a hundred and forty feet of shadow, the garden breathed.

It wasn't pretty. It was milk crates and cut-down recycling bins and two bathtubs Teo had hauled up the ramp at a cost to his lower back that he still brought up. The soil came from a landscaper's dumpster and a lot of coffee grounds. Rainwater ran from a cracked expansion joint on Level 4 into a gutter of split PVC, then into a rain barrel, then out through a mile of irrigation tubing punctured with a pushpin. Above it all hung grow lights salvaged from a dispensary that had lost its license, wired to a timer and an outlet that technically belonged to the city.

Tomatoes, mostly. Chard that refused to die. Basil Priya swore was the best in the county, which was not a claim anyone could verify. Peppers. A single stubborn zucchini plant that had produced one zucchini the size of a forearm and then nothing else, like it had made its point.

"So," said Teo. "We take it down tonight."

"Tonight," Dolores repeated. "Two years in one night."

"He's on Level 2 by Thursday. Here by Friday."

Nobody said what they were all thinking, which was that none of them actually knew each other. Marcus knew Dolores's daughter was in nursing school. Dolores knew Teo's knees were bad. Teo knew Priya hated cilantro, though she grew it anyway because the others liked it. They had never once met in daylight. They had a shared spreadsheet titled *Inventory (Garage)* and a rule that nobody talked about the garden at work, and that was the whole of it.

It was a strange kind of closeness, knowing exactly how someone ties a tomato stake and nothing about their middle name.

"There's the weight," Priya said. She'd looked it up. "Wet soil is a hundred pounds a cubic foot, give or take. Bathtubs full of it. On a slab that's being assessed for structural failure."

"So we're a hazard," said Teo.

"We're a variable," said Priya. "He'll find the stains anyway. The tubing leaves marks."

Marcus pulled the notebook from his jacket. It had started as a way to track the watering. Then he'd started noting which joints dripped, and when, and how much after which storms, because the barrel had to be emptied and he liked knowing things. Two years of handwriting. Dates, rainfall, gallons. A map, badly drawn, of every leak on Levels 3 and 4.

"He's going to want to know where the water gets in," Marcus said. "We know where the water gets in."

They argued for twenty minutes, quietly, in the practical way of people who are tired and have to be somewhere at six. Trespassing. Theft of electricity. Whether a city employee with a moose thermos was the kind of man who wrote people up. In the end, it was Dolores who said it.

"We're losing it either way," she said. "I'd rather lose it on purpose."

On Friday the engineer came around the barrier and stopped. He looked at the bathtubs. He looked at the zucchini. He looked at Marcus, who held out the notebook like a man surrendering a weapon.

The engineer read for a long time. His name was Gil. He had a slow way of turning pages.

"This joint here," he said finally. "You're sure it's always this one?"

"Every storm since March of last year."

Gil nodded, wrote something, and drank from the moose. "The tubs have to come off the slab. Today."

"Understood."

"The rest of it." He looked up at the dead lights, the living ones. "I'm logging it as an ongoing moisture study. Through demolition. I'll want copies of the notebook."

It did not save the garden. In three months the garage would come down, the bathtubs with it, and the basil would go into a community plot across town where it would have to be ordinary.

But that night the group text lit up for the first time with a photo taken in daylight: four strangers on Level 3, holding a zucchini the size of a forearm, squinting like they'd never seen the sun.
