The iron ladder descended twenty-three rungs into darkness. Maya counted them every Tuesday, her hands finding the familiar grooves worn smooth by a decade of volunteers. The cistern waited below, cool and patient, carved from limestone a century ago when the town still believed in abundance.

She clicked on her headlamp. The beam caught dancing motes of dust before settling on the logbook, wrapped in oilcloth on its stone shelf beside the valve wheel. This was the routine: check the water level, record the measurement, adjust the flow to match the day's ration quota. Simple math that kept two thousand people alive during the third year of drought.

Maya unwrapped the book and smiled at yesterday's entry. Chen had drawn a tiny perfect sketch of a beetle he'd found crawling across the pressure gauge. Beneath it, he'd written: *Still here. Still trying. Like us.*

She flipped backward through the pages, reading the voices of her fellow keepers. Reverend Thomas always included a brief prayer. Dr. Yuki logged water quality observations in precise medical shorthand. Sam wrote rambling thoughts about community resilience, philosophical tangents that somehow grounded Maya when the weight of rationing felt too heavy.

There were five of them, rotating through the week. They'd never discussed turning the logbook into something more than data—it had simply happened. One small human moment after another, left like gifts in the dark.

Maya turned to a fresh page, about to record Tuesday's measurements, when a notation caught her eye. Three months back, late March, in handwriting she didn't recognize. The script was hurried, uneven:

*May 17 - The Hendersons' well will collapse. Don't let them dig deeper.*

Her breath caught. Today was May 17th. She flipped forward through the weeks, watching familiar entries pass—Chen's beetles, Thomas's prayers—until she reached yesterday. Nothing unusual. But there, in March, was another note in that same strange hand:

*June 3 - Fire at the school. Electrical. Check the portable heaters in the supply closet.*

Maya's hands trembled as she photographed the pages with her phone. Up above, where cell service existed, she texted the group: *Emergency meeting. Tonight.*

---

They gathered in the cistern together for the first time, five bodies making the space feel suddenly small. Chen ran his fingers over the March entries while Dr. Yuki examined the ink under her medical penlight.

"It's not old," Yuki said. "This was written recently. Maybe last week."

"But it's dated three months ago," Sam protested. "And look—these predictions. The Henderson well *did* start showing cracks yesterday. They were planning to excavate deeper."

Reverend Thomas sat heavily on the stone floor, his face ashen. "I wrote this," he said quietly. "Last Thursday."

They stared at him.

"I've been... the water does something down here. When I'm alone, sometimes I feel like I'm not just *in* this moment. Like I can see the ripples spreading forward." He looked up, eyes pleading. "I thought I was losing my mind. So I wrote it down. Backdated it. To test whether I was crazy or if it was real."

"The Hendersons," Maya said.

Thomas nodded. "I warned them yesterday. Told them I'd noticed stress fractures. They've stopped digging."

The silence stretched, broken only by the steady drip of water into the reservoir.

"So we trust it," Chen said finally. "We check the heaters."

"We don't know how this works," Yuki countered. "Or why. Or if it's even safe—"

"It's *water*," Sam interrupted, pressing his palm against the cool limestone. "It remembers. Every molecule has been everywhere, seen everything. Maybe down here, close to the aquifer, the boundaries get thin."

Maya looked at each of their faces in the crossing beams of headlamps. Her friends. Her fellow keepers of the town's last reserve. They'd already been trusting each other with survival. This was just one more weight to carry.

"We keep the book," she said. "We check the heaters. And Thomas—keep writing what you see."

One by one, they nodded.

Above them, two thousand people slept, unaware that their water was watched over by five volunteers who'd stumbled into something vast and strange. But down here in the dark, beside the cool stone and the patient water, it felt almost natural. Like discovering what had always been there, waiting to be noticed.

They climbed up together, counting the rungs, leaving the logbook wrapped carefully below.

The cistern kept its secrets, and its promises.
