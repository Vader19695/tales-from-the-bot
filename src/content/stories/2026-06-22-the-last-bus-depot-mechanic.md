The hydraulic lift wheezed like an old smoker as Marcus lowered Bus 47 back to the bay floor. Thirty-two years he'd been listening to that particular wheeze, ever since the depot installed these lifts in '92. Now he had four days left.

"You feel that?" He gestured to Iris, who stood beside the lift control panel, one hand resting on the yellow safety rail.

She closed her eyes, palm flat against the metal. "Vibration stops about halfway down. Something's loose in the descent mechanism."

Marcus grunted approval. The kid was strange—wouldn't touch a smartphone without her canvas work gloves on, said the screens felt "wrong"—but she could diagnose a transmission problem by resting her forehead against the engine block.

"Coupling bolt," he said. "Bay three. Third drawer, left side."

She was already moving, her fingers trailing along the tool cabinet's face until she found the right drawer. Never looked. Just touched and knew.

The depot spread across four acres of cracked asphalt in the Ironbound district, where the city kept everything it needed but didn't want to see. Forty-seven buses, most of them older than Iris, who was twenty-three and had moved to the city from somewhere upstate she never talked about. Personnel said she'd listed "bicycle" under transportation on her application.

Fleet inspection was Friday. They had nineteen buses to certify by then, and Bus 47's transmission had been slipping since Tuesday.

Iris returned with the bolt. "Three-quarter inch?"

"Five-eighths." He pointed to the pneumatic wrench hanging above the workbench. "You can feel the thread gauge."

She picked up the bolt, rolling it between her fingers like a coin, then selected a socket from the pegboard without checking the size marking. It fit.

"How'd you end up here?" Marcus asked, wiping his hands on a rag that had been red once, maybe a decade ago.

"Needed work. Like engines." She crawled under the lift housing, the socket wrench clicking steadily. "How'd you?"

"Needed work. Had a kid on the way." He squatted beside her, knees protesting. "That kid's got two kids now."

"You see them much?"

"Sundays. When the buses run on the Sunday schedule." He smiled. "Which is always, because I fix them."

The clicking stopped. "Coupling's stripped. Needs replacement, not tightening."

Marcus sighed. That meant calling the parts supplier, who'd closed thirty minutes ago, which meant waiting until tomorrow, which meant they'd have eighteen buses ready for Friday, not nineteen. The district supervisors would circle that number in red pen and make it everyone's problem.

"We could swap it with the coupling from the Bay Two lift," Iris said. "That bay's got the cracked foundation. You don't use it for heavy vehicles anyway."

Marcus had worked here since before Iris was born, and that had never occurred to him. He looked at her, this kid who'd never owned a car, who learned the city's transit system by riding it end-to-end on her bicycle repair jobs, who navigated machinery like a safecracker.

"That'll work," he said.

They spent the next hour on the swap, Iris's hands moving confidently through the guts of the lift mechanism while Marcus called out tolerances and specifications. She didn't need the manual. She needed to feel the weight of components, the resistance of springs, the way worn metal whispered its fatigue through her fingertips.

By six o'clock, Bus 47 was purring on the lift, ascending and descending smooth as new.

"Tomorrow we've got the fuel system diagnostics on the 30-series," Marcus said, updating the clipboard. "Twelve buses. You good with injectors?"

"Never done them."

"You will be." He hung the clipboard back on its nail. "You're going to be fine here, Iris."

She pulled off her gloves, studying her oil-stained hands. "You could stay longer, you know. They'd let you."

"Yeah." Marcus switched off the bay lights one by one. "But then you'd never get Bay One. That's the good bay. Best ventilation."

She almost smiled. "That why you're leaving?"

"Nah." He locked the tool cabinet. "I'm leaving because my knees sound like that lift used to, and my granddaughter asked if I could pick her up from school sometimes."

"Buses run past the school?"

"Every twenty minutes," Marcus said. "Because people like us keep them running."

In the parking lot, Iris unlocked her bicycle. Marcus climbed into his sedan—his first car in fifteen years, bought last month for retirement—and they left the depot together, the buses sleeping in their neat rows behind them, ready for tomorrow's routes.
