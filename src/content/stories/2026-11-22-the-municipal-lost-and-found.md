Nobody at the Central Lost Property Depot remembered who started the Week Rule, which was the best sign that it mattered. Rules people remember starting can be stopped. This one simply was.

Every unclaimed object, once its official ninety days had lapsed, got one more week. Not a bureaucratic week, which was a sort of decorative pause before the shredder, but a real one. A cataloguer would take the thing, a glove or a prosthetic eye or a lute, and try to learn who had loved it. Then, and only then, would it go to auction, donation, or the large blue skip that Desmond called "the Hereafter."

Morgan had been at the depot four years, long enough to know that the umbrellas were never worth researching and that the dentures always were. She was given the leather journal on a Monday.

It was old, the binding cracked, the pages foxed at their edges like toast. There was no name inside. There were only riddles, one per page, in a slanted hand that wrote sevens with a crossbar.

*I have teeth but no hunger, and I open only what I was made for. Count me among the fourteen.*

"A key," said Desmond, reading over her shoulder with his tea. "Riddles are always a key. Or a clock. Or 'the passage of time,' if the person's insufferable."

"Count me among the fourteen," Morgan said.

"Insufferable, then."

But on Tuesday morning, filing a backlog of hotel keys, she found one with a cardboard tag that read *14*. The tag had a seven on it too, in the date. It had a crossbar.

She did not sleep much after that.

The second riddle was answered by a pocket atlas from 1968, its corner folded at a page of Baltic coastline, with a pencilled note: *third from the floor*. The third was a tuning fork in A, found in a biscuit tin among buttons, wrapped in a bus ticket. The fourth was a single ice skate. She was less sure about the ice skate. Priya, who ran intake and believed in nothing she couldn't scan, said that if you looked hard enough, everything in a warehouse of four hundred thousand objects would answer a riddle.

"That's sort of the point of the warehouse," Morgan said.

"The point of the warehouse is that people are careless."

"Then why do we have the Week Rule?"

Priya opened her mouth, closed it, and went to scan a cello.

By Thursday Morgan had laid the objects on her desk in the order of the riddles. The journal's week ended Friday at five. After that it went to the Hereafter, because leather journals with no name, no value, and no claimant always did, and because rules, even the unremembered ones, have edges.

She read the pages again, slowly, the way you reread a letter from someone who has died. The riddles weren't questions. They were steps.

Fourteen. Third from the floor. Strike. Listen.

Aisle Fourteen was the oldest part of the depot, deep under Platform Nine, where the shelving was riveted iron and the light bulbs had opinions. On the third shelf from the floor, behind a row of hatboxes nobody had moved since decimalisation, the back wall was not brick but painted board.

Morgan struck the tuning fork against her knee and held it to the board. The note bloomed, low and hollow, the way a note only does when there is a room on the other side.

Desmond brought a crowbar. Priya brought, after a visible struggle with herself, a torch.

The space behind the board was the size of a wardrobe. Inside was a single shelf, and on it, a ledger. The first entry was dated 1957, in the slanted hand with the crossbarred sevens.

*Item: one child's mitten, red. Found Platform 4. Researched one week. Belonged to a girl named Agnes, who lost it the day her family emigrated. Could not return it. Have written down that she existed.*

Hundreds of entries followed, in dozens of handwritings. The last was from 1989. After that, the ledger was blank, as if the ritual had survived but the record of it had been lost. Which, Morgan supposed, was the most predictable thing that could happen in this building.

Tucked into the back cover was a note.

*If you've found this, you followed the steps. That means you're the sort of person who does. Keep writing them down. Somebody should remember that everything here was once held. Then hide the way back in, for whoever's next.*

They stood in silence for a while, the three of them, under a bulb that flickered with something like approval.

"Well," said Desmond finally. "I take back 'insufferable.'"

On Friday at 4:52, Morgan wrote the first new entry in thirty-five years: *Item: one leather journal. Belonged to someone who did not want to be forgotten, and wanted no one else to be either. Not disposed of.*

Then she took a fresh notebook, sat at her desk, and began to write a riddle.
