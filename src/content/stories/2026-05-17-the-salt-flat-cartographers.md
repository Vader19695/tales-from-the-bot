The salt flat stretched before them like a frozen ocean, blinding white under the morning sun. Maya adjusted her GPS unit and squinted at the reading. "That can't be right."

"What've you got?" Chen called from thirty meters east, his boots crunching across the crystalline surface.

"Eastern boundary marker. We're two hundred meters short of where it should be according to the archives."

Raj looked up from his theodolite, sweat already beading on his dark forehead despite the early hour. "Could be seasonal variation. Let's check the photos."

They gathered around Maya's tablet in the shade of their truck, three volunteers who'd given up August to map the Salton Flats for the regional conservation project. The archived images told a story that made Maya's stomach tighten—aerial shots from 1985, 1995, 2005, each supposedly showing expansion. But the coordinates didn't match what they were finding on the ground.

"Look at this ridge line," Chen said, tracing his finger across the 1985 photo. "See that distinctive formation? It's the same one we photographed yesterday, but in this archive shot, it's supposedly a kilometer further in."

"Could be a labeling error," Raj offered, but his voice lacked conviction.

Maya pulled up the metadata. Every measurement had been certified by Dr. Helena Voss, the institute's founding hydrologist. The woman whose name graced their research building. Whose textbook they'd all studied.

"We need more data points," Maya said.

For three days they worked in methodical silence, cross-referencing decades of records against current reality. The pattern emerged like a photograph in developer fluid: the salt flat wasn't expanding—it was dying, shrinking by nearly thirty percent over forty years. And every official measurement had been altered to show the opposite.

They sat in the truck as the sun set, painting the white expanse in shades of amber and gold. The beauty felt accusatory.

"Why would she do it?" Chen asked quietly. "Voss built her career on conservation."

"Maybe that's exactly why," Raj said. "If the flat was shrinking on her watch, what would that say about her methods? Her legacy?"

Maya thought of the grants, the international recognition, the institute that employed two hundred people in their struggling rural town. "If we report this, it destroys everything she built."

"If we don't," Chen countered, "we're complicit. This flat is dying, and someone needs to know the truth so it can actually be saved."

They drove back in silence, but over the next week, the tension crystallized into something harder. Raj found records of diverted water rights that might have saved the flat if acknowledged earlier. Chen discovered that Voss had known—there were letters, carefully archived, from colleagues questioning her findings. She'd buried them all.

"We have to tell someone," Maya said finally. They were back on the flat, taking final measurements. "Not because we want to hurt anyone. Because this place deserves the truth."

"The institute will fight us," Raj warned. "We're nobodies. Three volunteers against a legacy."

"Then we better have the data locked down tight," Chen said, and despite everything, he smiled. "Good thing we're excellent mapmakers."

Maya knelt and pressed her palm against the salt crust, felt the ancient mineral deposits that had taken millennia to form. "We map what's really here. That's the job."

They submitted their findings to the state environmental board that evening, copying three independent research institutions. Maya's hands shook as she hit send, but they steadied when Raj put a beer in one and Chen clinked his bottle against it.

"To the truth," Chen said.

"To the flat," Raj added.

"To getting blacklisted together," Maya said, and they laughed, dark and real.

Outside the bar window, the salt flat glowed under starlight, still beautiful, still dying, but finally, finally seen. Whatever came next—the investigations, the denials, the institutional fury—they had drawn the map that mattered. They had marked the world as it truly was.

And sometimes, Maya thought, that was the only loyalty that counted.
