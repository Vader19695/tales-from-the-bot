The orientation binder for the Dalton Street Municipal Garage was eleven pages long, and nine of them concerned the demolition. Renata read it twice in the booth before her first round, learning that the structure was "functionally obsolete," that the last paying car had left in March, and that her job was to make sure nobody stole the copper before the city could.

The binder did not mention tomatoes.

She found them on Level Four, Row K, at 1:40 a.m., growing out of six orange buckets lined up beneath a cracked skylight. Beside the buckets, someone had built raised beds from shipping pallets and filled them with dirt that had clearly come from somewhere other than a parking garage. There was lettuce. There were beans climbing a trellis made of zip-tied traffic cones. A hand-lettered sign taped to a pillar read: PLEASE DO NOT STEP ON THE CHARD. IT HAS BEEN THROUGH ENOUGH.

Renata stood there long enough for the motion-sensor lights to give up on her.

"You're early," said a voice behind her. "Most of them don't find it until the second week."

The man was in his sixties, wearing the same gray uniform she was, holding a watering can that had once been a bleach jug. His name tag said WENDELL. He did not seem alarmed. He seemed like a man who had been waiting for an inspection he knew he would pass.

"This is a municipal facility," Renata said, because the binder had said it several times.

"It's a municipal hole," Wendell said. "Facilities have people in them."

---

There were five of them on the overnight rotation, and by Thursday she had met them all, mostly by accident and always near vegetables.

Wendell had been a machinist for thirty-one years at a plant that closed in an afternoon. "They sent an email," he told her, pinching suckers off a tomato vine with great tenderness. "Thirty-one years. I got a *read receipt.*" He'd started the first bucket, he said, just to see if anything would grow here. Something did. He took that personally.

Priya worked Tuesdays and Fridays and studied pharmacology in the booth. She had rigged the drip irrigation from a busted standpipe on Level Three, and she talked about water pressure the way other people talked about ex-boyfriends. Her grandmother had kept a garden on a fire escape in Queens for forty years. When the building sold, the new owners hauled every pot to the curb on a Monday. "Nobody was mean about it," Priya said. "That was the worst part. Nobody even noticed."

Big Tomas had planted the peppers. He would not say why. He just said, "They're hot," with grave satisfaction, and Renata decided that was reason enough.

Lorraine, who worked weekends and had a laugh like a car alarm, ran the compost out of a decommissioned parking pay station. She'd been in a shelter two winters ago. "Everything in there was temporary," she said. "Your bed, your locker, your name on the list. I wanted one thing that stayed put." She looked at the beans. "Funny place to pick for that, I know."

---

The demolition date was November ninth. Twenty-three days.

Renata learned this the way she learned everything here: sideways. Wendell had written it on a strip of masking tape stuck to the trellis, a countdown he updated each night in pencil. Nobody talked about it. They just kept watering.

"You could report it," Priya said on Renata's second Friday. She said it kindly, as though offering a way out of a burning building. "You're probationary. They'd probably give you a commendation. Maybe a pin."

"Would they stop the demolition?"

"No."

"Would they save the plants?"

Priya laughed. "They'd fine us for the dirt."

Renata thought about the binder, and all eleven pages of nobody caring. She thought about her own last job, at a call center that had replaced her with a cheerful recorded voice that mispronounced the company's name. She thought about how long it had been since anyone had asked her to keep something alive.

"What needs doing?" she said.

---

They harvested on November eighth.

All five of them came, plus Renata, which Lorraine pointed out was technically a staffing violation, a trespassing violation, and possibly a misuse of a pay station. They filled grocery bags with tomatoes and beans and Tomas's terrifying peppers. Priya dismantled the irrigation with surgical care. Wendell carried each bucket down four flights alone, refusing help, slow and stubborn as weather.

The last thing left was a single pallet bed of kale, too heavy to move.

"Leave it," Wendell said. "Let them knock it down. Let them have to drive the bulldozer through something green."

At dawn, Renata did her final round. On Level Four, Row K, the kale stood dark and upright under the broken skylight. She took out a pen and, below Wendell's masking tape, added a sign of her own.

PLEASE DO NOT STEP ON THE KALE.

Then, after a moment, she crossed out the *step on* and wrote *demolish*, and underneath that, in smaller letters:

WE WERE HERE. IT GREW ANYWAY.

She clocked out at 6:00 a.m. sharp. The binder, she noted, would have been proud.
