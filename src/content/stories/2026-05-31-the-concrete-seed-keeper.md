The first sign of disaster was a jar labeled "Pretty Blues—Spring?"

Margaret stood in the climate-controlled vault of the Riverside Seed Bank, staring at the container in her hand. Inside: delphinium, forget-me-nots, bachelor's buttons, and—she squinted—possibly borage. Four different species, four different germination requirements, all jumbled together because someone thought organization was an aesthetic choice.

She'd retired from the university three years ago, but when the seed bank called desperate for help cataloguing their collection, she'd agreed. Now she understood the desperation.

"Oh, you found my cheerful section!"

Margaret turned to see Jamie, the volunteer coordinator, beaming at her from the doorway. Twenty-six years old, art school degree, zero botanical training. According to the bank's director, Jamie had been "keeping things running" for eight months.

"This is a disaster," Margaret said, setting down the jar with trembling hands. "These seeds have completely different stratification needs. The bachelor's buttons don't even need cold treatment. And are these labeled by... color?"

Jamie's smile faltered. "I created a system. Rainbow organization—it's intuitive. People can find things easily."

"What people? These are *seeds*. They need to be organized by genus, species, collection date, viability—"

"The old system was just numbers and Latin names. Nobody could navigate it."

"I could navigate it. I *built* it."

The silence that followed felt as cold as the vault itself.

Margaret learned the scope of the catastrophe over the next two days. Eight months of Jamie's rainbow sorting had scattered fifteen years of careful organization. Brassicas mixed with nightshades. Perennials with annuals. Heritage varieties—irreplaceable seeds from farms that no longer existed—tossed in with common garden center stock.

And the funding inspection was in five days.

"We'll have to work together," Margaret finally said on the third morning, her voice tight. "Every seed needs proper documentation. I'll identify, you'll record."

"I can help identify too—"

"You catalogued by *color*."

Jamie's jaw set. "I did my best. When I started, this place was falling apart. No staff, no funding, just boxes of seeds nobody looked at. I kept it alive."

"You kept it *colorful*. That's not the same thing."

But Margaret noticed, as they worked, that Jamie had done more than organize by rainbow. The vault's humidity logs were meticulous. The temperature had never varied by more than half a degree. The seed packets themselves, though incorrectly sorted, were carefully sealed, labeled with collection dates Jamie must have preserved from the original containers.

The improvisation drove Margaret mad—storage boxes made from repurposed wine crates, labels printed on the back of old fundraising flyers—but nothing had been neglected. Nothing had been lost.

On day four, Margaret's hands cramped. Seventy-three years old, arthritic fingers that had once precisely cross-pollinated hybrid roses now struggled with small seed packets. She fumbled a container of lettuce seeds—four varieties, finally correctly separated—and watched in horror as they scattered across the metal table.

Jamie's hands were there instantly, younger fingers carefully pinching seeds, sorting them by subtle differences in size and shape.

"The darker ones are 'Black Seeded Simpson,'" Jamie said quietly. "I remember from the original label. These longer ones are 'Romaine Rouge.' I photographed everything before re-sorting it. I thought... I thought I was supposed to make it better."

Margaret's throat tightened. "You kept it alive," she said finally. "That matters."

They worked through the night, Margaret's precision guided by Jamie's unexpected memory for detail. The rainbow jars became properly labeled specimens. The wine crates became "temporary housing, pending funding." The fundraising-flyer labels became "evidence of resourceful stewardship."

When the inspectors arrived, they found a seed bank in transition—not perfect, but living. Margaret's expertise, rebuilt on Jamie's foundation of stubborn care.

"What happens after the inspection?" Jamie asked, as they waited for the verdict.

Margaret looked at the vault, at the thousands of seeds representing possibility, survival, second chances.

"We keep working," she said. "Together. If you'll have me."

Jamie smiled—a real smile this time, not quite rainbow-bright, but warm. "I'd like that. Though I'm keeping my color-coded calendar system."

Margaret almost smiled back. "We'll discuss it."
