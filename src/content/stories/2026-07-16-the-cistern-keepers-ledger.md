The cistern smelled like stone and time. Maria descended the iron ladder first, her headlamp cutting through darkness that had pooled there for over a century. Behind her came Chen and Rodriguez, their boots echoing against concrete as they reached the bottom.

"Readings are off again," Chen muttered, tapping his digital monitor. "Showing empty when we know there's at least six feet."

"Third time this month," Rodriguez said. "Corporate keeps sending the cheapest sensors."

Maria was only half-listening. Her light had caught something wedged between two pipes—a leather-bound book, wrapped in plastic. She pulled it free, her gloves leaving prints in the dust.

"What's that?" Chen asked.

The ledger was heavy, its pages yellowed but intact. Maria opened it carefully. The first entry was dated April 17th, 1962.

*Water level: 14'3". Evaporation rate higher than charts predict. Adjust expectations.*

"Someone's been tracking the water," she breathed.

They crowded around. Page after page of careful observations, each in different handwriting—fountain pen, then ballpoint, then pencil. Different engineers across different decades, all recording the same information: water levels, rainfall patterns, seasonal fluctuations. But there was more. Predictions. Calculations that shouldn't have been possible with the technology of the time.

"Look at this." Rodriguez pointed to an entry from 1987. *Summer drought predicted. Official estimates too optimistic. Will drop to 8'2" by August regardless of rainfall. Supplement immediately.*

Chen pulled up historical data on his tablet. "That's... that's exactly what happened. The city almost ran dry that year."

Maria turned pages, finding more predictions, more warnings. Each proven accurate. The most recent entry was dated three months ago, in shaking handwriting: *Final entry. Trust the patterns. October will be worse than they say. Prepare for 4'6".*

"October is next week," Chen said quietly.

"This is crazy," Rodriguez said. "We can't make decisions based on some anonymous journal. We have protocols, official instruments—"

"That don't work," Maria interrupted.

The next morning, she brought the ledger to their supervisor, Director Patel. He listened, frowning, then shook his head.

"We can't present this to the council. They'll ask for credentials, verification, proper documentation. Some dead engineer's journal isn't—"

"Engineers plural," Maria corrected. "Different people, across decades. All maintaining the same record."

"Which means what? A conspiracy?"

"A legacy," she said. "Knowledge passed down because they knew official channels wouldn't listen."

Patel rubbed his temples. "The official forecast shows adequate reserves through November."

"The official instruments said the cistern was empty yesterday."

When October came, the drought hit harder than predicted. The city's models had been optimistic, based on averaging algorithms that didn't account for shifting climate patterns. But the ledger's prediction—4'6"—that was precise. By the third week, the official monitors had been recalibrated and confirmed it.

Maria stood in the cistern again, this time with authorization to implement emergency measures. The ledger's unnamed authors had included contingency plans: which valves to close, which reserves to tap, how to maximize every gallon.

"The council wants to know who wrote this," Patel said over her radio. "They're calling it classified municipal records, want an investigation."

Maria smiled in the darkness. "Tell them the authors were just engineers. Doing their job."

That night, after the crisis protocols were activated, she returned to the cistern alone. She'd brought the ledger and a new pen. In careful handwriting, she added her own entry:

*October 23rd. Water level 4'6" as predicted. Emergency measures implemented. Crisis managed. To whoever finds this: the patterns are real. Trust them. Trust us. We were here, watching the water, same as you.*

She wrapped the ledger in fresh plastic and placed it back between the pipes. Some knowledge was too important for official channels, too valuable to be lost to bureaucratic turnover and budget cuts. Some truths needed guardians willing to work in shadows, leaving breadcrumbs for strangers they'd never meet.

The water whispered against stone, its secrets kept by the silent dead and the living who heard them.
