The lost-and-found room at the Halvering Street Depot smelled of wet wool, diesel, and the particular vanilla sweetness of old paper going soft. Marguerite Osei had worked there for thirty-one years and still could not tell you where the vanilla came from.

"Forty-two thousand items," she told the boy on Monday. "Give or take an umbrella."

The boy was called Teo. He was seventeen, wore headphones around his neck like a stethoscope, and looked at the shelves (floor to ceiling, aisle after aisle, bins bleeding into bins) the way people look at the sea.

"We have five days," he said.

"We have five days," she agreed. "The city sold the building. Lofts. As if people need more places to leave things."

The rules were simple. Valuables went to auction. Anything with a name went into a box for one last letter. Everything else went to charity or the skip. Teo held up a single brown glove. "Skip?"

"Skip," said Marguerite, and as he tossed it, "Somebody's left hand has been cold since 1998."

He laughed, surprising them both.

By Tuesday they had a rhythm. Teo read the tags aloud (date, route, description in Marguerite's looping hand), and she told him what she remembered, which was more than she should have. The accordion found on the 14 belonged to a man who came every Friday for two years to ask whether anyone had turned in his wife. "He knew she wasn't here. He just liked having somewhere to ask."

"That's bleak."

"He brought me biscuits. It wasn't all bleak."

Wednesday was letters. A shoebox of them, mostly bills, and one unsealed envelope addressed to Miss Cora Lind on Fenwick Road, stamped, never posted. Teo hesitated. "Is it wrong to read it?"

"Extremely," said Marguerite. "Go on."

It was half a page. *I keep starting this on the bus and getting off before I finish. Short version: I was wrong, and I miss the way you hum when you read. If you want me back, leave the porch light on Thursday.*

Teo turned it over. Nothing more. "Did she leave the light on?"

"Did he ever send it?" Marguerite said. "That's the real question."

He put it in the name box. She noticed he smoothed it flat first.

On Thursday they found the painting: a canvas the size of a tea tray, wrapped in a bin bag, tagged *Route 9, 2006*. A harbour at dusk, the water done in confident blues, the sky finished, the boats only pencil ghosts. In the corner, in tiny letters: *tomorrow*.

Teo stared at it a long time. "I used to draw," he said. "I got a place on an art course this summer. I didn't go."

"Why not?"

He shrugged with his whole body. "Mum's on her own. Someone has to work. And it was easier not to go than to go and be bad at it."

Beneath the hum of the strip lights, rain ticked on the corrugated roof, the sound Marguerite had listened to for three decades. She thought of her sister Abena in Leeds, the argument over their mother's house in 2009, and the eleven Christmas cards in a drawer at home, each one written, each one stamped.

"I have a box of things I never posted," she said. "Very tidy. I'm good at keeping things. I'm terrible at returning them."

Neither said anything for a while, which was its own kind of conversation.

Friday was the shoe. Small, red, a buckle, scuffed only at the right toe, no tag at all. They found it under the last shelf as the lorries reversed outside.

"How do you lose one shoe off a kid and not notice?" Teo said.

"You notice," said Marguerite. "You just notice at home. Then it feels too far to come back." She turned it in her hand. "Some things you can't go back for. Most things you can. People assume the distance is longer than it is."

At five o'clock the room was empty. Their footsteps rang. The vanilla smell lingered, unsolved.

Teo held out the red shoe. "Skip?"

Marguerite slipped it into her coat pocket. "Retirement present." She nodded at the harbour painting tucked under his arm. "And that?"

"Someone should finish the boats," he said.

Outside, the rain had stopped. At the bus stop she found Abena's number before she could think better of it. Beside her, Teo was typing an email, deleting it, typing again.

"What are you writing?"

"Asking if the course has a spring intake." He looked up. "You?"

The phone was ringing in her hand, a long way off, in Leeds.

"Halfway there," she said.

The 9 pulled in, lit up and warm, and they got on together, carrying almost nothing.
