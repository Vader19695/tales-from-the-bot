Marta Kowalczyk had been reading Dunmore's pavement for eleven years before it started reading back.

Her job title was Infrastructure Condition Technician II, which meant she walked the east side of the city with a tablet, a measuring wheel, and a can of orange marking paint, logging every crack wider than a quarter inch. Alligator cracking, longitudinal, transverse, block. She knew the vocabulary of failure. Dunmore had been a steel town once, then a bearing town, and now mostly a town of parking lots that remembered being full. The ground shifted under all that absence. The cracks were how it talked.

The first time she noticed, it was a transverse fracture in the alley behind Kessler's Tool & Die, a clean diagonal she'd logged on a Tuesday. On Thursday, a delivery van clipped a dumpster at exactly that angle. She stood over the scrape marks with her coffee and told herself the alley was simply narrow there. Of course things went wrong where things were already wrong.

Then there was the bus shelter on Ferris Avenue. Two longitudinal cracks ran parallel for twenty feet and then, inexplicably, converged into one. A week later she watched a woman drop a folder of papers there, and a man in a Dunmore Water jacket stopped to help her gather them. They were still talking when the 14 pulled away without either of them.

Marta started bringing the field data home.

Her apartment over the laundromat on Second Street had always been cluttered: her late father's survey maps, a framed 1952 sewer plat, coffee cans of drafting pencils. Now butcher paper covered the kitchen table and crept up the walls. She plotted every fracture by hand, colored by type. Blue for transverse, red for alligator, green for the hairlines most technicians didn't bother logging. She marked incidents in pencil: a fender-bender, a lost dog found, a kid's bike stolen, an old man who fell and was caught by a stranger.

The correlation was not perfect. It was something worse. It was *good*.

By October, she could read it the way she read weather. Spiderweb cracking meant small collisions, unkind words, things breaking. Branching meant choices. Convergence meant meetings. The hairlines, the faint green ones, seemed to mark where people would be gentle with each other, briefly and for no reason.

She told no one. Who would she tell? Her supervisor, Dale, measured his life in budget cycles. The city's response to any crack was the same: seal it, patch it, or ignore it until it became a pothole and someone complained.

But in early November, a dense red web opened across the parking lot of the Save-Rite on Halsted, radiating toward the pharmacy entrance. Marta stood at its center at seven in the morning, watching the cracks fan out like a held breath.

She did the only thing she could think of. She coned off the lot's east entrance, filed a hazard ticket, and spent two hours redirecting traffic in her orange vest. Nothing happened. A teenager called her a word she pretended not to hear. Dale called to ask what the hell she was doing.

That afternoon, a car backed into a shopping cart corral at the *west* entrance. No one was hurt. The corral folded like paper.

She went home and sat with the map for a long time.

Maybe she'd moved the accident. Maybe she'd caused it. Maybe a parking lot is just a parking lot, and grief and loneliness make patterns out of anything. Her father had taught her that surveying was about humility: you measure what is there, not what you want to be there.

So she measured.

The next morning she found a new hairline, green, so faint she almost missed it, running down Second Street, past the laundromat, and right up to the cracked concrete of her own stoop. There it met another hairline coming from the building next door, where a retired machinist named Walt Brenner lived. She had nodded at Walt for six years. She didn't know his first name was Walter until she read it on a package.

Marta looked at the two lines meeting. She thought about warning, and dismissing, and how both were ways of standing outside the thing.

On Saturday she bought two coffees and sat on the stoop.

Walt came out at nine with his newspaper. He looked at the extra cup, then at her.

"For me?"

"The sidewalk suggested it," she said.

He laughed, not understanding, and sat down anyway.

The crack beneath them was due to be sealed in spring. Marta wrote it up as *monitor only*.
