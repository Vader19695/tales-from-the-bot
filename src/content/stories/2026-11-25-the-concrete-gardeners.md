The inspector arrived on a Tuesday at 4:40 p.m., which Marisol noted in the logbook because noting things in the logbook was technically her entire job.

"Ruth Abernathy, City Planning," the woman said, holding up a lanyard. She had a hard hat under one arm and a tablet under the other. "I need access to all six levels."

"Lift's been dead since 2021," Marisol said.

"Stairs, then."

"Stairs it is." Marisol unlocked the gate and did not mention that the stairwell between levels three and four smelled faintly of basil.

Abernathy only got as far as Level Two before her phone rang and she left, promising to return the following Tuesday with the structural engineer. On her way out she pinned a laminated notice to the booth window: *Proposed Demolition — Harlow Street Mixed-Use Development. Public comment period closes Friday.*

That night, eleven people sat on upturned crates on Level Four, under the strip of sky where a concrete expansion joint had cracked open years ago. Around them, in neat rows, were three hundred and forty containers: paint buckets, recycling bins, a bathtub Teodor had carried up four flights with Dev, an argument that had lasted the whole climb. The grow lights hummed on a timer, wired to a circuit Marisol was fairly sure the city had forgotten it was paying for.

"We could just take it all down," said Priya, who cleaned the hospital across the road from midnight to eight. "Bins go in vans. Soil goes in the river park. Nobody's the wiser."

"The tomatoes are three weeks from ripe," said Teodor.

"The tomatoes don't have criminal records."

"Neither do I," said Teodor. "Mostly."

Dev had a spreadsheet. Dev always had a spreadsheet. He drove for three apps and slept in a two-bed flat with five people, and the spreadsheet was, Marisol suspected, the only thing in his life that sat still. "Last quarter we grew four hundred and twelve kilos of produce," he said. "That's beans, chard, potatoes, the tomatoes, the squash that tried to kill the bathtub. Forty-one households took food home. That's not nothing."

"It's not legal either," said Priya.

"Neither is the wiring," said Marisol, "and I'm the one who'd lose a job over it."

That landed. Everyone knew the arithmetic. Marisol had the keys. If the garden came out, it came out through her.

Old Mr. Huang, who had been a night porter for thirty years and now mostly just came to water the peppers, cleared his throat. "When I started here," he said, "nobody knew my name. I clocked in, I clocked out. Now people ask me if the chillies are hot yet." He shrugged. "They are not hot yet."

"That's not an argument," said Priya.

"No," he agreed. "It's a fact."

They talked until four. They talked about the shelter on Corbin Street that took their surplus, about Dev's cousin who'd lost his licence, about whether a petition with eleven signatures counted as a movement or a birthday card. Priya walked the rows twice, touching leaves like she was counting children.

"If we hide it," she said finally, "we're just people who used to do something."

They voted with raised hands. Ten to one. The one was Priya, who then volunteered to write the public comment because her handwriting was best.

By Friday they had a statement, photos, Dev's spreadsheet printed in colour at a library that charged twenty pence a page, and letters from the shelter and two primary school teachers whose classes had visited on a "community field trip" Marisol had chosen not to log. Priya delivered it by hand at 4:58 p.m.

On Tuesday, Ruth Abernathy came back with the engineer, a young man who kept saying "fascinating" about the cracks. Marisol walked them up past Two, past Three, and on Four she simply stepped aside.

Abernathy stood very still for a long time. The lights hummed. Somebody had left a watering can shaped like a frog by the bathtub.

"You know I can't approve any of this," she said.

"We know."

"The wiring alone—" She stopped. She walked to the tomatoes, which had decided over the weekend to turn red, and picked one without asking. She ate it standing up, juice on her wrist.

"The development has funding," she said. "It's going ahead. I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

"Okay," said Marisol.

Abernathy looked at the engineer. "That expansion joint," she said. "I'd want a full core survey before anyone signs off. Six months, minimum."

"At least," he said, catching on. "Fascinating joint."

"And the developer's plans include a rooftop amenity space," Abernathy added, "which currently has no tenant. Your comment's on file. People read the file."

When they'd gone, Marisol wrote in the logbook: *4:51 p.m. Inspection complete.* Then, underneath, smaller: *Tomatoes ripe.*

Six months wasn't forever. But it was a growing season, and they knew exactly what to do with one.
