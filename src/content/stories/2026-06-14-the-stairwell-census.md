The discrepancy was minor at first—six inches between the third and fourth floor measurements that didn't add up. Elena Vasquez had been a municipal building inspector for twelve years, long enough to know that old buildings held secrets in their bones, but this felt different.

The Meridian Tower wasn't particularly old, built in 1987, thoroughly unremarkable except for its persistence. While newer buildings rose and fell around it, the Meridian endured, home to sixty-four units spread across sixteen floors. Elena's audit was routine, triggered by a water pressure complaint on the ninth floor.

She found the door on Tuesday afternoon, tucked behind a maintenance panel on the seventh floor that her blueprints insisted should back onto unit 7C's bedroom wall. Instead, there was a narrow stairwell, winding both up and down in a tight spiral that seemed to fold impossibly within itself.

Elena descended first, her flashlight catching brass numbers on unmarked doors: 6½, 5¾, 4⅓. The architectural impossibility of it made her head swim. She knocked on 6½.

The woman who answered was perhaps seventy, wearing a cardigan despite the spring warmth. "Yes?"

"I'm with the city building department. I'm conducting an audit and—" Elena paused. "How long have you lived here?"

"Forty-two years," the woman said. "Since they built it. I'm Mrs. Chen. Is something wrong?"

Elena looked past her into a tidy apartment filled with afternoon light. "Do you know your neighbors?"

Mrs. Chen smiled. "Of course. Mrs. Rodriguez across the hall in 6B. Mr. Patel in 6D."

"And... directly next to you? This wall?" Elena touched the wall beside the door.

"That's exterior, dear. My bedroom has a lovely view of the park."

But Elena had traced the stairwell. That wall connected to another unit, one numbered 6¾. Mrs. Chen's "view of the park" was somehow genuine, and yet impossible.

Over three days, Elena mapped the unmapped stairwell. She met seventeen residents living in units that shouldn't exist, their doors accessible only through this hidden spine of the building. Each believed they lived in a conventional apartment. Each had neighbors they'd known for decades—but none knew of the others, the people living inches away through walls that seemed, to them, to face entirely different directions.

On Thursday, she had coffee with Mr. Berkowitz in 9⅔ and lunch with Diana Torres in 9¼. They lived, according to her measurements, in overlapping space. Both had east-facing living rooms. Both impossible, both real.

She discovered the pattern that evening. Every resident of the unmapped units lived alone. Widows, widowers, lifelong singles. Mrs. Chen's husband had died the year she moved in. Mr. Berkowitz's wife had left in 1994; he'd requested a transfer to a smaller unit that same week.

The building hadn't just hidden them. It had gathered them.

Friday morning, Elena sat in her car outside the Meridian, her completed audit report on the passenger seat. The water pressure issue was resolved—a simple valve replacement. Her inspection had found all systems adequate, no safety violations, no code breaches worth noting.

The unmarked stairwell wasn't in her report.

She thought about Mrs. Chen's smile when she'd mentioned Mrs. Rodriguez, a neighbor she'd known for decades. She thought about the impossible geometries that let Mr. Berkowitz believe he lived in solitude while seventeen others lived their own separate lives within the same impossible spaces.

What they had wasn't isolation—it was being held. The building had done something she didn't understand, something that bent space into a kind of grace.

Elena filed her report that afternoon, marking the Meridian Tower as satisfactory in all respects. That evening, she returned with a bottle of wine and knocked on 6½.

"Mrs. Chen? I was wondering—do you ever get lonely? In such a big building?"

Mrs. Chen considered this. "Sometimes," she admitted. "But less than you'd think. This place has always felt like it was holding me somehow. Does that sound strange?"

"No," Elena said softly. "Not strange at all."

She never mentioned the stairwell. Some mysteries, she decided, weren't meant to be solved. Some were meant to be kept.
