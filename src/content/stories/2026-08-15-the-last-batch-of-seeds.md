The basement smelled of earth and old concrete, a combination Maria had come to associate with purpose. She descended the metal stairs into the warehouse space where sixteen wooden tables held the month's work: mason jars labeled in careful cursive, brown paper packets folded with origami precision, and the logbook—that sacred text of dates and percentages that told them whether their work meant anything at all.

"Seventy-three percent on the Brandywine," called out Dennis from table four, recording the number with satisfaction. Good. Above seventy meant viability, meant those tomato seeds could travel to next year's gardens.

Maria moved to her station, where last month's germination tests waited in their plastic trays. She'd tested fifty varieties from the Kowalski collection—thirty years of saving, harvested from a Detroit community garden before it was paved over. The paper envelopes had come to them soft as cloth, labeled in fading pencil. Mrs. Kowalski's daughter had wept when she'd delivered them, said her mother would have wanted this.

Maria counted the sprouted seeds in the first tray. Eight out of twenty. Forty percent. Her stomach tightened. She moved to the next. Seven out of twenty. Then five. Then three.

By the time she'd checked all fifty trays, her hands were shaking. The average germination rate was thirty-eight percent. Failure. Complete, inexplicable failure.

"Everyone." Her voice cracked the industrious quiet. "I need everyone to look at this."

They gathered—all twelve of them who came monthly to this unglamorous work. Dennis with soil under his fingernails. Yuki, who'd driven three hours from the seed orchard. Robert, who'd founded the collective in 2003 and knew the provenance of every line they'd saved.

"The Kowalski collection is dead," Maria said. "Or close to it."

Robert reached for the logbook, flipping back to where Maria had recorded receiving the seeds. "Storage conditions?"

"Climate-controlled cabinet, same as everything else. Thirty-five degrees, forty percent humidity."

"Could be age," Yuki offered, but her tone was uncertain. "Sometimes genetics just—"

"Look at the moisture rings." Dennis held a seed packet up to the light. The brown paper showed telltale watermarks, darker around the edges. "These got wet."

The silence changed texture. Everyone knew what moisture meant to seeds—the premature activation of enzymes, the fatal trigger that killed viability within days.

"The cabinet's watertight," Maria said. "I checked it this morning. Bone dry. This happened before storage."

Robert's jaw worked. "Who had access to the Kowalski seeds before Maria logged them?"

The question hung there. They all had keys. They all came and went on different schedules, trusting each other with collections that represented decades, sometimes centuries, of careful selection. The Kowalski seeds alone were irreplaceable—varieties adapted to short growing seasons and industrial soil contamination, evolved solutions to problems most seeds would never face.

"Maybe it was an accident," someone said.

But Maria had found the other evidence that morning: a damp towel, folded and hidden behind the humidity monitors. Someone had wetted those seeds deliberately.

She watched her colleagues avoid each other's eyes. Yuki, who worked for a commercial seed company and sometimes made dark jokes about their "competitive intelligence." Dennis, whose own collection had failed certification last year, who'd seemed bitter ever since. Robert, who'd been arguing with the board about expanding, about abandoning their purist stance against any biotech collaboration.

Or Maria herself—she had to consider it—perhaps sleepwalking through some subconscious sabotage, her own mind the traitor.

"We're not going to solve this today," Robert said finally. "I'm changing the locks. New keys go to everyone, but from now on, two people minimum for any collection intake. Nothing gets stored until two people verify conditions."

They nodded, resumed their work. But the rhythm had broken. Maria caught Dennis watching Yuki. Yuki's smile at Robert seemed studied. The quiet that had always meant solidarity now meant suspicion.

Maria sealed the failed Kowalski seeds in a bag labeled "DECEASED COLLECTION—38% VIABILITY—INSUFFICIENT FOR DISTRIBUTION." A lifetime of adaptation, ended. She filed it next to other failures, other small extinctions.

Someone here had killed it. Or no one had, and chaos needed no conspiracy.

She wasn't sure which possibility frightened her more.
