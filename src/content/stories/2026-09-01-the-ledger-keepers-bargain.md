The complaint about the Hendersons' barking dog was gone.

Marla Chen stood in the basement archive, holding what should have been form 3847-B from 1987, watching the last words fade like breath on glass. The paper itself was shrinking, curling at the edges, becoming something gossamer and insubstantial. Within seconds, she held only a translucent slip the size of a postage stamp.

Then nothing at all.

She'd first noticed it three weeks ago. A missing file here, an incomplete record there. Nothing alarming in an archive spanning seventy years of municipal complaints. But yesterday, she'd watched it happen in real-time: the Kowalski boundary dispute of 1993, ten years of bitter legal wrangling, simply *evaporating* from the page.

Marla pulled another box from the shelves. The fluorescent lights hummed overhead, casting the concrete basement in sickly yellow. She opened a file marked "Noise Violations, Elm Street, 1978-1982."

Half the pages were already transparent wisps. As she touched one, she felt it—a curious lightness, like releasing a held breath. The complainant's anger, preserved in carbon copy for decades, was being unmade.

Her phone buzzed. A text from her sister: *You won't believe it. Mom and Aunt Linda are having coffee. Together. After fifteen years.*

Marla's hands trembled. She knew that complaint. She'd filed it herself as a teenager when her mother had called the city about her sister's renovations, the noise, the property line dispute. A family rift preserved in triplicate in Box 847.

She found the box. Empty. Not even dust remained.

Over the following days, Marla became an archaeologist of fading grievances. The pattern was unmistakable. As each complaint dissolved, its corresponding wound in the community healed. The Brennan-Chou parking feud that had poisoned two families for twenty years? Gone. The complainants were now carpooling.

Mrs. Patterson from Oakwood Drive stopped Marla at the grocery store, chatting pleasantly about the weather. Marla remembered her file: forty-seven noise complaints spanning three decades, each one a monument to misery. All of them now erased.

"Isn't it wonderful," Mrs. Patterson said, "how peaceful the neighborhood has become?"

The archive became Marla's obsession. She arrived early, stayed late, documenting the dissolution. She photographed pages before they vanished, but even her digital copies began to corrupt, pixels scattering like autumn leaves.

The question gnawed at her: should she stop it?

She didn't know if she even could.

In the deepest part of the basement, she found the oldest boxes. Complaints from the 1950s—racist zoning grievances, complaints about "undesirables" moving in, petitions to keep neighborhoods segregated. These pages were brown and brittle, but still intact, still holding their poison.

She watched one for an hour. It didn't fade.

Only certain complaints were disappearing. The petty ones. The grievances that had metastasized into feuds, transforming neighbors into enemies over minor infractions. The heavy weight of small injustices.

But the true injustices—the systematic, the cruel, the historically significant—those remained. As if whatever was doing this understood the difference between remembering pain for wisdom's sake and clutching it for spite's.

Marla's supervisor found her there at midnight, surrounded by vanishing papers.

"Go home, Chen. These are just old complaints. Nobody needs them."

"We're supposed to preserve records," Marla said. "That's our job. Memory matters."

"Does it?" Her supervisor gestured at the half-empty shelves. "The city's happier than it's been in decades. Crime's down. Community meetings are actually civil. Maybe some memories are just... ballast."

After he left, Marla sat in the quiet basement, feeling the weight of her decision. She could sound the alarm, demand an investigation, preserve every fading page in hermetically sealed containers. Hold onto the grudges like precious artifacts.

Or she could turn off the light and walk away.

She thought of her mother and aunt, laughing over coffee. Of Mrs. Patterson's smile. Of the Brennans and Chous sharing a vehicle.

Marla turned off the light.

In the darkness, she heard the whisper of paper becoming air, of grievances becoming weightless, of a community learning to breathe without the burden of every small unkindness.

She climbed the stairs and locked the door behind her.

Some truths, she decided, were too heavy to keep.
