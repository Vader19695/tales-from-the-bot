Marjorie Keene had spent forty-three years measuring the world's indifference to precision. Rivers that refused to flow where boundaries insisted they should. Mountains that made surveyors' transits shake with what she'd come to think of as geological laughter. But the salt flats had always been reliable—unchanging, sterile, honest in their emptiness.

Which is why the shrinking bothered her.

Three inches per year, according to her measurements. Not melting. Not subsiding. Just... less. She'd brought it up at the county commission meeting, where Dale Hooper suggested she needed "a hobby that doesn't involve obsessing over dirt." Dale didn't understand that dirt was never just dirt, and salt was a kind of diary.

Marjorie began driving out at different hours, parking her truck at various angles to the flats' edge. She photographed the crystalline surface at dawn when it blushed pink, at noon when it went blind-white. She mapped the patterns of absence.

That's when she saw it: the holes were deliberate. Geometric. Someone was harvesting in careful spirals that, when she stitched her survey maps together, formed something larger. Something intentional.

She bought night-vision binoculars with her pension money.

The harvesters came at 2 AM on Tuesdays and Fridays, seventeen people with hand tools and surgical precision. They worked in silence, filling canvas bags with salt crystals, leaving pockmarks that measured exactly four inches deep. Marjorie watched through binoculars that turned the darkness green and revealed the harvesters' strange choreography—more ceremony than theft.

On the third Friday, she simply walked out to meet them.

"You're the surveyor," said a woman with a hammer and chisel, not looking up from her work. "We wondered when you'd come."

"I'm retired."

"No one retires from needing to know where things are." The woman sat back on her heels. Her name was Yolanda. She had a degree in dendrochronology and hands like dried riverbeds. "Want to see what we're making?"

They took her to an abandoned grain elevator fifteen miles south. Inside, the stolen salt had been transformed into a mosaic covering two thousand square feet of concrete floor. Marjorie recognized it immediately from her aerial maps: it was the town as it had existed in 1952, before the highway bypass, before the mill closure, before the slow hemorrhage of young people toward elsewhere.

"We're running out of time," explained Marcus, a former chemist who now worked at the feed store. "The salt flats are protected land—no mineral extraction, no development. But the state's planning to lease them for lithium mining. Three years, maybe four, and they'll be gone."

"So you're stealing them first," Marjorie said.

"We're translating them," corrected a young man named Sam who spoke with the fervor of the recently converted. "Salt remembers everything—ancient seas, mineral deposits, time itself. We're making a map that shows what was here. What mattered."

The mosaic included details no digital archive would preserve: the precise curve of Stella Mendoza's garden wall, the footprint of the elementary school's original foundation, the location of the cottonwood where teenagers carved their eternities. Every handful of salt had been placed with archaeological reverence.

Marjorie ran her fingers across the textured surface. Salt felt like frozen time—rough, essential, slightly bitter.

"You're destroying evidence to create evidence," she said.

"Yes," Yolanda admitted.

"And when the mining starts, this will be hidden. Underground. Invisible to everyone except from directly above." Marjorie was thinking of satellites, of the long view, of futures that might look back.

"We're burying treasure for archaeologists who don't exist yet," Marcus said. "It's either absurd or essential. Some days I can't tell which."

Marjorie was quiet for a long time, studying the map of her town rendered in crystals stolen from public land, preserved through minor felony, hidden through necessity.

"Tuesday nights," she finally said. "I have better topographical maps. You're three inches off on the water tower's position."

Yolanda smiled. "We work in silence."

"I'm aware."

"And it's technically theft."

Marjorie thought about all the invisible thefts—of place, of memory, of the small permanences people build their lives around. She thought about the difference between what's legal and what endures.

"I'm retired," she repeated. "I have time to steal."
