The cistern walls wept in spring. Moisture traced the old brick like tears, and Maya pressed her palm against the cool surface before descending the final steps into what they called the Archive. Twenty feet below the abandoned pump house, the air tasted of earth and forgotten things.

"You're late," Raj said without looking up from his sorting table. Photographs surrounded him in careful stacks—the discarded memories of strangers, rescued from estate sales and dumpsters across three counties.

"Traffic." Maya unwound her scarf. "Where's Chen?"

"Back corner. Says he found something."

Chen sat cross-legged on the floor, surrounded by photographs dating from the 1970s. His flashlight made long shadows on the vaulted ceiling. He looked up when Maya approached, his expression careful.

"Show her," Raj said, joining them.

Chen held out a photograph: a ribbon-cutting ceremony. A shopping plaza, new and gleaming. Mayor Hendricks with oversized scissors, councillors flanking him in wide lapels. Chen flipped it over. Someone had written in faded blue ink: *Riverside Plaza opening, June 12, 1974. Three weeks after the vote.*

"So?" Maya asked.

Chen produced another photo. Same plaza, different angle. Then another: a community meeting, faces tense with worry. Another: houses, small and neat, some with children on porches. All from the same neighbourhood that no longer existed.

"I've been cataloguing the Kowalski collection all month," Chen said quietly. "Her husband was a city planner. These were in a box marked 'Riverside Renewal Project.'" He spread out more images. "Look at the dates."

Maya knelt beside him. The photographs told a story in reverse: a thriving Black neighbourhood, then bulldozers, then empty lots, then the plaza's grand opening. But it was the documents mixed among the photos that made her stomach tighten—meeting minutes, vote tallies, assessor reports with property values deliberately lowered.

"The vote was supposed to be in July," Raj said. "After the community appeal. These show they moved it up, voted before the appeal hearing."

"They knew they'd lose if people had time to organize," Chen added. "Sixty-two families displaced. The compensation was based on those fraudulent assessments."

Maya sat back on her heels. She'd grown up three blocks from Riverside Plaza. Her mother still shopped there. And Mrs. Patterson, who'd lived next door her whole childhood—Mrs. Patterson, who sometimes stared at that plaza with an expression Maya had never understood—she would have been one of those families.

"We have to—" Raj started.

"Do we?" Chen interrupted. "Mayor Hendricks is dead. Half the council members too. But their kids still live here. Hendricks Junior runs the community center."

"Which was built with guilt money, probably," Raj said.

"Or genuine attempt to make amends," Chen countered. "We don't know their hearts."

Maya walked to the cistern's far wall, where they'd mounted their completed catalogue cards. Thousands of anonymous moments, preserved and organized. They'd started this project to save ordinary history from oblivion. Now they'd stumbled into something that wanted to stay forgotten.

"Mrs. Patterson is eighty-three," Maya said finally. "She rents a one-bedroom apartment. So do a dozen other families I grew up around. They never got made whole."

"Exposing this won't give them their houses back," Chen said gently. "It might just poison what community we have left."

"Or it might mean reparations," Raj argued. "Justice, even delayed."

The cistern dripped. Water found the low places, as it always had.

"Historical truth isn't neutral," Maya said. "Keeping this hidden is also a choice."

She photographed each document, each image, her phone's flash bright in the underground dark. Chen didn't stop her. After a moment, he helped organize them chronologically.

"We present it to Mrs. Patterson first," Maya said. "And the other families. Let them decide if the truth comes out. It's their story. Their grief. Their choice whether to carry it into the light."

Raj nodded slowly. Chen closed his eyes, breathed out.

They worked until midnight, building a second archive within the archive. By morning, they would begin the careful work of returning history to those it had been taken from.

Above them, the city slept, unaware of what the water had preserved.
