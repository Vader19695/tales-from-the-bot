The letter arrived on a Tuesday, taped to the garden gate like a parking ticket.

"Well, shit," said Marcus, reading it for the third time. Behind him, tomato plants climbed their cages in the August heat, indifferent to municipal code violations.

Diane looked up from deadheading zinnias. "How bad?"

"Section 47-B, unauthorized agricultural use of city property. Thirty days to clear out or face fines." He turned the paper over as if better news might be written on the back. "Someone complained about 'unsanitary conditions and unregulated plant distribution.'"

The seed library sat exactly where it had for five years: a dented gray filing cabinet under the tool shed's overhang, each drawer labeled in Rosa's careful handwriting. *Tomatoes. Beans. Flowers. Herbs.* Inside, hundreds of envelopes held seeds saved from plants that had fed this neighborhood since before the parking lot existed, back when these blocks were farmland at the city's edge.

By Thursday evening, twelve gardeners crowded into the shed. The meeting had the feel of a funeral, everyone speaking in hushed tones until Chen said, "This is ridiculous. We should fight it. Get media involved, embarrass them into backing down."

"And if we lose?" Diane asked. "Then we've painted a target on our backs. Every community garden in the city becomes a problem to solve."

"We could move the cabinet," suggested James, who'd been gardening here since his retirement. "Keep it at someone's house. Operate underground."

Rosa shook her head. The overhead light caught silver in her hair—she'd been saving seeds since before most of them knew what heirlooms were. "You know why this works? Because it's *here*. Because a kid can walk past and get curious. Because someone's neighbor brings their primo from the old country and shares it, and suddenly six plots are growing it. You hide it, you kill it just as dead as the city would."

Marcus leaned against the doorframe, watching the group fracture into camps. Fight or flight, public or private, risk or safety. The same arguments recycling like compost.

"What are we protecting?" he asked.

The shed went quiet.

"Because if it's just the seeds, James is right—we pack up the cabinet tonight, keep it alive in somebody's basement. But if it's..." He gestured vaguely at the garden beyond, where Mrs. Kowalski's Polish cucumbers grew next to the Nguyens' bitter melon, where teenagers from the youth program had planted sunflowers in the shapes of their initials.

"If it's the idea that this space belongs to us," Diane finished. "That we made something from nothing and nobody gave us permission first."

Rosa pulled an envelope from her pocket—rattlesnake beans, dark purple seeds that predated the parking lot by a century. "My grandmother gave me these. She got them from her grandmother. You know what they told me? Guard them, but not so carefully you forget to plant them."

Chen laughed despite himself. "That's either very wise or completely impractical."

"Both things can be true."

They voted just after sunset. Fight publicly, but protect the collection—divide the seeds among a dozen homes, make copies, create redundancy. If the garden fell, the library would survive. If the library was lost, the garden could rebuild it. Neither perfect safety nor reckless exposure, but the messy middle ground where actual living things had always existed.

Marcus wrote the response letter that night, careful and polite, requesting a public hearing. Rosa made a list of who would take which seeds. Diane started a phone tree. They'd probably lose, but they'd lose together, and something would survive—maybe not everything they wanted to save, but enough. There was always enough, if you knew how to save seed.

When Marcus returned to the garden the next morning, someone had planted morning glories along the fence, their purple trumpets already climbing toward the sun. He didn't recognize the variety. Probably an heirloom, something that had waited patiently in the filing cabinet's darkness for exactly this moment of defiance.

He made a note to save seeds from it in the fall, assuming there was a fall to save them for.

Assuming, as gardeners must, that there would be another season.
