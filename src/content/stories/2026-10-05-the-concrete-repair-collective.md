The Fulton Street Repair Café occupied Level P2 of a parking garage the city had stopped needing, and on Tuesdays, Thursdays, and Saturdays it smelled of solder, burnt coffee, and Marco's egg sandwiches. Painted arrows on the concrete still pointed toward an EXIT that now led to the lamp table.

Walt, seventy-three and formerly of Consolidated Hydraulics, ran the small mechanisms bench as if it were a surgical theater. Priya, sixteen, needed forty service hours for graduation and had logged sixty-two, a fact she disclosed to no one, least of all her mother. Marco had been a cabinetmaker until the shop closed, and now he fixed chair legs for free and told himself it was temporary.

Mrs. Ines Albescu arrived on a Thursday with a shoebox held level against her chest, like a cake.

"It hasn't played since 1984," she said. "I'm told you people like a challenge."

Inside was a walnut music box the size of a hardcover book, its lid inlaid with a pale wooden swallow. Walt put on his magnifying visor. Priya put down her phone, which Marco noted on the whiteboard under MIRACLES.

By Saturday, they were obsessed.

The spring was sound. The cylinder pins were intact. The comb had only two cracked teeth, which Walt pronounced "survivable." Marco cleaned decades of grime from the case and rubbed it with oil until the swallow seemed to lift off the lid. Still the thing wouldn't turn. The spring held its tension like a breath that wouldn't come out.

It was Priya who found it, shining her phone light into the gap beside the governor, the tiny fan that regulates the tune's speed. Wedged against the blades was a folded scrap of paper, tight as a seed.

"That's not a manufacturing defect," Walt said quietly.

The paper had been there long enough to fuse with the old lubricant. Marco tried it with tweezers and it flaked like pastry. Under magnification, they could make out a few words in blue ink through the translucent fold: *Ines* — and *if you ever* — and, near the edge, *forgive*.

Nobody touched it after that.

"We pull it, it disintegrates," Walt said. "Then it plays."

"Or we leave it," said Marco, "and it's a box."

"It's not *our* box," said Priya, who had spent eleven unrecorded hours on it.

They argued through two pots of coffee. Walt held that a machine is a promise to do a thing, and a broken promise should be mended. Marco said some cracks in furniture he'd never fill, because they were where somebody's grandfather had dropped it on the way out of a country. Priya said that maybe a message nobody reads isn't a message, it's just a lock, and then looked embarrassed, as though she'd said something too large for the room.

On Tuesday, they told Mrs. Albescu everything.

She sat on a folding chair beneath a sign that read COMPACT CARS ONLY and looked at the box for a long time.

"His name was Tomas," she said. "He gave it to me the week before he went to work in Hamburg. He was supposed to send for me. He sent a letter instead, saying he'd met someone. I wound the box that night and it wouldn't play. I thought it had broken out of sympathy." She smiled faintly. "I was twenty-nine. I was dramatic."

"He must have put it there before he left," Priya said. "Before the letter."

"So he knew before he left," said Mrs. Albescu. "Or he was afraid he would." She folded her hands. "Either way, he made sure I'd have to break it to hear the song. That's very Tomas. He never could say anything plainly."

Walt cleared his throat. "We could photograph what's visible first. Ma'am."

"You've read what's visible. *Forgive.*" She shrugged, a small, complete gesture. "I did, eventually. It took about thirty years. I don't need the rest of the sentence."

She nodded at Walt.

He worked slowly, the whole café gone silent around him, a teenager boxing a toaster and a man holding a disassembled vacuum both pausing to watch. The paper came out in pieces, then dust. Marco brushed the dust into a small envelope without being asked, and Mrs. Albescu took it and put it in her coat pocket.

Walt released the catch.

The governor spun. The cylinder turned. And out came a lullaby, thin and bright and slightly off-key at the two cracked teeth, filling the concrete level all the way to the painted exit arrows.

Mrs. Albescu closed her eyes. After a moment, she began to hum along, a half-beat behind, as if catching up with someone she had once known very well.

Priya didn't log the hour. Marco erased MIRACLES from the whiteboard and wrote, underneath, in smaller letters: *Some.*
