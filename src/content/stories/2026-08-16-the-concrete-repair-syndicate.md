Maurice had spent forty years designing bridges that would outlast him. Now he spent his days shoveling cold-patch asphalt into potholes on Riverside Avenue, and honestly, the irony wasn't lost on him.

"You're doing it wrong," he told the kid—Tyler, nineteen, court-ordered community service for spray-painting a water tower. "You need to compact the edges first."

Tyler didn't look up from his phone. "Dude, it's a hole. I'm filling it. That's literally the job."

"And we'll be back here next week when it fails."

"Great. Job security."

Kiana laughed, a sound like wind chimes that seemed incongruous with her safety vest and steel-toed boots. At forty-two, she'd retrained after her knees gave out on dancing, and she treated asphalt repair with the same precision she'd once applied to fouettés. "He's right, Tyler. We do come back here. Every three weeks. Same six blocks."

Maurice straightened, his lower back protesting. "You've noticed that too?"

"Hard not to. I've got the repair logs." She pulled out her tablet. "Seventeen visits to this neighborhood since April. Everywhere else in the city? Average two, maybe three."

That evening, Maurice stayed late in the depot office, scrolling through records that nobody had bothered to make particularly secure. The pattern emerged like rust through paint: Riverside neighborhood repairs failed consistently. A private contractor—Donnelly & Sons—was then hired for "comprehensive reconstruction" at triple the rate. City Councilman Frank Donnelly's cousin owned the company.

The next morning, Maurice showed them his findings in the cab of their truck.

"So what?" Tyler said. "Rich people being corrupt. Breaking news."

"Someone's sabotaging our work," Kiana said quietly. "Making us look incompetent."

"Making taxpayers pay three times for the same road," Maurice added.

Tyler finally put down his phone. "Okay, that's actually messed up."

They watched in silence as Supervisor Krantz emerged from his office, climbing into an expensive truck that seemed well beyond a civil servant's salary.

"We report it," Kiana said.

Maurice shook his head. "To who? The councilman who's profiting? The city manager who approved the contracts?"

"So we just let them steal?"

"I didn't say that."

That night, Maurice sat at his drafting table—he still kept one, couldn't break the habit—and designed. Not a bridge this time. Something smaller. Something that would last.

Over the next week, the three of them worked Riverside with meticulous care. Maurice engineered each repair to specification, calculating compaction rates and cure times. Kiana documented everything with photos and videos, her dancer's eye for detail capturing every precise movement. Tyler, it turned out, had an unexpected talent for social media.

"Your generation thinks everything's performative," Maurice grumbled as Tyler filmed their work.

"Your generation thinks everything happens in boardrooms," Tyler countered. "Watch and learn, bridge guy."

The video went up on a Friday: a time-lapse of proper pothole repair, intercut with footage of Donnelly crews doing shoddy work, overlaid with the cost comparison. Tyler had a knack for making municipal corruption almost entertaining. By Monday, it had sixty thousand views.

By Tuesday, the local news had picked it up.

By Wednesday, the state attorney general's office had opened an investigation.

Krantz didn't show up Thursday morning. Neither did Councilman Donnelly's cousin.

Maurice found Kiana and Tyler in the depot break room, watching the news coverage on someone's phone.

"We could've gotten fired," Kiana said.

"We still might," Maurice agreed.

"Worth it though," Tyler said. He was already filling out an application for the city's technical training program.

Maurice looked at these two unlikely colleagues—a dancer learning to read asphalt density, a kid learning that caring about things wasn't actually uncool—and felt something he hadn't experienced since his last bridge dedication.

Pride in work that would outlast him.

"Same crew tomorrow?" he asked.

"Someone's got to fix these potholes properly," Kiana said.

Tyler grinned. "Job security, baby. The legitimate kind."

Outside, summer sun baked the fresh asphalt on Riverside Avenue, where three strangers had filled holes in more than just the road.
