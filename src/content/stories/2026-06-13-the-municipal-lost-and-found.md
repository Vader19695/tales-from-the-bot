Elena had worked at Central Lost & Found for eleven years without incident. She preferred it that way. The warehouse sprawled across four city blocks, organized by item category and decade of acquisition, which meant she could spend entire weeks in Umbrellas 1990-1999 without speaking to anyone.

The discrepancy appeared on a Tuesday.

A Mrs. Chen called about her wedding ring, which she'd just lost at the farmer's market. Elena pulled up the database, expecting nothing—items took at least forty-eight hours to process through intake. But there it was: one gold band, size 6, inscribed "Forever MC & TC," logged into the system six days prior.

Elena checked the intake timestamp three times. She pulled the physical item. The inscription matched Mrs. Chen's description exactly.

"When did you say you lost this?" Elena asked when Mrs. Chen arrived.

"This morning. Around nine."

The database showed a 3:47 PM entry from last Thursday. Elena's supervisor, Marcus, would say it was a clerical error. Marcus said everything was a clerical error, which was why half their filing system still used carbon paper.

Elena didn't mention it. She returned the ring and opened a spreadsheet.

By Friday, she'd found eleven more cases. A laptop reported missing Tuesday that had been logged the previous Saturday. A child's stuffed elephant that arrived before the child left it on the bus. A parking validation ticket that entered the system three days before the date stamped on it.

She began working backwards through the archives.

The pattern held in the 2010s, though less frequently. In the 2000s, the instances increased. The 1990s showed one or two pre-lost items per month. The paper records from the 1980s required four days to audit, but she found them: objects catalogued before they'd been lost, sometimes weeks in advance.

The earliest case she could document was from 1967. A brown leather wallet belonging to James Fisk, logged March 3rd. According to a yellowed incident report, Fisk had called on March 15th asking about a wallet he'd lost "this morning." The intake clerk noted the odd timestamp but released the item without investigation.

Elena cross-referenced every pre-lost item she could verify. No pattern in the objects themselves—wedding rings and wallets and keys and glasses. No pattern in the owners' demographics. The only consistency was the lag time: never more than fourteen days between logging and actual loss.

On her fifteenth day of investigation, she found the note.

It was tucked inside a 1973 eyeglass case, discovered only because she was physically examining every pre-lost item still in storage. A small card in faded blue ink: "Check the Patterson umbrella."

The Patterson umbrella—black, wooden handle, monogrammed CP—had been logged in 1982. Christine Patterson reported it missing in 1983, nearly eleven months later. Elena had flagged it as an outlier: the longest pre-loss interval in the database.

Inside the umbrella's handle, unscrewed with effort, she found a rolled piece of paper.

"You're looking at this wrong. They're not arriving early. You're receiving them exactly on time. Check who processed intake on 3/3/67."

Elena returned to the 1967 logbook. The intake clerk's initials: EKM.

Her initials. Elena K. Morrison.

Impossible. She was thirty-four years old.

She pulled every pre-lost item's intake record. Different handwriting, different clerk names, spanning decades. But when she looked closer at the 1967 entry, something caught her attention—the handwriting wasn't the original clerk's. It was squeezed in the margin, added later. A correction.

She checked the 1982 Patterson umbrella entry. Same thing. A marginal addition in different ink.

Someone had been accessing the archives and altering records, making it appear items arrived before they were lost. But they'd left messages in the objects themselves, messages that could only be found by someone who looked closely enough.

The next note was in a 1991 watch: "The messages aren't for you. They're from you. You'll understand in twenty-three years."

Elena looked at the date on the note: 1991.

Twenty-three years later was 2014.

The year she'd started working Tuesdays in Archives. The year she'd gotten the keys to the restricted pre-1970 storage room.

She sat in Umbrellas 1990-1999 for a long time, thinking about temporal logistics and the particular kind of person who would spend decades planting messages in lost objects.

Then she pulled out her pen and began writing notes.
