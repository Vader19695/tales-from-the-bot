The work order said eight concrete planters, Fifth Street underpass, remove and dispose. Dolores Reyes read it twice in the truck, then handed it to Marcus, who was new enough to still read work orders.

"Ornamental," he said. "Says they're ornamental."

"They were, in 1998."

They parked on the shoulder and walked down with a pry bar and a clipboard. The underpass smelled like wet concrete and diesel. The planters ran along the east wall, waist-high, the kind the city bought by the hundred during a beautification push nobody remembered funding. Dolores expected dead junipers and cigarette butts.

Instead she found collards. Thick, blue-green, stripped from the bottom up the way you harvest when you plan to come back. Tomatoes staked with rebar. Peppers. A squash vine trained along a chain-link panel someone had wired to the wall. Under the leaves, a black drip line ran planter to planter, back to a rain barrel fed by a downspout off the overpass.

"Somebody plumbed this," said Teo, crouching. "Gravity-fed. That's clean work."

"Thank you," said a man behind them.

He was maybe sixty-five, in a canvas jacket, holding a five-gallon bucket of what turned out to be compost. He said his name was Abel. He did not ask what they were doing there. He looked at the clipboard and the city logo on the truck, and he knew.

"Eleven years," he said. "Fourteen people eat from this most weeks. More in August."

Dolores didn't promise anything. She asked when the tomatoes would be done. He said October. The removal was scheduled for next Thursday.

---

Back at the yard, she pulled the original complaint. It came through the councilmember's office: *planters obstruct visibility at underpass exit, safety hazard for vehicles turning onto Alder.* Attached was a photo of the north end of the underpass.

The next morning she went back alone with a measuring wheel and the traffic engineering manual. She stood where a driver would stop at Alder and looked south. Two planters, the northernmost pair, sat inside the sight triangle. The other six were thirty feet back along the wall and blocked nothing.

She called her supervisor, Ray Okafor, from the cab.

"The complaint is about two planters," she said. "The order says eight."

"The order says eight because the council aide said 'the planters.' I've got a councilmember who wants this closed out and a budget line that pays for one trip with the forklift, not three." He sighed. "Dolores. If I leave six planters full of vegetables under an overpass, somebody's going to ask who's liable when someone gets hurt down there."

"If someone adopted them, they'd be liable."

Silence. The Adopt-a-Spot program let neighborhood groups take responsibility for city fixtures—litter pickup, plantings, a signed liability waiver. It was mostly used for medians.

"Groups," Ray said. "Not individuals. Not unhoused individuals, especially. They need an address and insurance."

"I'll find a group."

"You've got until Wednesday at noon for the paperwork. If it's not on my desk, we pull all eight. And the two in the sightline go regardless."

---

St. Brendan's ran a food pantry four blocks east. The coordinator, a brisk woman named Patrice, already knew about the garden; some of Abel's collards ended up on her tables. She had insurance. She also had a board that met monthly.

"I can sign it," she said. "I can't tell your gardeners what to grow, and I won't. But if I'm on the hook, I need someone to call when the water barrel overflows onto the sidewalk."

That part fell to Abel, who was not happy about a church's name going on his garden. He said so plainly. Dolores didn't argue. She laid out the options: the pantry's name on a form, or a forklift on Thursday.

Abel went quiet, then asked whether the form said anything about who harvests.

It didn't.

He agreed to check the barrel every morning and gave Patrice the number of a phone he kept charged at the library. Patrice signed Tuesday afternoon. Dolores walked it to Ray's desk herself.

---

Thursday, they brought the forklift anyway. Teo and Marcus strapped the two north planters, soil and all, and Dolores moved them—slowly—forty feet south to the end of the row, clear of the sight triangle, where Abel had already extended the drip line with a coupling and a length of hose he'd bought from the hardware store.

One tomato plant lost a stem in the move. Abel cut it clean, put it in his bucket for compost, and checked the emitters.

Ray drove by at eleven, looked down Alder, and got back in his car.

Dolores closed the work order: *Two planters relocated per sightline requirement. Six transferred to Adopt-a-Spot, St. Brendan's. Complete.*

Then she went back out to the truck, where Marcus was eating a pepper.
