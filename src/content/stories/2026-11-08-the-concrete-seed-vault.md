Edith Varga had parked on Level P4 for eleven years because it was the only level where nobody dinged her Buick. She discovered why on a Tuesday at 11:40 p.m.: nobody went down there except a teenager with a backpack full of spray paint and an alarming lack of self-preservation.

"You're vandalizing a load-bearing wall," Edith said.

The girl froze, can mid-hiss. "It's not load-bearing. It's hollow. Listen." She rapped it with her knuckles, and the concrete answered with a deep, wet echo, like a cough in a cathedral.

Edith was seventy-three and had spent forty years at the university herbarium. She knew the sound of a sealed room. She also knew, from long experience, that curiosity was the one vice that improved with age.

"Do you have a flashlight?" she asked.

The girl's name was Dez. She had a flashlight, a crowbar she claimed was "for art," and a working knowledge of the garage's security cameras, which she explained were "mostly decorative, like your hat." Edith let that pass. The crack in the wall was already wide enough for a skinny person, and with some persuasion from the crowbar, it became wide enough for a skinny person and a retired botanist with a bad hip.

Beyond it, stairs descended into darkness that smelled of rust, limestone, and something Edith hadn't smelled in decades: old paper holding its breath.

The cistern was enormous, a vaulted brick chamber flooded ankle-deep with water so cold it ached. Their flashlight beams skittered across the surface and up the walls, where iron shelving rose in orderly rows. On the shelves sat hundreds of sealed tins, each stenciled in careful white paint: *CIVIC SEED PRESERVATION LEAGUE — 1934.*

Edith pried one open with trembling fingers. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper, were seed packets labeled in faded fountain pen. *Cherokee Trail of Tears bean. Brandywine tomato, Mrs. Halloran's strain. Prairie larkspur, collected from the lot behind the Fourth Street mill.*

"Is that good?" Dez whispered.

"Some of these varieties are listed as lost," Edith said. Her voice sounded strange to her. "People went hungry in 1934, and somebody spent their spare hours saving *seeds*. They were betting on a future they'd never see."

Pinned to the nearest shelf, beneath oilcloth, was a note: *To whoever finds this: the city is a garden that forgot. Remind it.*

Dez read it twice. "That's a tag," she said with professional respect. "That's literally a tag."

They sat on the stairs, shoes squelching, and argued in the way that only two stubborn people at midnight can.

"We have to report it," Edith said. "This is historically significant. There are protocols."

"Protocols," Dez said, "are how you get a plaque. You know what's above us? The Hollister Avenue redevelopment. They'll call this a 'structural liability,' pump in concrete, and put up a sign saying *Heritage Lofts*." She shook the tin gently. Seeds whispered inside like rain on a roof. "These weren't saved to be a plaque."

Edith wanted to argue. She had spent her career filing specimens in climate-controlled cabinets, labeled and dead. She thought of every rare plant she had pressed flat and catalogued while the meadows it came from turned into parking lots. Possibly this parking lot.

"Germination rates on ninety-year-old seed are terrible," she said finally.

"So we plant a lot of them."

"It's technically theft of municipal property."

"It's technically *returning* municipal property. Look at the note. They wanted it planted."

Edith looked at the girl's paint-stained hands, at her own liver-spotted ones, and felt something shift in her chest, a pressure like a root testing soil.

"We do it properly," she said. "We document everything. Every variety, every lot. I'm not letting you scatter Mrs. Halloran's tomatoes like birdseed."

Dez grinned. "Deal, Professor."

It took them all summer. Edith tested germination in egg cartons on her windowsill; roughly one seed in thirty woke up, and each one felt like a resurrection. Dez scouted the city's empty lots and marked each planting with a small stenciled symbol: a seed cracking open, painted in white.

The larkspur came up first, behind the old mill, violently blue against the rubble. Then beans climbed a chain-link fence on Delancey. Then tomatoes nobody had tasted in ninety years ripened beside a shuttered laundromat, and the neighborhood kids ate them warm, juice running down their wrists.

People noticed. People started copying the stencil. Strangers began leaving seeds of their own on Edith's doorstep in envelopes marked *For the League.*

In September, the city council held a ribbon-cutting at the Delancey lot, praising the "spontaneous community greening initiative." A councilman posed beside the beans.

Edith and Dez watched from across the street.

"Should we tell them?" Dez asked.

Edith adjusted her decorative hat. "Let them have the plaque," she said. "We have the garden."

Below them, in the dark, the tins were already half empty, and the city had begun, slowly, to remember.
