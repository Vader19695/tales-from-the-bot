The flowers appeared on a Tuesday, which was fitting because Tuesdays were when everything weird happened at the municipal parking garage on Fletcher Street.

"You seeing this?" Marcus held his push broom at parade rest, staring at the third level's northwest corner.

I followed his gaze. Purple blooms—some kind of wildflower—sprouted from the expansion joints where the concrete slabs met. A week ago, there'd been nothing but the usual cigarette butts and oil stains.

"Huh," I said, which was my standard response to municipal mysteries after fifteen years with the city.

By Thursday, the purple had company. Yellow flowers clustered around the stairwell. Orange ones traced the drainage channels. Chen from maintenance took samples, muttering about invasive species and structural integrity. Our supervisor, Diane, made us photograph everything for the weekly report.

"They're just flowers," said Ray, our youngest crew member, still optimistic enough to believe the city made decisions based on common sense.

"'Just flowers' is how it starts," Diane said. "Then it's endangered habitats and environmental impact statements. I've seen parking meters trigger six-month delays."

The following Monday, Diane called an emergency meeting in the garage's office—a glorified closet that smelled like motor oil and disappointment.

"City's accelerating the renovation," she announced. "They're sealing the garage in two weeks. The flowers have them spooked about foundation damage."

"Two weeks?" Marcus dropped into the folding chair that only he was brave enough to use. "We were supposed to have until August."

"Apparently, someone's been deliberately planting seeds. They found nursery receipts and gardening equipment hidden in the electrical room." Diane's expression was carefully neutral. "They want to know if we've seen anything suspicious."

The five of us exchanged glances. We'd all seen Mrs. Chen.

Not Chen from maintenance—Mrs. Chen, his mother, who'd been coming to the garage every morning at six for the past three months. We'd assumed she was doing tai chi or meeting friends. The canvas bag we'd thought held exercise equipment probably held trowels and seed packets instead.

"The city's offering a reward for information," Diane continued. "Five hundred dollars. No questions asked."

Ray's student loans could use five hundred dollars. Diane's daughter's braces could too. But none of us spoke.

After the meeting, I found Chen smoking by the south ramp, which meant things were bad—he'd quit two years ago.

"My mom's seventy-six," he said without preamble. "My dad died in this garage. Heart attack on level four, fifteen years back. Security guard found him next to his Buick."

I remembered. I'd been the one who'd stayed with Chen until his family arrived.

"She used to leave flowers at the spot," he continued. "But the maintenance crews kept sweeping them away. Policy." He laughed bitterly. "So she started planting them in the cracks. Figured if they grew on their own, nobody could complain about littering."

"They're beautiful," I said, because they were.

"They're destroying city property," Chen said. "And Mom's on a fixed income. If they press charges—"

"Nobody's pressing charges," Marcus said, appearing from the stairwell with Diane and Ray. "We're the only ones who know, and we don't know anything. Right?"

Diane sighed. "I could lose my pension."

"You won't," Ray said. "Because we're going to fix this."

We spent the next week documenting everything: growth patterns, species identification, structural assessments. Ray's girlfriend, an environmental studies major, connected us with a professor who'd been studying urban biodiversity. Marcus's cousin worked for the local news.

By the time the renovation started, the "Concrete Garden" was a minor viral sensation. The environmental professor testified that the flowers actually helped channel water away from structural joints. The city council, facing an election year, suddenly discovered their deep commitment to "green infrastructure initiatives."

They kept three levels open during renovation. Installed proper planters. Added interpretive signs about native species. Even acknowledged Mrs. Chen at a ceremony she refused to attend.

Every Tuesday, I still sweep level four. The flowers there grow thicker than anywhere else, a riot of purple and yellow blooming from cracks in the concrete. 

I never sweep them away.

Some policies aren't worth following.
