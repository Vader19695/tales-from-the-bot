The logbook lived in a steel cabinet bolted to the wall of Pump Station Four, forty feet below Calloway Avenue, and nobody had read it properly in years. You signed it, you wrote the gauge reading, you went home. The city had switched to telemetry in 2009. The book was a formality, like the fire extinguisher nobody had ever pulled.

Rosa Ibarra only opened it past the current page because the binding finally split. The back half slid onto the concrete, and when she gathered it up, a column of numbers in faded pencil caught her eye. They ran down the margin beside the official readings, never in the same hand twice.

"Marcus," she said. "Teo. Come look at this."

Marcus Bell set down the sampling kit with the sigh of a man eight months from retirement. Teo Okafor, who'd been on the crew less than a year and still wore his hard hat even when there was nothing to bump, crouched beside her with a flashlight.

The official column, in ink, matched what the telemetry said: the cistern at 31 percent, then 28, then 24 as the drought dragged into its third summer. But the pencil column, labeled only *B*, never dropped below sixty. Rosa flipped back through the decades. 1998. 1983. 1971. Always the same small *B*, always higher. And every few years, a note at the bottom of a page.

*Report the main basin only. B is not on the drawings. —H.L., 1974*

*Pumped 2 ft from B into the east main during the Mercer fire. Saved the block. Logged as hydrant pressure test. —D.A., 1981*

*If you are reading this, you are the keeper now. Measure B on the first of the month. Tell no one above the rank of operator. —J.W., 2003*

Teo read it twice. "There's a second chamber?"

It took them forty minutes to find it. Behind the overflow weir at the north end, a section of brick had been mortared in a slightly different color, and set into it was a valve wheel painted the same gray as the wall. Below the wheel, a brass staff gauge, polished by decades of thumbs, read 6.4 feet.

Marcus did the math out loud, slowly, like he wanted to be wrong. "That's maybe two million gallons. That nobody upstairs knows about."

"Somebody knows," Rosa said. "Twenty somebodies. Just not anybody with a parking space."

They ate lunch in the truck without talking much. The radio said the council would vote Thursday on the emergency sale of municipal water rights to Halvorsen Agricultural. The deal was justified, the mayor said, by the fact that the city's own reserves were nearly exhausted.

"We have to report it," Teo said finally. "That's two million gallons of public water. It's falsified records. If an auditor finds this before we say anything, we're done. All three of us."

"Report it to Dunleavy?" Marcus snorted. "Dunleavy will have it on the Halvorsen inventory by Friday."

"You don't know that."

"I know Dunleavy."

Rosa watched the heat shimmer off the asphalt. "Read the 1981 one again."

Teo did. The Mercer fire. Six blocks of rowhouses in the Flats, the hydrants gone dry because the main had been throttled for a downtown hotel opening. Someone named D.A. had opened a valve that didn't officially exist and kept a neighborhood from burning.

"It's not a theft," Rosa said. "It's a spare tire. Every keeper who came before us decided the city couldn't be trusted with it. Not because they were saints. Because they'd seen what happens when the people deciding never have to stand down here."

"And what if we're wrong?" Teo asked. "What if the city really needs it, and we're sitting on it because some guy in 1974 had a hunch?"

Nobody answered that, because it was a fair question.

On Thursday the council approved the sale. On Friday, Dunleavy emailed a memo reminding all field staff that log entries must match telemetry exactly.

That afternoon, Rosa took the logbook down from the cabinet and taped the binding back together. She wrote the official reading in ink: 22 percent. Then she held the pencil out.

Teo looked at it a long time. "If someone needs it," he said, "really needs it, and it's sitting here, I'll open that valve myself. I'm not keeping it for nobody."

"That's the whole point," Marcus said. "That's what they all wrote."

Teo took the pencil. In the margin, in small careful letters, he wrote: *B — 6.4.* Underneath, after a moment, he added: *It's for the Flats, for whoever's next. Not for sale. —T.O., R.I., M.B.*

Rosa closed the cabinet. Above them, the city went on wanting water, and forty feet down, in the dark behind the weir, it waited.
