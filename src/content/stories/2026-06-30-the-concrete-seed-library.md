Dr. Helen Ortiz ran her finger along the rim of a mason jar labeled *Brandywine Tomato—1982*. The seeds inside rattled like tiny promises. Around her, the repurposed parking garage hummed with fluorescent light and the whisper of careful hands sorting through decades of botanical memory.

"We got the letter," Marcus said, emerging from behind a filing cabinet marked *Native Grasses M-Z*. He still wore his postal uniform, though his shift had ended an hour ago. He held up the official envelope with the city seal.

Helen didn't reach for it. She'd known it was coming ever since the new zoning board took office. "How long do we have?"

"Thirty days to obtain proper licensing or cease operations." Marcus set the letter on a work table cobbled together from parking garage concrete stops and plywood. "They're calling us an 'unlicensed agricultural distribution center.'"

"We're a library," Helen said quietly.

"They don't see it that way."

A clatter from the southwest corner made them both turn. Jenna, the teenager assigned to them through the court-ordered community service program, had knocked over a box of seed packets. She scrambled to gather them, her dyed-green hair falling across her face.

"Sorry," she muttered. "These were already falling apart."

Helen walked over and knelt beside her, joints protesting. The packets were hand-labeled in her own writing from fifteen years ago: *Monarch Waystation Mix*. The paper had gone brittle, but the seeds inside would still be viable if stored properly.

"Nothing we can't fix," Helen said.

That evening, the three of them sat around the plywood table with instant coffee in chipped mugs. The garage stretched around them, twenty-seven filing cabinets arranged in rough alphabetical order, hundreds of jars on metal shelving, envelopes of seeds organized by season and climate zone. Seven years of work. Fourteen years if you counted Helen's initial collection.

"The licensing requirements are impossible," Marcus said, reading from his phone. "Quarterly inspections, insurance, germination testing for every accession. The fees alone would be fifteen thousand a year."

"Could we fundraise?" Jenna asked.

"We'd need to go public. Right now we operate on trust—people take what they need, donate what they can. The moment we formalize, we're competing with commercial seed companies. And those companies have lawyers."

Helen sipped her coffee. Through the garage's street-level windows, she could see ankles and shoes passing by. People walking dogs, carrying groceries, living their lives on top of this hidden archive.

"There's that condemned maintenance tunnel," Jenna said. "The one that connects to the old subway station. I saw it when I was looking for the circuit breaker."

Marcus shook his head. "No ventilation. No light. We'd lose everything to mold in six months."

"So we fight," Helen said.

They both looked at her.

"I'm too old to be romantic about this," she continued. "But I'm also too old to pretend that moving deeper underground is anything but surrender." She stood, walked to the nearest cabinet, pulled out a drawer. Inside, hundreds of small envelopes nested in neat rows. "Every one of these represents someone who cared enough to collect, dry, label, and preserve. Garden club ladies. Farm families. Indigenous seed keepers. Some of these varieties don't exist anywhere else."

"The city doesn't care about that," Marcus said, but his voice lacked conviction.

"Maybe not the zoning board. But the city is also the people who come here every week. The immigrant families growing vegetables from home. The school teachers doing garden projects. The kids who—" She glanced at Jenna. "Who keep coming back even after their hours are complete."

Jenna's cheeks reddened. "I like it here. It's quiet."

"It won't be quiet if we fight," Marcus warned.

"No," Helen agreed. "It'll be messy and probably futile. But futile isn't the same as meaningless."

She pulled out an envelope of seeds—*Blue Lake Bush Beans*—and set it on the table between them. Such a small thing. Such ordinary hope.

"We have thirty days," she said. "Let's make them count."

Marcus looked at Jenna. Jenna looked at the seeds. Outside, the city rumbled on, indifferent and immense.

"Okay," Marcus said finally. "Okay."

They got back to work.
