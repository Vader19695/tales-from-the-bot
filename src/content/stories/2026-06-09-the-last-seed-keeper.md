The vault exhaled its familiar cold breath as Marie descended the concrete steps for the second-to-last time. Forty-three years she'd been making this journey into the belly of the old Red Line tunnel, and her knees kept a precise count of every one. The fluorescent lights flickered on in sequence, responding to her presence like a faithful dog. The hum—that eternal, methodical drone of climate control—wrapped around her like a threadbare blanket.

She went first to the Brandywines, as she always did. Drawer C-447. Her fingers, gnarled now but still precise, pulled the handle. Inside, glass vials nested in foam, each containing seeds the color of sunset promises. She lifted one vial. Felt its weight.

Too light.

Marie's breath caught. She unscrewed the cap with trembling hands. The seeds inside were perfect—too perfect. Machine-uniform. She'd catalogued the original Brandywines herself in 1987, each one unique, some cracked, some scarred. These were simulacra.

She checked the Mortgage Lifters next. Then the Cherokee Purples. The Moon and Stars watermelons. All replaced. All flawless forgeries.

The vault seemed to hold its breath with her.

---

*September 1994. The Cherokee Purples arrived in a shoebox, wrapped in a dead woman's kitchen towel. Her grandson stood in Marie's office, hat in hands.*

*"Grandma Rose said these seeds were all she brought from Tennessee. Said they fed her family through the Depression." His eyes were wet. "Said they were worth keeping."*

*Marie had taken the towel with reverence. The seeds inside were wrinkled, dark as old blood. "They'll be safe here," she'd promised. "Forever."*

*Forever. That word echoed now through forty-three years of identical promises.*

---

Marie spent three days checking drawers, her list growing. Seventy-eight varieties replaced. The pattern was surgical, meticulous. Only the rarest specimens. Only those thought extinct in the wild. Someone had studied the collection the way she had, loved it perhaps, but reached a different conclusion about its purpose.

She found the evidence on Thursday in the security logs that no one thought she knew how to read. Access times that matched only one other person's schedule. David Chen, her protégé of seven years. The man she'd chosen to inherit her position.

Her hands shook as she pulled up his file. Impeccable credentials. Letters of recommendation that glowed. But there—buried in his undergraduate thesis, which she now read for the first time—was the philosophy she'd missed: *Seed banks are beautiful tombs. Seeds exist to grow, to feed, to continue. Preservation without propagation is a kind of death.*

The vault's hum seemed louder now, almost interrogative.

---

*June 2003. The Mortgage Lifters came from a seed swap in Ohio. An old man, Italian accent thick as August humidity.*

*"My father grew these in the '30s. Sold them for a dollar each—a mortgage payment per tomato, they said." He'd laughed, gaps in his teeth. "Now nobody knows them. Nobody grows them."*

*The vault had accepted them into its cold embrace. Perfect preservation. Perfect stasis.*

---

Marie sat in her office on Friday, her final report open on the screen. One phone call and David's career would end. The theft would be documented. The replacements would be noted. The seeds—wherever they'd gone—would be hunted down, possibly destroyed as evidence.

She thought about the vault, its thousands of drawers holding futures that would never arrive. She thought about Rose's grandson, whether he'd ever tasted a Cherokee Purple, whether his children would. She thought about the seed bank's mission statement: *Preservation for posterity.*

But whose posterity? The one locked in drawers, or the one growing in soil?

Marie closed the report. She typed instead: *Final inspection complete. All collections verified and intact. I leave the vault in capable hands.*

On her last day, she descended the stairs one final time. The fluorescent lights greeted her. The climate control hummed its eternal song. She placed her hand on drawer C-447, felt the cold metal.

"Grow well," she whispered to the seeds that were no longer there.

Then Marie climbed back into the light, leaving the vault to its new keeper, who understood what she had finally learned: some futures must be freed to become real.

The vault hummed on, a cathedral of calculated temperatures, guardian now of both seeds and secrets.
