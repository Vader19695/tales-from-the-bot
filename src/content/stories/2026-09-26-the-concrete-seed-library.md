The Henderson Municipal Services Building had been ugly in 1973, and forty-seven years had not improved it. Grey concrete, narrow windows, and a profound commitment to right angles—it squatted on Fifth Street like a filing cabinet turned to stone.

"Five days until demolition," Cheryl announced, consulting her clipboard. She was the volunteer coordinator, which meant she'd been copied on the most emails. "Parks and Rec needs us to clear out any salvageable furniture. Also, apparently there's a seed vault."

"A what?" Marcus looked up from the desk he was labeling with masking tape that read *POSSIBLY ASBESTOS???*

"Seed vault. Basement level. Some seventies food security initiative that got defunded." Cheryl squinted at her printout. "Says here it's 'probably fine to ignore.'"

"That's not ominous," offered Diane, who taught biology at the community college and had only volunteered because her department head said it would "demonstrate civic engagement."

The three of them stood in the lobby, joined by two teenagers completing court-ordered community service, both named Josh. The Joshes were inseparable and communicated primarily through vaping and shrugs.

The elevator to the basement had been broken since 1987, so they took the stairs. The air grew cooler, damper. At the bottom, they found a corridor lined with doors labeled in faded lettering: RECORDS, JANITORIAL, ARCHIVES, and finally, AGRICULTURAL CONTINGENCY.

Marcus tried the handle. "Locked."

Younger Josh produced a screwdriver from his oversized jacket. "Dude, the hinges are on this side."

Ten minutes later, they'd removed the door entirely.

The smell hit them first—rich, loamy, almost sweet. Then the sound: a soft rustling, like paper shuffling itself. Diane found a light switch. The fluorescent tubes flickered, and they all inhaled sharply.

The room was alive.

Metal shelving units still lined the walls, but the orderly rows of labeled seed packets had exploded into an impossible garden. Wheat stalks climbed toward the flickering lights. Tomato plants, gone feral and rangy, draped across collapsed cardboard boxes. Squash vines had conquered an entire corner, their leaves the size of dinner plates. Mushrooms—white, brown, and an alarming purple—bloomed from the concrete floor in dense clusters.

"How?" Cheryl's voice was barely a whisper.

Diane stepped inside, careful not to crush anything. "There's a leak." She pointed to a water-stained pipe running along the ceiling, steadily dripping. "And the lights must have been on a circuit that stayed active. Seeds stored together, with moisture and light..." She knelt by a cluster of flowering beans. "They just... did what seeds do."

A beetle, iridescent green, crawled across her shoe. She didn't brush it off.

Marcus pulled out his phone, started taking pictures. "This is incredible. This is—do you know how many species established themselves here? There's a whole ecosystem. Look, those mushrooms are decomposing the old cardboard, which feeds the soil, which—"

"Which will be rubble in five days," Cheryl interrupted, but her voice had lost its bureaucratic certainty.

They stood in silence, surrounded by life that had organized itself in the dark, with no permission slips or planning committees. Just water, light, and time.

"We could document it," Diane said. "Collect samples. I know people at the university—"

"My cousin does like, urban exploration videos," Older Josh offered. "Gets millions of views."

Cheryl was already typing on her phone. "I'm emailing the mayor's office. And the newspaper. And that guy from Channel 7 who does the 'Local Wonder' segments." She looked up, and for the first time since Marcus had met her, she was smiling. "They want to knock down an eyesore? Fine. But they're going to have to acknowledge what they're destroying."

"The developer's not going to care about mushrooms," Marcus said gently.

"Maybe not," Diane said, pressing her hand against a tomato plant's stem, feeling its persistent, improbable warmth. "But we will. And that's worth something."

The demolition happened on schedule. But first, three volunteers, two teenagers, and eventually seventeen reporters, four scientists, and a surprisingly emotional city councilman spent four days cataloging every species in the basement. They collected seeds. They took cuttings. They told the story.

The Henderson Building fell. The garden, scattered now into a hundred new locations, grew on.
