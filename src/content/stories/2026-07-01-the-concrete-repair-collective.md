Margaret had repaired 847 items in three years of Saturday volunteering at the Riverside Repair Depot. She kept count in a small notebook with a blue cover, each tally mark representing a lamp rewired, a chair leg reglued, a zipper replaced. The arithmetic of repair pleased her engineer's mind—problems solved, waste diverted, community served.

But it was the toaster that finally broke her system.

She recognized it immediately when Carlos brought it to her workstation that March morning. A Sunbeam Radiant Control, chrome finish dulled by age, the model her mother had owned. The same toaster she'd fixed in November. And September before that. And six times before that, stretching back through her notebook's pages like a recurring decimal.

"Heating element again?" she asked Carlos, who managed intake.

"How'd you know?" He consulted his form. "Donor says it won't toast evenly."

"Same donor?"

Carlos squinted at the address. "Mrs. Chen, Parkside Apartments. You think she doesn't know it's broken?"

Margaret turned the toaster over in her calloused hands, finding the screws worn smooth from repeated removal. Someone had been inside this machine many times. Someone who knew exactly what they were doing.

That afternoon, Margaret fixed the heating element—a simple matter of reattaching a wire deliberately, cleanly disconnected. Not broken. Unhooked. She wrote Mrs. Chen's address in her notebook with a question mark.

---

The Parkside Apartments loomed gray against an grayer sky. Margaret clutched the toaster, rehearsing diplomatic approaches. Perhaps Mrs. Chen was lonely. Perhaps she had dementia. Perhaps—

"You're from the depot." The woman who answered was Margaret's age, maybe older, sharp-eyed behind wire-rimmed glasses. Her apartment was immaculate, smelling of jasmine tea and lemon oil.

"Mrs. Chen, I've repaired your toaster seven times now. The same issue, always."

"Eight times, actually. You weren't there in February." Mrs. Chen gestured to the kitchen table. "Sit, please."

Margaret sat, confused. "You're breaking it on purpose."

"I'm unbreaking other things." Mrs. Chen poured tea into delicate cups without asking if Margaret wanted any. "Do you know Mr. Kowalski in 3B? His wife died last year. He hasn't spoken to anyone since. But he goes to the depot every Saturday, fixes radios, teaches young people about circuits. He needs that place."

Margaret's engineer's mind tried to process this. "You're donating a fake repair so—"

"So the depot stays busy. So the grant committee sees steady numbers. So Carlos keeps his part-time job." Mrs. Chen's hands wrapped around her cup. "I worked in nonprofits for thirty years. I know how these things survive. They need to prove their value, every quarter, every grant cycle. They need numbers."

"But it's fraud."

"It's a toaster, repaired with genuine skill, returned to working order, used until I need it again. Nothing false about that." Mrs. Chen met her eyes. "You think I don't see you there every Saturday, Margaret? Teaching people that things have value beyond their purchase price? That's religion in an age of disposable everything. The depot is a church, and I'm just keeping the collection plate full."

Margaret looked down at her calloused hands, marked by sixty years of fixing things. She thought of Mr. Kowalski's careful soldering. Of teenage volunteers learning that repair was possible. Of the strange communion of their Saturday mornings, the congregation of the broken and fixable.

"The heating element wire," Margaret said slowly, "would last longer if you disconnected it at the far terminal instead."

Mrs. Chen smiled. "Is that so?"

"Less stress on the connection point. It would be... more authentic. As a natural failure point."

"That's very helpful. Perhaps you could show me?"

Margaret pulled her toolkit from her bag, the tools she always carried, the instruments of her faith. "The thing about Radiant Control toasters," she said, unscrewing the base plate, "is they were built to last forever. But nothing lasts forever without a little help."

Mrs. Chen refilled both their teacups.

Outside, the March wind scattered leaves and trash. Inside, two engineers bent over their work, maintaining the careful machinery of community, one deliberate disconnection at a time.
