The first note turned up in March, wedged between the cushion and the frame of Seat 14C on the Number 9 bus, the last one to limp into the Harmon Street depot each night.

Dolores found it while vacuuming. She was sixty-three and claimed she had vacuumed more bus seats than any woman alive, though nobody had ever checked. The paper was folded into a neat square. In small, careful handwriting it read:

*You are allowed to rest before you are finished.*

"Well," Dolores said, and put it in her apron pocket, and did not mention it to anyone for two weeks.

By then there were four more. *Grief is just love with nowhere to go. Give it somewhere.* And: *Call the person. The worst they can do is not answer, and you've already survived that.* And, slightly less profound: *The vending machine on Fourth gives two Twix if you hit the left side.*

That one was how Marcus found out. He came into the break room holding two Twix bars like a man who had witnessed a miracle.

"Who told you about the machine?" Dolores asked.

"Nobody. I just—" He stopped. "You found one too."

Then Ana, the youngest, who had quit nursing school one semester short and not told her mother, admitted she had been collecting them since April. She kept them pressed in a paperback she never read.

After that, it became a ritual. At 1:40 a.m., when the Number 9 groaned in, all three of them found reasons to clean it together. Dolores took the front, Marcus the middle, and Ana went straight for 14C. Most nights, there was a note.

They read them aloud over vending-machine coffee. Dolores's husband had died in the autumn, and she had kept his reading glasses on the kitchen table for eleven months. The night a note said *You don't have to put away the things you aren't ready to put away*, she went quiet, and Marcus pretended to be very interested in his Twix.

Marcus hadn't spoken to his son in three years. The argument had been about something so stupid he was embarrassed to remember it. He kept the *Call the person* note in his wallet behind his bus pass, where it pressed against him like a thumb.

Ana's favorite was *Starting over is not the same as starting from nothing.*

Hal, the Number 9's driver, knew who it was. "Older lady. Green coat. Gets on at St. Bernadette's, rides to the end, rides back. Always sits behind the back door." He shrugged. "Says she likes the company."

"What company?" Ana asked. "It's one in the morning."

"Me, I guess," Hal said, looking pleased.

Then, in late September, the notes stopped.

One empty night was nothing. Three was a coincidence. After two weeks, Ana started checking 14C twice, then checking the seats around it, then checking under it with a flashlight, as if the notes might have fallen and rolled away.

"Hal says she hasn't been riding," Marcus reported. "Not since the eighteenth."

They sat in the break room with their coffees going cold.

"We could go to St. Bernadette's," Ana said. "Ask at the front desk. Green coat, nice handwriting, possibly a vending machine expert."

"And say what?" Marcus said. "'Hi, we've been reading your secret notes in the garbage'?"

"They weren't garbage."

"I know they weren't."

Dolores turned her cup slowly. "She never signed them. Maybe she didn't want to be found. Maybe that was the point. You can say true things to strangers that you can't say to anybody else." She paused. "I would know. I told a bus seat about Frank."

Nobody laughed, but it was close.

They didn't go to the hospital. Instead, the next night, Ana tore a page from her paperback, the blank one at the back, and Dolores wrote on it in her best church-bulletin handwriting:

*Thank you. Whoever you are, wherever you are. You kept us company. — The night crew.*

Ana tucked it deep into 14C.

It stayed there for six nights. On the seventh, it was gone.

In its place was a new square of paper, the handwriting shakier now, but unmistakable.

*I was in the hospital longer than planned. I'm home now. I didn't know anybody was reading. I'm so glad somebody was.*

Ana read it aloud twice. Dolores put on Frank's reading glasses, which she had started carrying, to read it a third time.

Marcus went out to the parking lot. Through the window, they watched him take out his wallet, unfold something small, and lift the phone to his ear.

Dolores and Ana did not watch long. It was his call to make. Besides, the Number 12 had just come in, and there were a great many seats on it, and every one of them had room for a note.
