The city maintenance crew found the seeds on a Tuesday, which somehow made it worse. Tuesdays were for patching potholes and clearing storm drains, not for existential quandaries about bird intelligence.

Marcus had been the one to drill into the hollow pillar first. They were supposed to be assessing structural integrity on the old Westfield Overpass—abandoned for fifteen years, slowly crumbling into the weeds. When the concrete dust cleared and seeds started pouring out like a slot machine jackpot, he'd actually laughed.

"What the hell?" said Yuki, catching a handful. She rolled them between her fingers with practiced ease—her grandmother had been a farmer. "These are heirloom tomatoes. And these... God, Marcus, these are expensive. Heritage squash, looks like. Some kind of melon."

By the time they'd drilled three more test holes, they'd collected enough seeds to fill two five-gallon buckets. Each pillar was stuffed with them, packed tight into the hollow cores like nature's safety deposit box.

"Pigeons," announced Chen, the crew's least chatty member, pointing upward.

They all looked. Above them, the old Greenhouse District's luxury rooftop gardens still clung to the office buildings, mostly untended since the tech bust. And between the gardens and the overpass, a steady stream of pigeons flew back and forth, purposeful as commuters.

"They're hoarding," Yuki said, watching one bird disappear into a gap in the concrete. "But why here?"

Marcus shrugged. "Dry. Protected. Maybe they just liked the acoustics."

That was the Tuesday. By Wednesday, everyone knew about it.

The drought had entered its third year. The aquifers were dry, the reservoir was at eleven percent, and the city's vertical farms were running on emergency power that wouldn't last past September. People were already leaving—those who could afford to. The rest were learning to live with dust and rationing and the particular anxiety of watching clouds that never quite delivered rain.

So when word got out about thousands of pounds of viable heirloom seeds—rare varieties, pre-drought genetics—sitting in an abandoned overpass, the phones started ringing.

"It's not technically anyone's property," said the city attorney on speakerphone. "The pigeons certainly don't have legal standing."

"The greenhouses above do, though," countered Yuki. "These seeds came from their stock. Technically it's theft, even if birds did it."

"The greenhouses are bankrupt," Marcus pointed out. "There's nobody left to file a claim."

The crew was sitting in their truck, parked in the overpass's shadow. Around them, the drought-dead grass crunched in the hot wind. Chen was watching the pigeons through binoculars, saying nothing.

The city manager wanted the seeds harvested immediately, distributed to the emergency agriculture program. The local ecology group wanted the site protected as a "spontaneous avian seed bank." The university wanted to study it. Everyone had a plan that made perfect sense, except for the part where nobody could agree.

"Question," Chen said suddenly. "How long have they been doing this?"

They all looked at him.

"The overpass closed fifteen years ago. Some of these seed varieties went extinct commercially eight, ten years back. The pigeons have been preserving them. By accident, sure. But they've been doing what we couldn't."

Marcus felt something shift in his chest—that strange feeling when you realize the world is weirder and more competent than you'd given it credit for.

"We take half," Yuki said finally. "Leave half. The birds keep their archive, we get our planting stock."

"That's not what the city manager—"

"The city manager isn't here," she interrupted. "We are. We're the ones with the drills."

Marcus thought about intention and accident, about survival and ownership, about pigeons with better disaster planning than most humans. He thought about his daughter, seven years old, who'd never tasted a tomato that wasn't grown in a factory.

"Half," he agreed.

Chen nodded once and started the truck.

They worked through the afternoon, drilling carefully, taking seeds from the lower pillars while leaving the upper ones untouched. Above them, pigeons circled and cooed, carrying more seeds to add to their hoard—still working, still planning, still saving for a future they couldn't possibly understand but somehow, pragmatically, believed in anyway.

Maybe intention didn't matter. Maybe only the work did.
