Maya had been apprenticing under Roland for three months before she realized the bells were talking.

Not to her, obviously. She'd been deaf since birth, felt sound only as pressure in her bones. But she'd learned to read Roland's angular handwriting, his careful notes about which bell sequence marked the 5:47 express, which announced platform changes for the overnight freight. She'd memorized the patterns through his instructions and the vibrations that traveled through the floor of the tower at Union Station, twenty feet above the main concourse.

What she noticed first was the cleaning crew.

At 2:14 AM on a Tuesday, Roland rang the pattern for a schedule delay that hadn't been announced. Maya checked the board—everything on time. But down on the concourse, she saw Luz from custodial services glance up at the tower, then redirect her cart toward the eastern food court instead of the western wing.

Maya started keeping a separate notebook.

By week four, she'd mapped seventeen bell sequences that didn't match any official schedule. At 11:30 PM each night, a pattern she privately called "Dinner Bell" preceded workers gathering at the closed Vietnamese restaurant by Gate 7, where someone always left out thermoses of soup and yesterday's rice. A quick three-chime sequence brought the night security guards to the family bathroom on the second floor, where apparently they'd been taking turns watching each other's kids in the storage closet. There was a pattern that meant "Immigration sweep"—platform crews would vanish into maintenance tunnels within seconds.

"You're noticing things," Roland said one morning, not quite a question.

Maya signed: *Should I not?*

He was one of the few people at the station who'd bothered to learn ASL. He signed back: *Safer if you don't.*

But he kept ringing the sequences. And Maya kept watching how a dozen illegal pattern meant survival for maybe two hundred people—workers whose shifts didn't align with food banks, whose kids couldn't afford formal childcare, who needed to know which supervisors were checking papers that week.

The automation notice came down in November.

"Budget efficiency measure," Roland explained, showing her the memo. "Computer can handle all the signals. More reliable, they say." He was sixty-three, three years from retirement with full pension. His hands shook slightly as he set the paper down.

The bells would be decommissioned in six weeks.

Maya spent three days thinking. Then she took the elevator down to the main concourse at 2 AM and waited outside the Vietnamese restaurant.

Luz found her there. "You're Roland's girl."

Maya nodded, showed her the notebook. Luz flipped through it, her expression unreadable. Then she went inside and came back with seven other people.

"How much time?" someone asked.

Maya held up six fingers.

"Can you ring them? After?"

Maya nodded.

"Will you?"

Here was the question. Roland had warned her a dozen ways: Don't get involved. These aren't your people. You need this job. But Maya thought about the single mother from the platform crew who studied for her GED during breaks. The Honduran guy who shared his insulin with the diabetic janitor who'd been denied health coverage. The network of small mercies that kept people alive in the gaps between what the city pretended to provide and what it actually did.

She signed slowly so they could read her hands: *I need help learning the computer system.*

A security guard named Marcus laughed. "You want to hack the automation?"

*I want to make sure the bells still say what they need to say.*

Over the next month, Maya learned things she definitely wasn't supposed to know about the transit authority's digital infrastructure. Marcus's boyfriend worked in IT. The overnight dispatcher owed Luz a favor. Someone's cousin had the system passwords.

On automation day, officials gathered in the tower for a ceremony. The computer took over at noon. Roland accepted his early retirement package. Maya stayed on as "Digital Systems Monitor"—someone still needed to be in the tower, they said, just in case.

At 11:30 PM that night, Maya typed a specific sequence into the computer.

Down in the concourse, the bells rang Dinner Bell perfectly.

Through the tower window, she watched Luz look up and smile.
