The jackhammer's rhythm changed when Marcus hit the hollow spot. He killed the engine and knelt on the concrete floor of Parking Level B2, running his gloved hand over the hairline crack that had started this whole maintenance project.

"Got something," he called to his supervisor, Janet, who was reviewing blueprints under a utility light.

She walked over, frowning at her clipboard. "There's nothing on the plans. This garage went up in '73."

They brought in ground-penetrating radar the next morning. By noon, they'd outlined a brick cistern roughly eight feet across, sealed beneath two feet of concrete. By Friday, the city engineer had approved opening it—mostly out of curiosity, partly to rule out structural issues.

Marcus expected puddles and rust. Instead, when they lifted the concrete slab and descended the ladder, their flashlights illuminated rows of mason jars on wooden shelves. Thousands of them, organized with military precision. Each jar contained seed packets wrapped in wax paper, and each packet bore careful handwriting in fading blue ink.

"*Brandywine tomatoes, 1968. Heat-resistant. Mrs. Chen's favorite.*"

"*Kentucky Wonder beans, 1970. Excellent for children's gardens.*"

"*Zinnia, State Fair Mix, 1972. Butterflies.*"

Janet photographed everything while Marcus carefully lifted jars, reading labels aloud like an archaeologist narrating a dig. Someone had created a library down here. A time capsule of growing things, preserved against some feared catastrophe.

The concrete above them suddenly felt heavier.

---

The research took three weeks. Janet started with city records, cross-referencing the parking garage's construction date. She found the answer in a 1973 newspaper on microfiche, a four-paragraph story titled "Urban Renewal Clears Blighted District."

The "blight" had been a six-block neighborhood called Riverside. The article mentioned one "minor delay": relocating a community garden managed by the Parks Department.

In older records, Janet found him: Thomas Whitmore, municipal gardener, employed 1951-1973. She discovered his retirement paperwork, his modest pension. A notation that he'd "declined alternate assignment" when Riverside was demolished.

The library's obituary archive provided the rest. Thomas Whitmore, died 1989, age 71. Survived by niece. The funeral notice mentioned his passion for "teaching neighborhood children about growing things."

Janet made calls until she found the niece, a woman named Carol living two states away.

"Uncle Thomas loved that garden," Carol said, her voice softened by memory. "When they announced the demolition, he spent months preparing. He knew exactly what would be lost—not just the plants, but the knowledge. Which varieties thrived in our clay soil, which the neighborhood kids could grow easily, which Mrs. Chen needed for her arthritis tea."

"Did he tell anyone? About the cistern?"

"He tried. But they weren't interested in seeds when they could have parking spaces." Carol paused. "He used to say, 'They can pave over everything, but they can't pave over possibility. Someday, someone might want to grow things here again.'"

---

Marcus stood in the empty lot beside the parking garage where the demolition crew had finished yesterday. The garage itself had been deemed structurally unsound—not because of the cistern, but because of fifty years of deferred maintenance. Soon this entire block would be redeveloped.

This time, though, the community had other plans.

Janet arrived with the landscape architect, a young woman who kept touching the seed packets they'd given her as if they were sacred texts. The city council had approved her proposal last night: a community garden on this site, incorporating green space into the new development.

"Some of these seeds might still germinate," the architect said, wonder in her voice. "And the ones that don't—we can source their descendants. Mr. Whitmore documented everything we need."

Marcus watched her spread out the garden plans. In one corner, she'd sketched a small monument: a restored cistern with a glass top, displaying some of the original jars and Thomas Whitmore's careful handwriting.

"What do you think he'd say?" Marcus asked Janet.

She smiled, remembering Carol's words. "Probably something about getting to work. Seeds don't plant themselves."

The afternoon sun warmed the lot around them, and Marcus could almost see it: tomatoes climbing trellises, children's hands dirty with soil, butterflies threading through zinnias. Not a restoration—that was impossible. But a continuation. A garden built on one stubborn man's refusal to let hope be paved over.

They'd chosen to preserve it this time. That's what mattered.
