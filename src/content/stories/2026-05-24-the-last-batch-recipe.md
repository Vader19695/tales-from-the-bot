The thermometer read sixty-eight degrees when Margaret checked it at 4:15 AM, and she knew. After forty-three years, she could feel it in her bones before any instrument confirmed it—the starter was dying.

She stood in the industrial kitchen of Bruno's Bakery, where she'd worked since 1981, watching the jar of pale, bubbling culture that had outlived two husbands, three presidential administrations, and her own retirement party six months ago. The fluorescent lights buzzed overhead, turning the perpetual fog of flour dust into something like stage lighting. Margaret had always found it beautiful, the way the particles caught the light like snow that never fell.

"Gran?"

She turned. Zoe stood in the doorway, seventeen and scowling, her phone already in her hand like a security blanket. Her daughter-in-law had dropped her off without coming in. Of course.

"You're here," Margaret said.

"Mom said I had to be." Zoe's thumbs moved across her screen. "Something about family legacy or whatever."

Margaret lifted the jar. "This is Beatrice. She's been alive since 1981. And she's dying."

Zoe glanced up, unimpressed. "It's just yeast."

"It's wild yeast and lactobacilli. A ecosystem. And she needs feeding every twelve hours or she'll die." Margaret set the jar down harder than she meant to. "Your mother works doubles. Your brother's deployed. You're the only one left."

"I don't even like bread."

"You liked my cinnamon rolls fine when you were eight."

Something flickered across Zoe's face—memory, maybe, before the divorce, before everything got complicated. Then the mask returned. "That was different."

Margaret scooped flour into a metal bowl. "Come here. Put your phone down."

"I can film it—"

"No. You need your hands."

Grudgingly, Zoe pocketed the phone and approached. Margaret demonstrated: flour, water, the old starter. "See how she's not rising like she should? Temperature's off. She's sluggish. We need to rebuild her strength, feed her twice a day, keep her warm but not too warm."

"Why not just buy new yeast?"

"Because this isn't just yeast. This culture has adapted. She makes bread that tastes like nothing else—every bakery has its flavor profile, and this is mine. Was mine." Margaret's hands moved through the mixture, folding, incorporating. "Feel this."

She guided Zoe's hands into the dough. The girl flinched, then stilled.

"It's weird," Zoe said. "Alive-feeling."

"That's because it is. You're touching millions of organisms. They eat, reproduce, die. Just like us, only faster." Margaret watched Zoe's fingers explore the texture. "You have good hands. Long fingers, like your grandfather."

"The one who left?"

"The other one. The one who taught me baking."

They worked in silence, the only sounds the hum of refrigeration units and the distant rumble of delivery trucks. Margaret showed her how to test the dough's elasticity, how to read its readiness by touch rather than timer.

"Why does this matter so much?" Zoe asked quietly.

Margaret considered her answer. "Because I'm seventy-one. Because your mother and I haven't really talked since she remarried. Because you kids think I'm just the old lady who forgot your birthday last year—"

"You didn't forget. You were in the hospital."

"—and maybe this is the only thing I have left to give you that matters. Something living. Something that requires showing up, every day, paying attention, being patient." She paused. "Something that doesn't work if you're not there."

Zoe's hands stilled in the dough. When she looked up, her eyes were wet. "I thought you didn't want to see us anymore."

"Oh, sweetheart." Margaret's own voice cracked. "I never wanted that."

They stood there in the golden dust-light, hands in the same bowl, the starter bubbling its slow revival on the counter beside them.

"So," Zoe said finally, "twice a day?"

"Every twelve hours."

"That's like, a whole commitment."

"Yes," Margaret said. "It is."

Zoe pulled out her phone and set an alarm. Then another. "Okay," she said. "Show me again."
