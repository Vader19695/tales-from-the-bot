The subway station at Fifth and Marlowe had been closed for thirty years, but its fluorescent lights still hummed the same industrial lullaby they always had. Down here, three stories below the city's frantic pulse, Elena sorted through other people's carelessness.

"Another umbrella," she announced to no one in particular, adding it to the forest of black nylon already crowding the corner. "That makes two hundred and forty-seven this year."

"Two hundred and forty-eight," Marcus corrected without looking up from his clipboard. He kept meticulous records of everything that passed through the depot—every forgotten scarf, every abandoned paperback, every pair of reading glasses that had slipped from someone's nose on the downtown express.

Yuki, the youngest of the three, was photographing items for the online database. She held up a child's drawing of what might have been a dragon or possibly a very angry cat. "This one's kind of sad. Someone's kid is missing their masterpiece."

"Bin Seventeen," Marcus said. "Children's artwork, miscellaneous."

They had bins for everything. Bins for jewelry that might be valuable and jewelry that definitely wasn't. Bins for phones with dead batteries that no one ever came back for. Bins for keys to apartments, cars, and lives that had moved on without them.

Elena had worked at the depot for eight years. She'd learned not to wonder too much about the stories behind each object. That way led to melancholy—like the time she'd found a love letter in a coat pocket, signed but never sent, the paper soft from being folded and unfolded countless times.

That letter had disappeared two months ago.

"Marcus," Elena had said then, "did anyone claim that letter from the blue peacoat?"

He'd checked his logs, his fingers tracing down the columns with practiced precision. "No one's claimed that coat at all. It's still here."

Except it wasn't. The coat remained, but the letter had vanished.

They'd started noticing other absences. Small things, items that should have been there but weren't. In their place: handwritten notes on cream-colored paper, tucked exactly where the missing object should be.

*For the person who wrote this, I hope you eventually found the courage to say it out loud.*

*This concert ticket from 1987—someone's perfect night. It deserves better than a box.*

*A watch that stopped at 3:47. Someone's last gift from their grandmother, I'd bet. I'll keep it safe.*

"She's here again," Yuki whispered one Wednesday afternoon.

The woman came every week, always at 2 PM when the depot was quiet. She was maybe sixty, with silver hair in a neat bun and a canvas bag slung over one shoulder. She'd wander the aisles they'd created between the bins, occasionally picking something up, studying it, setting it down with reverence.

"Should we say something?" Yuki asked.

Marcus shook his head. "She never takes anything when we're watching."

They'd debated reporting it. That was the logical thing to do—someone was stealing from the depot, even if they were stealing unclaimed items destined for donation or disposal anyway. But there was something about the notes, about the careful curation, that made them hesitate.

Elena made a decision. The next Wednesday, she approached the woman directly.

"The Brahms concert," Elena said. "1987. That was yours, wasn't it?"

The woman's hands stilled. She was holding an old compass, its brass surface tarnished but beautiful.

"First date with my husband," she said quietly. "I lost my ticket stub when we moved. Found it here last month in a wallet no one claimed. Felt like finding a piece of myself."

"So you started collecting other things," Elena said. "Other pieces of people."

"Only the ones that matter. The ones that mean something." The woman set down the compass. "I give them proper homes. Put them in albums, frames. Create the shrines they deserve. We lose so many important things just by living."

Elena thought of the bins, the endless accumulation of forgotten objects. The lost umbrella was just an umbrella. But the child's drawing, the unsent letter, the stopped watch—those were fragments of souls.

"Wednesdays at 2 PM," Elena said finally. "We take our break then. Fifteen minutes. The back section is unwatched."

The woman's eyes crinkled. "Thank you."

After she left, Marcus looked up from his clipboard. "We're not supposed to—"

"We're not supposed to care," Elena interrupted. "But down here, someone should."

She returned to sorting umbrellas, but her hands were gentler now, wondering which one might mean something to someone after all.
